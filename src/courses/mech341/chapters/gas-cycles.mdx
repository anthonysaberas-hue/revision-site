# Gas power cycles and jet engines

<div className="lede">

A gas turbine is three machines on one shaft: a **compressor** that raises the pressure of air, a **combustor** that adds heat at (nearly) constant pressure, and a **turbine** that expands the hot gas and gives back more work than the compressor took. What is left over is either **shaft power** (an industrial turbine, a helicopter, a ship) or **thrust** (a jet engine, where the leftover pressure is spent accelerating the exhaust). This chapter is the **Brayton cycle**: its ideal form, its real form with component efficiencies, the three modifications (regeneration, intercooling, reheat), stagnation properties (indispensable once the gas moves), and the turbojet and turbofan analysed on that basis. It is the heaviest chapter of the midterm and always on the exam.

</div>

<Machine name="the CFM56 turbofan and the GE 7FA industrial turbine">

Two machines built on the same cycle, tuned for opposite goals. Approximate values (≈, from manufacturers' public data; treat as "typical"):

- **CFM56-7B** (Boeing 737NG): bypass ratio **≈ 5.1**, overall pressure ratio **≈ 32** at take-off, take-off thrust **≈ 120 kN**, fan diameter ≈ 1.55 m. Most of the thrust comes from the cold bypass air, so the engine is quiet and has a low thrust-specific fuel consumption, **TSFC ≈ 17 mg/(N·s)** at cruise (≈ 0.6 lb/(lbf·h)).
- **GE 7FA** (utility gas turbine): about **160 MW** from one machine at about **36 % simple-cycle efficiency**, pressure ratio **≈ 15–16**, turbine inlet **≈ 1290 °C** (the course's problem 9-109 uses 159 MW, 35.9 %, $r_p=14.7$, 1288 °C). Its exhaust leaves at **≈ 600 °C**, which is why a combined cycle (chapter 10) can recover it to reach **≈ 60 %**.
- **Why the jet engine has a higher turbine temperature limit than the efficiency suggests**: the first-stage turbine blades see about **1600–1800 K** gas continuously; they survive because they are single-crystal alloys with internal cooling and a ceramic coating (the metal itself is kept below about 1300 K).
- Pressure ratios have climbed from 6 to 8 in early engines to **40+** in the latest, because $\eta=1-r_p^{-(k-1)/k}$ rewards pressure ratio, and materials now allow it.

</Machine>

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="What to know cold">

1. **Ideal Brayton cycle** (air standard, cold air): 1→2 isentropic compression, 2→3 constant-$P$ heat addition, 3→4 isentropic expansion, 4→1 constant-$P$ heat rejection. $\eta=1-\dfrac{1}{r_p^{(k-1)/k}}$ depends **only** on the pressure ratio.
2. **Net work** $w_{net}=c_p(T_3-T_4)-c_p(T_2-T_1)$ depends on $r_p$ **and** $T_3/T_1$ and has a **maximum** at $r_p=\left(\dfrac{T_3}{T_1}\right)^{k/(2(k-1))}$, where $T_2=T_4=\sqrt{T_1T_3}$. Maximum work is **not** at maximum efficiency.
3. **Real cycle**: $\eta_C=\dfrac{h_{2s}-h_1}{h_{2a}-h_1}$, $\eta_T=\dfrac{h_3-h_{4a}}{h_3-h_{4s}}$. Back-work ratio $r_{bw}=w_C/w_T$ (about 40–60 % for a gas turbine, versus 1–2 % for a steam plant). No $c_p$ or $k$ given: use the air table and $P_{r2}/P_{r1}=P_2/P_1$.
4. **Modifications**: regeneration raises $\eta$ with no change in work; intercooling and reheat raise work and lower $\eta$ unless combined with regeneration.
5. **Stagnation**: $T_0=T+\dfrac{V^2}{2c_p}$ stays constant in adiabatic flow without work; $P_0/P=(T_0/T)^{k/(k-1)}$ drops with friction.
6. **Turbojet**: $w_T=w_C$ fixes $T_{05}$; the nozzle takes the rest; thrust $F=\dot m\,(V_6-V_1)+A_6(P_6-P_a)$.

</Idea>

## The ideal Brayton cycle

The combustion process of a real engine is replaced by constant-pressure heat addition from an external source and the exhaust by constant-pressure heat rejection to the atmosphere, so the **open** engine becomes a **closed** air-standard cycle. Assumptions: air-standard analysis (fixed mass of air, the fuel's mass is neglected), constant specific heats ($c_p=1.005$, $k=1.4$) unless told otherwise, and kinetic and potential energy neglected.

<BraytonTsFigure />

Because 2→3 and 4→1 are isobars and 1→2 and 3→4 are isentropes, the temperature ratios are tied together:

$$\frac{T_2}{T_1}=\frac{T_3}{T_4}=r_p^{(k-1)/k}\equiv c,\qquad r_p=\frac{P_2}{P_1},\qquad t=\frac{T_3}{T_1}.$$

### Efficiency

$$q_{in}=c_p(T_3-T_2),\quad q_{out}=c_p(T_4-T_1),\quad \eta=1-\frac{T_4-T_1}{T_3-T_2}=1-\frac{T_1(T_4/T_1-1)}{T_2(T_3/T_2-1)}.$$

Since $T_4/T_1=T_3/T_2$ the brackets cancel and

$$\boxed{\,\eta=1-\frac{T_1}{T_2}=1-\frac{1}{r_p^{(k-1)/k}}\,}$$

**Why**: the average temperature of heat addition rises with $r_p$ while heat is still rejected at $T_1$ (Carnot reasoning). The efficiency is independent of $T_3$.

### Net work and the pressure ratio for maximum work

$$\frac{w_{net}}{c_pT_1}=t\left(1-\frac1c\right)-(c-1).$$

For fixed $T_1$ and $T_3$ this is zero at $c=1$ (no compression) and again at $c=t$ ($T_2=T_3$: no heat to add), so it has a maximum in between. Setting $dw/dT_2=0$ with $T_4=T_1T_3/T_2$:

$$\frac{d}{dT_2}\Big[c_p(T_3-T_4-T_2+T_1)\Big]=c_p\left[\frac{T_1T_3}{T_2^2}-1\right]=0\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{T_2=T_4=\sqrt{T_1T_3}},\qquad r_p=\left(\frac{T_3}{T_1}\right)^{k/(2(k-1))}.$$

"For maximum work the temperature of the air at the compressor exit must equal the temperature of the gas at the turbine exit." For Ex. 9-5 ($T_1=300$ K, $T_3=1300$ K): $r_p=(4.333)^{1.75}=13.0$, $T_2=T_4=624.5$ K.

<Remember title="Maximum work is NOT at maximum efficiency">

Efficiency keeps rising with $r_p$ (it only depends on $r_p$). Work peaks at $r_p\approx13$ here and then falls because the compressor consumes more and more of the turbine's work. A **jet engine** is designed near the work/thrust optimum (small, light engine for a given power); a **power plant** goes for efficiency (higher $r_p$) because fuel cost dominates. In both cases, real losses push the work peak to a lower $r_p$ and flatten the efficiency curve.

</Remember>

## Actual cycles: component efficiencies and the air-table method

Real compressors and turbines are not isentropic; the T–s diagram's end points move to the right (see the dashed cycle above). With the isentropic efficiencies:

$$T_{2a}=T_1+\frac{T_{2s}-T_1}{\eta_C},\qquad T_{4a}=T_3-\eta_T\,(T_3-T_{4s}),\qquad \eta_{th}=\frac{w_T-w_C}{q_{in}}=\frac{(h_3-h_{4a})-(h_{2a}-h_1)}{h_3-h_{2a}}.$$

**Back-work ratio** $r_{bw}=w_C/w_T$. A steam plant spends about 1–2 % of its turbine work on the pump (pumping a liquid is cheap); a gas turbine spends **40–60 %** on the compressor (compressing a gas is expensive). That is why a few percentage points of component efficiency swing a gas turbine's output so much: losses subtract from a small difference of two large numbers.

**Which method?** Habashi's rule: *if they don't tell you $c_p$ and $k$, use the air table*. Take $T_1\to P_{r1}$, multiply by the pressure ratio to get $P_{r2}=P_{r1}(P_2/P_1)$, read $h_{2s}$; for the turbine take $T_3\to P_{r3}\to P_{r4}=P_{r3}/r_p\to h_{4s}$. You **cannot** use $T_2/T_1=(P_2/P_1)^{(k-1)/k}$ and $h=c_pT$ with variable specific heats; you can use constant-$c_p$ relations only if told to.

## Stagnation properties

Once the gas moves fast (jet engines, ram effect, nozzles) the enthalpy that matters is the **stagnation enthalpy**: what the gas would have if it were brought to rest adiabatically with no work,

$$h_0=h+\frac{V^2}{2},\qquad T_0=T+\frac{V^2}{2c_p}\ \ (V\text{ in m/s},\ c_p\text{ in kJ/kg·K: }T_0=T+\tfrac{V^2}{2000\,c_p}).$$

From the steady-flow equation, if there is **no heat and no shaft work**, $T_0$ is constant along the flow, *even with friction and area changes*. The **stagnation pressure** is defined by bringing the gas to rest **isentropically**:

$$\frac{P_0}{P}=\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{k/(k-1)}.$$

$P_0$ stays constant only if the flow is also frictionless. With friction, $T_0$ is unchanged but $P_0$ **drops** (entropy increases, so the same $T_0$ corresponds to a lower $P_0$). For incompressible flow $P_0=P+\tfrac12\rho V^2$ (the "dynamic pressure" term); for compressible flow $P_0=P\left(1+\tfrac{k-1}{2}Ma^2\right)^{k/(k-1)}$, and the incompressible formula is only the low-Mach limit.

**Consequence for engines** (the slide that many students never absorb): in a real compressor, friction means that $T_0$ rises more than the isentropic value, but the **stagnation pressure** reached is lower. If the engine must reach a given $P_{03}$, it must do **extra work** compared with the ideal cycle. Equivalently, for the same work you reach a lower pressure than the ideal compressor.

From here on, jet-engine calculations are done with **stagnation** $T_0$ and $P_0$ at every station.

## Brayton modifications: regeneration, intercooling and reheat

### Regeneration

The turbine exhaust (≈ 650 °C for a 7FA) is hotter than the air leaving the compressor (≈ 380 °C). A counterflow heat exchanger transfers that heat to the compressed air before the combustor, so less fuel is needed for the same work.

$$\eta_{regen}=1-\frac{T_1}{T_3}\,r_p^{(k-1)/k}=1-\frac{c}{t},\qquad w_{net}\ \text{unchanged}.$$

It is derived from $h_5=h_4$ (ideal regenerator, $T_5=T_4$): $q_{in}=c_p(T_3-T_4)$ instead of $c_p(T_3-T_2)$. **When does regeneration stop being useful?** When $t=c$, i.e. $T_4=T_2$, which is exactly the pressure ratio for maximum work. Above that, the exhaust is colder than the compressed air and the regenerator would cool it. Practical consequence: regeneration pays off at **low** pressure ratios and **high** $T_3/T_1$ (microturbines, tanks), while high-$r_p$ engines (jets) do not use it.

**Actual regenerator**: it has an **effectiveness** $\varepsilon=\dfrac{T_{05}-T_{02}}{T_{04}-T_{02}}$ (not an efficiency: the streams meet too briefly to equalize temperatures). With the energy balance $c_{p,g}(T_{04}-T_{06})=c_{p,a}(T_{05}-T_{02})$ (hot side loses what the cold side gains) you solve for the two unknown temperatures.

### Intercooling and reheat

**Intercooling**: compress in two stages with cooling between, ideally with equal pressure ratios, $P_{2}=\sqrt{P_1P_4}$. The ideal compression is isothermal (smallest work for a given pressure ratio, since $v$ stays small); intercooling approximates it. With $c=r_p^{(k-1)/k}$ and $t=T_3/T_1$:

$$\frac{w_{net}}{c_pT_1}=2-2\sqrt c-\frac tc+t,\qquad \frac{q_{in}}{c_pT_1}=t-\sqrt c\ \Rightarrow\ \eta=\frac{2-2\sqrt c-t/c+t}{t-\sqrt c}.$$

**Reheat**: expand in two stages, reheat in between (ideal: equal pressure ratio each, $P_4=\sqrt{P_3P_6}$). The first turbine runs the compressor, the second delivers net power:

$$\frac{w_{net}}{c_pT_1}=2t-\frac{2t}{\sqrt c}-c+1,\qquad \frac{q_{in}}{c_pT_1}=2t-c-\frac{t}{\sqrt c}\ \Rightarrow\ \eta=\frac{2t-2t/\sqrt c-c+1}{2t-c-t/\sqrt c}.$$

Both raise the **work** and **lower** the efficiency: the extra part of the cycle has a lower average $T_H$ (a Carnot argument). The remedy is to add regeneration, which recovers the efficiency and lets the exhaust heat pay for the extra heat.

## Jet engines

<TurbojetStationsFigure />

A **turbojet** is a gas turbine whose turbine takes *only as much as the compressor needs*, $w_T=w_C$. The leftover high-pressure gas (at $P_{05}$ above ambient) expands in a **nozzle** and leaves at high speed; the thrust is the momentum change of the stream. A **turbofan** adds a ducted fan on the same shaft: a fraction of the air bypasses the core and is accelerated less, so the same fuel pushes a larger air mass more slowly, which is far more efficient at subsonic speeds (the **bypass ratio** is the ratio of the two flows). A **turboprop** and a **turboshaft** extend the idea further: a second turbine takes nearly all the leftover energy to turn a propeller or rotor.

### Station-by-station analysis (his labels: 1 free stream, 2 after the intake, 3 compressor exit, 4 turbine inlet, 5 turbine exit, 6 nozzle exit)

1. **Intake (1→2).** Adiabatic, no work: $T_{02}=T_{01}=T_1+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2000\,c_p}$. A perfect diffuser gives $P_{02}=P_{01}=P_1\left(\dfrac{T_{01}}{T_1}\right)^{k/(k-1)}$, the **ram** pressure ratio (1.69 at Ma 0.9, 11 km). If a diffuser efficiency $\eta_D$ is given: $\eta_D=\dfrac{T_{02s}-T_1}{T_{01}-T_1}$ and $P_{02}=P_1\left(\dfrac{T_{02s}}{T_1}\right)^{k/(k-1)}$.
2. **Compressor (2→3).** $T_{03s}=T_{02}\,r_c^{(k-1)/k}$ and $T_{03}=T_{02}+\dfrac{T_{03s}-T_{02}}{\eta_C}$; $P_{03}=r_cP_{02}$; $w_C=c_{p,a}(T_{03}-T_{02})$.
3. **Combustor (3→4).** $P_{04}=P_{03}$ (no pressure loss unless told). Heat per kg of air $q_A=c_{p,g}(T_{04}-T_{03})$ and fuel flow $\dot m_f=\dfrac{\dot m_a\,q_A}{HV}$ (fuel mass neglected in the flow, as on the exams).
4. **Turbine (4→5).** $w_T=w_C$: $c_{p,g}(T_{04}-T_{05})=c_{p,a}(T_{03}-T_{02})$ gives $T_{05}$. If $\eta_T$ is given, $T_{05s}=T_{04}-\dfrac{T_{04}-T_{05}}{\eta_T}$ and $\dfrac{P_{05}}{P_{04}}=\left(\dfrac{T_{05s}}{T_{04}}\right)^{k_g/(k_g-1)}$. The gas has $c_{p,g}\approx1.147$, $k_g\approx1.333$ (hot combustion products); use them if given.
5. **Nozzle (5→6).** *Converging–diverging, expanding to ambient:* $T_{6}=T_{05}\left(\dfrac{P_a}{P_{05}}\right)^{(k_g-1)/k_g}$ (for an ideal nozzle), $V_6=\sqrt{2000\,c_{p,g}(T_{05}-T_6)}$. *Converging only:* check for choking: it chokes if $\dfrac{P_{05}}{P_a}>\left(\dfrac{k_g+1}{2}\right)^{k_g/(k_g-1)}\approx1.85$ ($k_g=1.333$). If choked, $M_6=1$, $T_6=\dfrac{2T_{05}}{k_g+1}$, $V_6=\sqrt{k_gRT_6}$ and the exit pressure $P_6=P_{05}\left(\dfrac2{k_g+1}\right)^{k_g/(k_g-1)}>P_a$, which adds a **pressure thrust** $A_6(P_6-P_a)$.
6. **Thrust and efficiency.**

$$F_{net}=\dot m\,(V_6-V_1)+A_6\,(P_6-P_a),\qquad \dot W_{prop}=F_{net}\,V_1,\qquad TSFC=\frac{\dot m_f}{F_{net}},\qquad \eta_{o}=\frac{F_{net}V_1}{\dot m_f\,HV}.$$

**Gross thrust** is the jet's momentum plus pressure term, $\dot m\,V_6+A_6(P_6-P_a)$; **net thrust** subtracts the momentum of the incoming air ($\dot m\,V_1$, "ram drag"). On a test stand ($V_1=0$) they coincide. For a turbofan, sum the contributions of the core and bypass streams: $F=\dot m_c(V_{j1}-V_a)+\dot m_b(V_{j2}-V_a)$.

<Remember title="Propulsive power, ESHP and ESFC (comparing engine types)">

Jet thrust and propeller shaft power are compared through the **equivalent shaft horsepower**, ESHP: for a jet, the thrust divided by a static conversion factor (his slide divides by 8.5), for a turboprop the shaft power plus the residual jet. The fuel use is then the **equivalent specific fuel consumption** $ESFC=\dfrac{3600\,\dot m_f}{ESHP}$. On the marathon slide, with the same fuel flow of 0.022 kg/s: turboprop ESHP 273.7 and ESFC ≈ 0.29, turbofan 145.1 and ≈ 0.55, turbojet 99.1 and ≈ 0.80. Same fuel, more power, lower consumption as you go from jet to fan to prop (at the speeds where props work).

</Remember>

## Industrial turbines: expanding to rest with an exhaust diffuser

In a land-based turbine the exhaust gas leaves the last turbine stage with considerable kinetic energy that is simply lost to the atmosphere. An **exhaust diffuser** slows the gas to rest, so that the *static* pressure at the turbine exit can be **below atmospheric** (the diffuser recovers the pressure rise up to ambient). The turbine then expands over a larger ratio $P_{03}/P_4$ and delivers more work. Habashi marks this as the one concept "many students, to the end of the semester, have trouble understanding": the turbine efficiency is defined with the isentropic temperature drop $T_{03}-T_{04s}=T_{03}\left[1-\left(\dfrac{P_4}{P_{03}}\right)^{(k-1)/k}\right]$, where $P_4$ is the (lower) static exit pressure, not ambient.

## Habashi's traps

<Trap title="Concepts the instructor says students still get wrong in chapter 9">

1. **Stagnation versus static**: after the intake, every station is a stagnation value; do not mix a static $T_1$ with stagnation $T_{03}$.
2. **Friction keeps $T_0$ but lowers $P_0$**: the same $T_0$ at a lower $P_0$ means higher entropy; a real compressor needs extra work to reach a target $P_{03}$.
3. **Maximum work is not maximum efficiency** (see the Brayton sim).
4. **Regeneration changes efficiency, not work; intercooling and reheat change work, and (without regeneration) lower efficiency.**
5. **Gross versus net thrust**: net thrust subtracts the ram drag $\dot mV_1$; a choked converging nozzle adds a pressure thrust.
6. **Jet engine 2000 K versus piston engine 3000 K** (his slide): the piston engine's peak is brief and the walls are cooled; the turbine sees the gas all the time.

</Trap>

## Check yourself

1. For $T_1=300$ K and $T_3=1300$ K, at what pressure ratio is the ideal net work maximum and what are $T_2$ and $T_4$ there?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$r_p=(1300/300)^{1.75}=13.0$, $T_2=T_4=\sqrt{300\cdot1300}=624.5$ K. The efficiency there is $1-13^{-0.2857}=0.52$, not the maximum possible (which keeps rising with $r_p$).

</details>

2. At the pressure ratio of maximum work, would a regenerator help? Why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No: there $T_4=T_2$, so the exhaust is no hotter than the compressed air; $\eta_{regen}=\eta_{simple}$. Regeneration needs $T_4>T_2$, i.e. a pressure ratio below the work optimum.

</details>

3. A real compressor ($\eta_C=0.85$) raises air from 288 K by a pressure ratio of 10. What is the exit temperature and why is it higher than 556 K?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$T_{2s}=288(10)^{0.2857}=556.0$ K; $T_{2a}=288+\dfrac{556.0-288}{0.85}=603$ K. Friction turns extra work into heat in the gas; the pressure ratio is the same, the entropy is higher.

</details>

4. When does a converging nozzle on a turbojet choke? What happens to the thrust formula?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

When $P_{05}/P_a>\left(\dfrac{k_g+1}{2}\right)^{k_g/(k_g-1)}\approx1.85$. Then $M_6=1$, $P_6>P_a$, and the thrust has an extra pressure term $A_6(P_6-P_a)$.

</details>

5. Why does net thrust fall with flight speed at fixed engine operation even though the exit velocity barely changes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$F=\dot m(V_6-V_1)$: the captured momentum $\dot mV_1$ grows with speed (ram drag). The ram pressure rise helps the cycle, but it never outweighs that term at constant fuel flow, which is why a turbojet's thrust decreases with $V_1$ at low Mach number.

</details>
