# Vapor power cycles

<div className="lede">

Most of the world's electricity (coal, gas, nuclear, solar-thermal, biomass) is made by boiling water, expanding the steam in a turbine, condensing it and pumping it back: the **Rankine cycle**. The cycle is simple; the exam is long because every modification (superheat, reheat, open and closed **feedwater heaters**, cogeneration) adds states and an energy balance. The discipline is always the same: **draw the T–s diagram, number the states, and write one energy balance per component**. The efficiency questions are answered by one idea, Carnot: *raise the average temperature of heat addition or lower the average temperature of heat rejection*. Habashi says that, to the end of the semester, students still miss two things: *the area under the curve is the heat*, and *using the Carnot principle to judge a modification*. Both are below.

</div>

<Machine name="a combined-cycle plant and a pressurized-water reactor">

- **Combined-cycle gas-and-steam plant**: **≈ 60–64 %** net efficiency, the highest of any thermal power plant. A gas turbine (≈ 40 % alone) exhausts at ≈ 600 °C into a **heat-recovery steam generator**, which raises steam for a bottoming Rankine cycle (typically 3 pressure levels with reheat, ≈ 125–170 bar, ≈ 565 °C).
- **Pressurized-water reactor (PWR)**: **≈ 33 %**. The reactor coolant (≈ 155 bar, ≈ 300–325 °C) never boils; it makes **saturated steam at ≈ 7 MPa** (≈ 286 °C) in the steam generators. Course check on that steam: an *ideal* Rankine cycle on 7 MPa saturated steam with a 7 kPa condenser has $\eta=37$ % but leaves the turbine at **quality 0.68** (32 % liquid droplets, which erode blades), so real plants use **moisture separators and reheaters** and feedwater heating, finishing at ≈ 33 %.
- **Ultra-supercritical coal plant**: ≈ 25–30 MPa, ≈ 600 °C, reheat: **≈ 42–47 %**. Same machine with a higher average $T_H$. (Course check: the ideal cycle at 25 MPa, 600 °C with reheat to 600 °C at 4 MPa gives $\eta=46$ %; with $\eta_T=0.9$, $\eta_P=0.85$ it is 42 %.)
- **Condenser**: ≈ 5–10 kPa, i.e. 33–46 °C, cooled by river, sea or cooling-tower water. Its low pressure (a partial vacuum) is what makes the cycle work; the vacuum is also why a plant is physically large.

</Machine>

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="The Rankine cycle in one box">

1. **Ideal Rankine** (states 1 to 4): 1→2 pump (isentropic, **liquid**), 2→3 boiler (constant $P$), 3→4 turbine (isentropic), 4→1 condenser (constant $P$, **saturated liquid at 1**).
2. $w_P=v_1(P_2-P_1)$, $q_{in}=h_3-h_2$, $w_T=h_3-h_4$, $q_{out}=h_4-h_1$; $\eta=\dfrac{w_T-w_P}{q_{in}}=1-\dfrac{q_{out}}{q_{in}}$.
3. **Real**: $\eta_T=\dfrac{h_3-h_{4a}}{h_3-h_{4s}}$, $\eta_P=\dfrac{h_{2s}-h_1}{h_{2a}-h_1}=\dfrac{v_1\Delta P}{h_{2a}-h_1}$.
4. **To increase $\eta$**: lower $P_{cond}$, superheat to higher $T$, raise $P_{boiler}$, add reheat, add feedwater heating. Each raises $T_{H,avg}$ or lowers $T_{L,avg}$.
5. **Open feedwater heater** (a mixing chamber): $y=\dfrac{h_3-h_2}{h_6-h_2}$; needs a pump after it; heater pressure = bleed pressure.
6. **Closed feedwater heater** (a heat exchanger): feedwater leaves at the bled steam's saturation temperature; the drain is throttled back (or pumped forward).
7. **Cogeneration**: $\varepsilon_u=\dfrac{\dot W_{net}+\dot Q_p}{\dot Q_{in}}$.

</Idea>

## The ideal Rankine cycle

Why not a Carnot cycle with steam? Compressing a *wet* mixture in a pump is impractical (two-phase compression, cavitation) and a turbine fed with a wet mixture is eroded. The Rankine cycle therefore (1) **condenses completely** so that the pump handles a liquid (small work), and (2) **superheats** before the turbine so the exhaust is dry enough. The pump compresses a liquid isentropically; the **specific volume is essentially constant**, so

$$w_{P}=\int v\,dP\approx v_1(P_2-P_1)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad h_2=h_1+w_P.$$

State by state (his labels): point 1 is **saturated liquid** on the left of the dome (found with $P_1$, $x=0$); point 2 is compressed liquid ($h_2=h_1+w_P$, table A-7 or the approximation $h=h_f+v\,\Delta P$); point 3 is superheated vapour ($P_3$, $T_3$ from A-6); point 4 is in the dome (or just outside): $P_4=P_1$ and $s_4=s_3$; if $s_4<s_g$ you get a quality $x_4=\dfrac{s_4-s_f}{s_{fg}}$ and $h_4=h_f+x_4h_{fg}$.

"Area under the curve" on the T–s diagram: the area under 2→3 is $q_{in}$, under 4→1 is $q_{out}$, the enclosed area is $w_{net}$, and the efficiency is the ratio of areas.

## Raising the efficiency (Carnot reasoning)

For an internally reversible cycle the efficiency equals that of a Carnot cycle running between the **average temperature of heat addition** and the **average temperature of heat rejection**:

$$\eta=1-\frac{T_{L,avg}}{T_{H,avg}},\qquad T_{H,avg}=\frac{q_{in}}{\Delta s_{in}}.$$

So any change that raises $T_{H,avg}$ or lowers $T_{L,avg}$ *must* raise efficiency; any change that lowers $T_{H,avg}$ (for instance adding a low-temperature heat addition) lowers it. This is the "Carnot principle for modifications" that Habashi tests. For the class cycle, the numbers from the lib:

| Change | $\eta$ | $T_{H,avg}$ (K) | $T_{L}$ (K) | Exit quality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base: 4 MPa, 400 °C, 10 kPa | 35.3 % | 493 | 319.0 | 0.816 |
| Lower condenser to 5 kPa | 37.3 % | 488 | 306.0 | 0.795 |
| Higher boiler pressure, 8 MPa | 38.0 % | 514 | 319.0 | 0.762 |
| Superheat to 600 °C | 38.4 % | 518 | 319.0 | 0.896 |
| Reheat at 400 kPa to 400 °C | 35.9 % | 497 | 319.0 | 0.967 |

The identity $\eta=1-T_L/T_{H,avg}$ holds exactly in each row (try it: $1-319.0/493=0.353$). Three lessons:

- **Lower condenser pressure** lowers $T_L$ (the saturation temperature at the condenser), but not below the cooling water: a practical limit of about 5 kPa, and it makes the exit **wetter** (0.795).
- **Superheating** raises $T_{H,avg}$ **and** dries the exhaust (0.816 to 0.896): double benefit, limited only by metallurgy (about 600 °C).
- **Higher boiler pressure** raises $T_{H,avg}$ but moves the expansion left on the T–s diagram into the wetter region (x falls from 0.816 to 0.762). That is the reason for **reheat**: expand part-way, take the steam back to the boiler, heat it to the original temperature, expand again.

## The ideal reheat Rankine cycle

Expand in a high-pressure turbine to an intermediate pressure $P_{rh}$, return **all** the steam to the boiler's reheater, heat it at constant pressure (usually back to the original temperature), and expand in the low-pressure turbine to the condenser. With states 1 (cond exit), 2 (pump exit), 3 (HP turbine inlet), 4 (HP exit at $P_{rh}$), 5 (reheater exit), 6 (LP exit):

$$w_T=(h_3-h_4)+(h_5-h_6),\qquad q_{in}=(h_3-h_2)+(h_5-h_4),\qquad w_P=v_1(P_2-P_1).$$

**Why it works**: reheat adds heat at a *high average temperature* (the heat at 400 kPa and above 143.6 °C), so $T_{H,avg}$ rises a little (493 to 497 K, i.e. $\eta$ 35.3 to 35.9 %) and, more importantly, the exhaust is **much drier**. The **work rises** a lot (1066 to 1293 kJ/kg, +21 %). The best intermediate pressure is typically about **one fifth to one quarter of the boiler pressure** (see 10-61 and the examples).

## The ideal regenerative Rankine cycle

In the plain cycle the water leaves the pump cold (≈ 46 °C) and enters the boiler, where the first part of the heat addition happens at a *low* temperature (the sloping part 2→2′ of the isobar), dragging $T_{H,avg}$ down. The fix: **take some steam out of the turbine** part-way (it has done some work already but still has plenty of energy) and use it to **preheat the feedwater**. That steam could have produced more work if it had expanded further, but the heat it saves in the boiler is worth more: the boiler no longer has to supply the coolest part of the heat addition, which is the part with the lowest temperature, so $T_{H,avg}$ rises.

<FeedwaterHeatersFigure />

There are two kinds of feedwater heater (FWH): **open** (the steam and the feedwater mix) and **closed** (a heat exchanger).

## Open feedwater heaters

An open (direct-contact) feedwater heater is a **mixing chamber**. Steam bled from the turbine at pressure $P_6$ mixes with the feedwater coming from the first pump. Ideally the mixture leaves as **saturated liquid** at the heater pressure ($x=0$ at state 3). Because streams mix, they must be at the **same pressure**: $P_2=P_3=P_6$.

**Notation for one OFWH** (his): 1 condenser exit; 2 pump I exit at $P_6$; 3 OFWH exit (saturated liquid at $P_6$); 4 pump II exit at boiler pressure; 5 turbine inlet; 6 bleed (fraction $y$ of the boiler flow); 7 turbine exit. Take the heater as a control volume: no heat, no work, so $\sum\dot m_{in}h=\sum\dot m_{out}h$, per kg of boiler flow

$$y\,h_6+(1-y)\,h_2=1\cdot h_3\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boxed{\,y=\frac{h_3-h_2}{h_6-h_2}\,}$$

and then, per kg of **boiler** flow:

$$w_T=(h_5-h_6)+(1-y)(h_6-h_7),\qquad w_P=(1-y)w_{PI}+w_{PII},\qquad q_{in}=h_5-h_4,\qquad q_{out}=(1-y)(h_7-h_1),$$

with $w_{PI}=v_1(P_6-P_1)$ and $w_{PII}=v_3(P_5-P_3)$ (state 3 is saturated liquid at the heater pressure, so a second pump is needed: with each open heater you add a pump). The turbine work *after* the bleed is multiplied by $(1-y)$: **fraction accounting is the whole difficulty**. The efficiency can also be written $\eta=1-\dfrac{q_{out}}{q_{in}}=1-\dfrac{(1-y)(h_7-h_1)}{h_5-h_4}$.

## Closed feedwater heaters

A closed feedwater heater is a **shell-and-tube heat exchanger**: the bled steam condenses on the outside of the tubes, the feedwater flows inside, and the two streams **do not mix**, so they can be at **different pressures** and no second pump is needed for the bled stream. Two idealizations always apply (and Habashi marks them with a "!"):

1. The feedwater leaves at the **saturation temperature of the bled steam**: $T_{fw,out}=T_{sat}(P_{bleed})$ (an ideal heat exchanger with zero temperature difference at the hot end). At the boiler pressure that water is a compressed liquid, $h_{fw}\approx h_f(T)+v_f\,(P_{boiler}-P_{sat})$.
2. The drain leaves as **saturated liquid at the bleed pressure** (the steam has fully condensed).

What happens to the drain gives two arrangements:

- **Drain throttled back** (to a lower-pressure heater or to the condenser): a valve, $h=$ const. The whole boiler flow passes through the tubes (from the pump exit, state 2, to the feedwater leaving the heater) and the bled fraction $y$ condenses on the shell side. The heater's energy balance (heat lost by the bleed steam equals heat gained by the feedwater) is $y\,(h_{bleed}-h_{drain})=1\cdot(h_{fw,out}-h_{2})$, so

$$y=\frac{h_{fw,out}-h_{2}}{h_{bleed}-h_{drain}}.$$

- **Drain pumped forward** into the feedwater line (the 10-61 case, below): a pump raises the drain to boiler pressure and it mixes with the main feedwater.

**The instructor's clarification of problem 10-61** (a one-page note from the course, Q10-65): the textbook's T–s figure for 10-61 is *wrong*, and the correct picture is: boiler 10 MPa (state 5), bleed at 0.8 MPa (state 6) to the closed heater, reheat at state 7, condenser 10 kPa. The drain (state 3, saturated liquid at 0.8 MPa) is **pumped forward** (3 → 10) to 10 MPa and mixes with the main feedwater (state 9) in a mixing chamber, giving state 4. Key relations: $T_9=T_3$ (feedwater leaves the closed heater at the drain's saturation temperature); $h_9\approx h_3+v_3(P_{10}-P_3)$; the drain pump gives the same $h_{10}=h_3+v_3(P_{10}-P_3)$; so $h_9=h_{10}$ and the **mixing-chamber exit satisfies $h_4=h_9=h_{10}$**. Do not rely on the textbook's state numbering for this problem.

## Cogeneration

A power plant rejects about two-thirds of its fuel energy in the condenser, at a temperature (≈ 40 °C) too low to be useful. A **cogeneration** plant turns the low-grade heat into a product: it takes some steam out at a **higher pressure** (process steam for a factory, district heating, a paper mill), so the "waste" heat is sold. The measure of performance is the **utilization factor**

$$\varepsilon_u=\frac{\dot W_{net}+\dot Q_{process}}{\dot Q_{in}}=1-\frac{\dot Q_{out}}{\dot Q_{in}},$$

which is *not* the thermal efficiency: counting a kilojoule of low-grade process heat as equal to one of electricity (it is not; see the exergy of heat in chapter 8) can make $\varepsilon_u$ look like 90 %. Look at how much *work* each plant produces too.

<Problem id="Cogeneration" title="Boiler 7 MPa / 500 °C, 15 kg/s, process heater at 500 kPa: three operating modes" tags="cogeneration, utilization factor, process heater">

## Combined gas–vapor cycles

The gas turbine's exhaust (≈ 600 °C) is a ready-made heat source for a steam cycle. In a **combined cycle** the exhaust passes through a **heat-recovery steam generator (HRSG)**, an energy balance between exhaust and water: $\dot m_g\,c_{p,g}(T_{exh,in}-T_{exh,out})=\dot m_s\,(h_{steam}-h_{feed})$ (with a pinch-point limit on the approach). The steam then runs through a Rankine cycle. If the gas turbine has efficiency $\eta_g$, the steam cycle $\eta_s$, and the HRSG captures a fraction $\varepsilon_R$ of the leftover heat,

$$\eta_{cc}=\eta_g+(1-\eta_g)\,\varepsilon_R\,\eta_s.$$

Illustration (typical, not from the notes): $\eta_g=0.41$, $\varepsilon_R=0.85$, $\eta_s=0.40$ gives $\eta_{cc}=0.41+0.59(0.85)(0.40)=0.61$, in the 60–64 % range of the Machine box. Every part of the plant is a Brayton cycle on top and a Rankine on the bottom, rejecting heat at 40 °C instead of 600 °C: Carnot reasoning again.

## Habashi's traps

<Trap title="The two concepts the instructor says students still miss in chapter 10">

1. **Area under the curve is the heat (or work in the enclosed area), not a property.** The area under 2→3 on the T–s diagram is $q_{in}$; the area enclosed is the net work. Compare modifications by *looking at what area is added or removed*.
2. **Use the Carnot principle to judge a modification.** Ask what it does to $T_{H,avg}$ and $T_{L,avg}$: lowering the condenser pressure lowers $T_L$ (better); superheating and a higher boiler pressure raise $T_{H,avg}$ (better); adding a low-temperature heat addition lowers it (worse); feedwater heating *removes* the coldest heat addition (better), reheat adds a *hot* one (slightly better, and drier).
3. **Check the quality.** A higher boiler pressure without reheat leaves the turbine with $x<0.8$: unacceptable.
4. **Compressed liquid**: $h\approx h_f(T)+v_f\,(P-P_{sat})$ (his note: "$\Delta h=v\,\Delta P$").
5. **A closed heater's feedwater leaves at the *bled steam's* saturation temperature**, and the **drain is saturated liquid at the bleed pressure**. An open heater's outflow is saturated liquid at the heater pressure.
6. **Do not trust the textbook's figure for 10-61**; use the instructor's redrawn version (drain pumped forward, $h_4=h_9=h_{10}$).

</Trap>
