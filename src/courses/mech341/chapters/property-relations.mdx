# Thermodynamic property relations

<div className="lede">

You can **measure** pressure, specific volume, temperature and $c_p$. You cannot measure $u$, $h$ or $s$. Every steam table and every "ideal-gas" formula is therefore built from measured quantities and **partial-derivative relations** that tie the unmeasurable properties to the measurable ones. This chapter derives those relations (the Gibbs equations, the Maxwell relations, the general $du$, $dh$, $ds$ expressions), uses them in two famous applications (the **Clapeyron equation** for the enthalpy of vaporization, and the **Joule–Thomson coefficient** for throttling), and then corrects the ideal-gas equation for real gases with the **compressibility factor** and the **departure charts**. It is the first chapter of the final: more derivation, fewer cycles, and every problem is one formula applied carefully.

</div>

<Machine name="liquefying natural gas by Joule–Thomson expansion, and pumping CO₂ underground">

- **LNG**: methane boils at **−162 °C (111 K)** at 1 atm; liquefied natural gas takes **≈ 1/600 of the gas volume**, which is what makes sea transport economical. Liquefying costs roughly **0.3–0.5 kWh per kg** (≈ 10 % of the gas's own energy). The classical liquefier (Linde cycle) compresses the gas, cools it with the returning cold stream in a heat exchanger and **throttles it through a valve**: for a real gas, $\mu_{JT}=(\partial T/\partial P)_h>0$ below the inversion temperature, so the expansion cools it a little each pass (typically **0.2–0.5 K per bar** for N₂ and CH₄ near room temperature, about 1 K/bar for CO₂); regeneration accumulates the cooling until the gas liquefies. ## The 30-second version

<Idea title="What to know cold">

1. **Gibbs equations**: $du=T\,ds-P\,dv$, $dh=T\,ds+v\,dP$, $da=-s\,dT-P\,dv$, $dg=-s\,dT+v\,dP$.
2. **Maxwell relations** (from the exactness of those four): $\left(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial v}\right)_s=-\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial s}\right)_v$, $\left(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_s=\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial s}\right)_P$, $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial v}\right)_T=\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v$, $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial P}\right)_T=-\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P$.
3. **General changes**: $du=c_v\,dT+\left[T\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v-P\right]dv$, $\ dh=c_p\,dT+\left[v-T\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]dP$, $\ ds=\dfrac{c_p}{T}dT-\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P dP$. For an ideal gas these collapse to $du=c_vdT$, $dh=c_pdT$.
4. **Clapeyron**: $\left(\dfrac{dP}{dT}\right)_{sat}=\dfrac{h_{fg}}{T\,v_{fg}}$ ($T$ in **kelvin**); **Clapeyron–Clausius** at low pressure: $\ln\dfrac{P_2}{P_1}=\dfrac{h_{fg}}{R}\left(\dfrac1{T_1}-\dfrac1{T_2}\right)$.
5. **Joule–Thomson**: $\mu_{JT}=\left(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_h=-\dfrac1{c_p}\left[v-T\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]$; throttling is $h=$ const; $\mu_{JT}=0$ for an ideal gas.
6. **Real gas**: $Pv=ZRT$; $\Delta\bar h=\Delta\bar h_{ideal}-R_uT_{cr}(Z_{h2}-Z_{h1})$, $\ \Delta\bar s=\Delta\bar s_{ideal}-R_u(Z_{s2}-Z_{s1})$ (departure charts, $T_R=T/T_{cr}$, $P_R=P/P_{cr}$). Use $R_u$ with molar quantities and $R$ with per-kg quantities.

</Idea>

## Why property relations

The **state postulate** says a simple compressible substance is fixed by two independent intensive properties. Everything else is a *function* of those two: $s=s(T,P)$, $h=h(T,P)$, $u=u(T,v)$. We need the *derivatives* of these functions (how does $s$ change with $P$ at constant $T$?) and we want to express them through the quantities we can measure ($P$, $v$, $T$, $c_p$). The mathematics needed is only three facts.

**Exact differentials.** If $dz=M\,dx+N\,dy$ and $z=z(x,y)$ is a point function, then

$$M=\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_y,\ \ N=\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)_x\ \Rightarrow\ \left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right)_x=\left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\right)_y\quad(\text{the order of differentiation does not matter}).$$

**Reciprocity** $\left(\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z=\dfrac{1}{(\partial y/\partial x)_z}$ and the **cyclic relation** $\left(\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z\left(\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial z}\right)_x\left(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_y=-1$. These two let you flip, rearrange and eliminate derivatives.

## The Gibbs equations and the Maxwell relations

The first law for a simple compressible closed system, reversible: $\delta Q=T\,ds$ and $\delta W=P\,dv$, so $du=T\,ds-P\,dv$. Because it contains only properties, it holds for **any** process of a simple compressible substance. Define $h=u+Pv$, the Helmholtz function $a=u-Ts$ and the Gibbs function $g=h-Ts$ and you get the four forms:

| $du=T\,ds-P\,dv$ | $dh=T\,ds+v\,dP$ |
|---|---|
| $da=-s\,dT-P\,dv$ | $dg=-s\,dT+v\,dP$ |

Applying the exactness test to each gives the four **Maxwell relations**:

$$\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial v}\right)_s=-\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial s}\right)_v,\qquad \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_s=\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}\right)_P,\qquad \left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial v}\right)_T=\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v,\qquad \left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial P}\right)_T=-\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P.$$

**Why they matter.** The last two are the useful ones: they replace the derivative of the **unmeasurable entropy** by derivatives of the **measurable** $P$, $v$ and $T$. "They provide a means of determining the change in entropy, which cannot be measured directly, by simply measuring the changes in $P$, $v$, $T$."

**Quick checks with an ideal gas** ($Pv=RT$): $\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P=\dfrac RP$ and $\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v=\dfrac Rv$. So the fourth Maxwell relation says $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial P}\right)_T=-\dfrac RP$ (which is just $ds=-R\,dP/P$ at constant $T$: ✓) and the third says $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial v}\right)_T=\dfrac Rv$ (✓ $ds=R\,dv/v$).

**Other derivatives to know** (his sheet): the expansion coefficient $\beta=\dfrac1v\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P$ and the isothermal compressibility $\kappa=-\dfrac1v\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial P}\right)_T$; $c_v=T\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_v$ and $c_p=T\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_P$. The **Gibbs-equation ingredients** from $dh=T\,ds+v\,dP$: $T=\left(\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial s}\right)_P$ and $v=\left(\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial P}\right)_s$ (from which $u=h-P\left(\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial P}\right)_s$, $a=u-Ts$, $g=h-Ts$: the 12-17 exercise).

## General relations for du, dh, ds, cp and cv

Choose the independent variables that match the property: $u(T,v)$, $h(T,P)$, $s(T,v)$ or $s(T,P)$. Then the differential and the Maxwell relations give a result containing only $P,v,T$ and $c_v$ or $c_p$. For internal energy:

$$du=\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}\right)_vdT+\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial v}\right)_Tdv=c_v\,dT+\left[T\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial v}\right)_T-P\right]dv=c_v\,dT+\left[T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v-P\right]dv.$$

The second equality uses $du=T\,ds-P\,dv$ at constant $T$; the third uses the Maxwell relation $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial v}\right)_T=\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v$. Likewise (from $dh=T\,ds+v\,dP$ and $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial P}\right)_T=-\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P$):

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}du&=c_vdT+\left[T\left(\tfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v-P\right]dv,\qquad dh=c_pdT+\left[v-T\left(\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]dP\\ ds&=\frac{c_v}{T}dT+\left(\tfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_vdv=\frac{c_p}{T}dT-\left(\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_PdP\end{aligned}}$$

Integrate from 1 to 2 along any path (these are properties!), usually: first at constant $T_1$ then at constant pressure. Further relations the lecture lists: $c_p-c_v=-T\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P^2\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial v}\right)_T=\dfrac{vT\beta^2}{\kappa}$ (always $\ge0$, and equal to $R$ for an ideal gas), and the pressure and volume dependence of the specific heats, $(c_v-c_{v0})_T=T\int_{\infty}^{v}\left(\dfrac{\partial^2P}{\partial T^2}\right)_vdv$, $(c_p-c_{p0})_T=-T\int_0^P\left(\dfrac{\partial^2v}{\partial T^2}\right)_PdP$.

**Ideal gas as a test.** With $Pv=RT$: $T\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v-P=T\dfrac Rv-P=0$, so $du=c_vdT$ (the volume dependence vanishes: Joule's law). And $v-T\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P=v-\dfrac{RT}{P}=0$, so $dh=c_pdT$. With $\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P=\dfrac RP$: $ds=c_p\dfrac{dT}T-R\dfrac{dP}P$, the formula from chapter 8.

**A "different" gas: $P(v-a)=RT$** (the notebook's 12-34, 12-35, 12-42 family; $a$ is a constant). Then $\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v=\dfrac{R}{v-a}$ and $T\dfrac{R}{v-a}-P=0$: $du=c_vdT$ again (like an ideal gas). But $v=\dfrac{RT}P+a$ gives $v-T\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P=a$, so $dh=c_pdT+a\,dP$. For an isothermal process: $\Delta u=0$, $\Delta h=a\,(P_2-P_1)$ and $\Delta s=-R\ln\dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$ (his 12-42 ✓). The **enthalpy depends on pressure** for this fluid; for an ideal gas it does not.

## The Clapeyron equation

During a phase change at fixed $T$, the pressure is fixed too: $P=P_{sat}(T)$, so the quantity $\left(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_v$ in the third Maxwell relation is just the slope of the saturation curve on a P–T diagram. For the liquid-to-vapour change at constant $T$:

$$\frac{dP}{dT}\bigg|_{sat}=\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial v}\right)_T=\frac{s_g-s_f}{v_g-v_f}=\frac{s_{fg}}{v_{fg}}=\frac{h_{fg}}{T\,v_{fg}}\quad(\text{because } h_{fg}=T\,s_{fg}\text{ at constant }T\text{ and }P).$$

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{dP}{dT}\right)_{sat}=\frac{h_{fg}}{T\,v_{fg}}}\qquad\text{(Clapeyron, } T\text{ in kelvin)}$$

It works for **any** phase change (replace $fg$ by $12$: melting, sublimation). Two uses: (i) from the slope of the saturation curve and the two volumes you get $h_{fg}$ (or $h_{ig}$) *without a calorimeter*; (ii) from one measured point and $h_{fg}$ you can extend the saturation curve. At **low pressure**, with $v_f\ll v_g$ and the vapour ideal ($v_{fg}\approx v_g=RT/P$), it becomes the **Clapeyron–Clausius** equation

$$\left(\frac{dP}{dT}\right)_{sat}=\frac{h_{fg}P}{RT^2}\ \Rightarrow\ \ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}\approx\frac{h_{fg}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right).$$

Because $h_{fg}$ is nearly constant over a small temperature range, $\ln P_{sat}$ is nearly linear in $1/T$: this is how vapour-pressure charts are drawn and why antoine-type correlations work. The slope of the saturation curve is *positive* for liquid-vapour (always) and for solid-vapour (always); for solid-liquid it is positive for most substances but **negative for water**, because ice is less dense than water ($v_{12}<0$ while $h_{12}>0$).

## The Joule–Thomson coefficient

A **throttling valve** (a porous plug, a capillary tube, a partly open valve) is a steady-flow device with no heat, no work and negligible kinetic energy change, so $h_1=h_2$: **throttling is isenthalpic**. Pressure drops; what does the temperature do? The answer is the **Joule–Thomson coefficient**, defined as the slope of a constant-enthalpy line on a T–P diagram:

$$\mu_{JT}=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_h.$$

From $dh=c_p\,dT+\left[v-T\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]dP=0$:

$$\boxed{\mu_{JT}=-\frac1{c_p}\left[v-T\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]=\frac{1}{c_p}\left[T\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P-v\right]}$$

So $\mu_{JT}$ is computed from $c_p$ and the $P$–$v$–$T$ behaviour. Reading it:

- **$\mu_{JT}>0$**: the fluid **cools** on expansion (the pressure falls, $dP<0$, so $dT<0$). This is the case for most gases at room temperature, and the basis of liquefiers.
- **$\mu_{JT}<0$**: the fluid **warms** on expansion (hydrogen and helium at room temperature; they must be pre-cooled before they liquefy by J–T).
- **$\mu_{JT}=0$**: at the **inversion temperature** (the maximum-cooling boundary) *and for an ideal gas*: $T\dfrac RP-v=0$. Throttling an ideal gas does not change its temperature, since $h=h(T)$ and $h$ is constant (his 12-58C: "helium behaves as an ideal gas, so $T$ stays constant").

Physically: expansion makes the molecules move apart, against intermolecular attraction; the work done against attraction lowers the kinetic energy (cooling) if attraction dominates, while at high temperature and high density the repulsion dominates and the gas warms.

**A liquid throttled below its saturation pressure** is different: it **flashes**, a fraction $x$ of it boiling, and the mixture falls to $T_{sat}(P_2)$ with $h_2=h_1$, so $x_2=\dfrac{h_1-h_f(P_2)}{h_{fg}(P_2)}$: this is the expansion valve of a refrigerator (the quality of state 4 in chapter 11).

## Real gases: compressibility and the departure charts

The ideal-gas law fails at **high pressure** and **low temperature** (near the critical point). The simplest correction is the **compressibility factor**

$$Z=\frac{Pv}{RT}=\frac{v_{actual}}{v_{ideal}},\qquad P_R=\frac{P}{P_{cr}},\quad T_R=\frac{T}{T_{cr}},\quad v_R=\frac{v_{actual}}{RT_{cr}/P_{cr}}.$$

The **principle of corresponding states**: at the same reduced $(T_R,P_R)$ all gases have nearly the same $Z$. This gives one generalized chart (Nelson–Obert; the Lee–Kesler correlation is the numerical version used in the sim). Rules of thumb: $Z\approx1$ for $P_R\ll1$ (low pressure) and for $T_R>2$ at moderate pressure; $Z$ is lowest (≈ 0.3) near the critical point; for $T_R<1$ and $P_R$ above the saturation value the gas is a **liquid** and the vapour chart does not apply.

### Enthalpy and entropy departure

The ideal-gas $h$ and $s$ tables ignore pressure. The **enthalpy departure** is the difference between the ideal-gas enthalpy and the real enthalpy at the *same $T$ and $P$*, obtained from the $P$–$v$–$T$ data by integrating the general relation $dh=c_pdT+\left[v-T\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]dP$ along $T=$ const from $P\to0$:

$$Z_h=\frac{\bar h^*-\bar h}{R_uT_{cr}}\quad(\text{enthalpy departure factor}),\qquad Z_s=\frac{\bar s^*-\bar s}{R_u}\quad(\text{entropy departure factor}),$$

where $*$ denotes the ideal gas at the same $(T,P)$. Both come from the lower charts as functions of $(T_R,P_R)$. For a change of state **1 → 2** of a real gas:

$$\bar h_2-\bar h_1=\left(\bar h_2-\bar h_1\right)_{ideal}-R_uT_{cr}\left(Z_{h2}-Z_{h1}\right),\qquad \bar s_2-\bar s_1=\left(\bar s_2^\circ-\bar s_1^\circ-R_u\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)-R_u\left(Z_{s2}-Z_{s1}\right).$$

The procedure: (1) $T_{cr}$, $P_{cr}$ from Table A-1 (or the lib's critical constants); (2) $T_R$, $P_R$ at each state; (3) $Z_h$, $Z_s$ from the charts (or Lee–Kesler); (4) the ideal-gas change from the molar tables A-17 to A-25 (or $\int c_pdT$); (5) apply the corrections. **Use $R_u=8.314$ with molar ($\bar h$ in kJ/kmol); use $R=R_u/M$ with per-kg quantities** (his highlighted rule). The same machinery gives **mixture** properties with Kay's rule $T_{cr}'=\sum y_iT_{cr,i}$ (chapter 13).

<Remember title="When to use which tool">

- **Table exists for the fluid** (water, R-134a): use it; it is the real-gas answer.
- **Ideal gas** ($P_R<0.1$ or $T_R>2$ at moderate $P_R$): $Pv=RT$, $\Delta h=\int c_pdT$.
- **No table, high pressure / low $T$**: $Z$ and the departure charts (or an equation of state: $P(v-a)=RT$, van der Waals, etc., with $du$, $dh$, $ds$ integrated from the general relations).
- **Phase change**: Clapeyron (or tables).
- **Throttling**: $h=$ const; compute $\mu_{JT}$ if you need the local cooling rate.

</Remember>

## Habashi's traps

<Trap title="The slips on Anthony's own 'mistakes' sheet for chapter 12, and the instructor's remarks">

1. **Clapeyron needs $T$ in kelvin**: $\left(\dfrac{dP}{dT}\right)_{sat}=\dfrac{h_{fg}}{T\,v_{fg}}$.
2. **Throttled means $h$ is constant and $P$ drops** (not $T$ constant, not $s$ constant).
3. **Ideal gas**: $\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P=\dfrac RP$, $\mu_{JT}=0$, $du=c_vdT$ and $dh=c_pdT$.
4. **Departure formulas**: $\bar h_2-\bar h_1=(\bar h_2-\bar h_1)_{ideal}-R_uT_{cr}(Z_{h2}-Z_{h1})$; the entropy version has $-R_u\ln(P_2/P_1)$ inside the ideal part. **Use $R_u$ with molar $\bar h$; $R$ with $h$ per kg.**
5. **Maxwell's relations, boxed with a warning**: three of the four have signs that are easy to flip; derive them from $dh=T\,ds+v\,dP$ and $du=T\,ds-P\,dv$ if in doubt (the minus sign appears in the relation involving $s$ and $P$ at constant $T$).
6. **Sublimation uses $h_{ig}$, not $h_{fg}$.**
7. **"The generalized chart gives the real gas"**: only on average; check with tables when available (12-72: 14 % error).

</Trap>

## Check yourself

1. Starting from $dh=T\,ds+v\,dP$, show that $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial P}\right)_T=-\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Define $g=h-Ts$, so $dg=dh-T\,ds-s\,dT=v\,dP-s\,dT$. Exactness: $\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_P=-\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial P}\right)_T$. (This is the Maxwell relation from the Gibbs function.)

</details>

2. Water has $h_{fg}=2257$ kJ/kg at 100 °C (101.3 kPa). Use Clausius–Clapeyron to estimate $P_{sat}$ at 90 °C.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\ln\dfrac{P_2}{101.3}=\dfrac{2257}{0.4615}\left(\dfrac1{373.15}-\dfrac1{363.15}\right)=4890(-7.38\times10^{-5})=-0.361$, so $P_2=70.6$ kPa (the tables: 70.1 kPa).

</details>

3. Why is $\mu_{JT}=0$ for an ideal gas, and what is it for steam at 1 MPa, 300 °C?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$T(\partial v/\partial T)_P=T\,R/P=v$ makes the bracket vanish. Steam at 1 MPa is close to ideal, so $\mu_{JT}$ is small but positive: 12.4 K/MPa (it cools by 2.5 K for a 0.2 MPa drop).

</details>

4. A gas at $T_R=1.5$ and $P_R=1$ has $Z=0.91$. What volume does one kmol occupy relative to the ideal gas?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$v_{actual}=Z\,v_{ideal}=0.91\,v_{ideal}$: 9 % smaller than the ideal gas predicts (attractive forces dominate).

</details>

5. Why is the generalized departure chart a poor tool for steam at 8.6 MPa near saturation?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Water is polar and hydrogen-bonded, so it does not follow the "simple fluid" corresponding-states correlation; state 1 sits almost on the dome where $Z_h$ changes steeply. For water use the steam tables: the 12-72 comparison gives 976 versus 1129 kJ/kg.

</details>
