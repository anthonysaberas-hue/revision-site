# Combustion and adiabatic flame temperature

<div className="lede">

Burning a fuel is a chemical reaction that **conserves atoms, not moles**, and the energy bookkeeping adds one new idea to the first law: every substance's enthalpy has a **reference** (the enthalpy of formation) so that reactants and products, which are different molecules, can be compared. Chapter 15 therefore has three skills: (1) **balance the equation** and compute air–fuel ratio, products and dew point; (2) apply the first law with **formation enthalpies** to get the heat released ($Q$, HHV, LHV); (3) find the **adiabatic flame temperature** (AFT) by iteration. Habashi's closing slide says where students get trapped: *the meaning of AFT, balancing equations, mol versus kmol versus kg, what "percent excess air" means, HHV versus LHV*. This chapter is organised around those.

</div>

<Machine name="a car engine, a condensing boiler and a jet combustor">

Approximate or typical (≈):

- **Car engine**: the stoichiometric air–fuel ratio of gasoline is **≈ 14.7 kg air per kg fuel** (pure octane gives 15.09 in this chapter's equations; real gasoline contains oxygenates and aromatics). An **oxygen (λ) sensor** in the exhaust keeps the engine at **λ ≈ 1** (equivalence ratio 1) by trimming the injected fuel, because the **three-way catalyst** only removes CO, unburned HC and NOx together within a very narrow window around stoichiometric. Slightly lean gives NOx; slightly rich gives CO.
- **Condensing boiler** (home furnace): natural gas has HHV ≈ 55.5 MJ/kg and LHV ≈ 50.0 MJ/kg, so HHV/LHV = 1.11. Flue gas cooled below its **dew point (≈ 55–57 °C)** condenses the water vapour and recovers its latent heat. The result is a thermal efficiency of **≈ 90–98 % on the HHV basis**, which is **≈ 100–109 % on the LHV basis**: "more than 100 %" without violating anything, because the LHV never counted that latent heat.
- **Jet combustor**: overall the mixture is **very lean**, fuel–air ratio **f ≈ 0.02** (stoichiometric kerosene is f ≈ 0.067, so $\lambda\approx3.3$, equivalence ratio ≈ 0.3), because the turbine cannot take the stoichiometric flame temperature (≈ 2400 K); the flame burns near stoichiometric in the **primary zone** and the rest of the air is added downstream to dilute the gas to the turbine inlet temperature (≈ 1400–1700 K).
- Course connection: 2021 final problem 2 is exactly this: decane, $f=0.0206$, air at the compressor exit temperature, find T₄ (≈ 1360 K).

</Machine>

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="Combustion in one box">

1. **Air**: 21 % O₂, 79 % N₂, so **3.76 kmol N₂ per kmol O₂** and 4.76 kmol of air per kmol O₂. Air $M=28.97$.
2. **Theoretical (stoichiometric) air** = exactly enough O₂ for complete combustion; no O₂ in the products. **Excess air** is extra: 50 % excess = 150 % theoretical. $\lambda=\%$ theoretical air $/100$; equivalence ratio $\phi=1/\lambda$.
3. $AF=\dfrac{m_{air}}{m_{fuel}}=\dfrac{N_{air}M_{air}}{N_{fuel}M_{fuel}}$ (kg/kg), $FA=1/AF$.
4. **Dew point of products**: $P_v=y_{H_2O}P$, $T_{dp}=T_{sat}(P_v)$.
5. **Enthalpy of a substance**: $\bar h=\bar h_f^\circ+(\bar h-\bar h^\circ)$ (formation + sensible); closed system add $-R_uT$ per kmol of gas ($\bar u=\bar h-R_uT$).
6. **Steady flow**: $Q-W=\sum N_P(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ)_P-\sum N_R(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ)_R$ (kJ/kmol fuel, $Q$ positive *into* the system).
7. **Heating values**: $HHV=|\bar h_c|$ with liquid water; $LHV=HHV-m_wh_{fg}$, i.e. $|LHV|=|HHV|-2441.7\,m_w$ (open) or $-2304.3\,m_w$ (closed).
8. **AFT** ($Q=0$, $W=0$): $H_P=H_R$, solved by iteration on $T_P$.
9. **Liquid fuel**: $\bar h_f(liq)=\bar h_f(gas)-M\,h_{fg}$.

</Idea>

## Balancing: atoms in, atoms out

Write the fuel, then the air as $a_{th}(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\,\mathrm{N_2})$, and balance **carbon, hydrogen, then oxygen**. For $\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y$ with theoretical air:

$$\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y+\left(x+\tfrac{y}{4}\right)(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\,\mathrm{N_2})\to x\,\mathrm{CO_2}+\tfrac{y}{2}\,\mathrm{H_2O}+3.76\left(x+\tfrac{y}{4}\right)\mathrm{N_2}.$$

For excess air, multiply the air by $\lambda$ and add the leftover $(\lambda-1)a_{th}$ O₂ to the right side. For incomplete combustion move a fraction of C into CO and recompute the O₂ balance. Habashi's two rules of thumb (from your notes): **"always balance with O₂"** (the last element to balance) and **"when you compute the stoichiometric coefficient you assume perfect combustion"**: $a_{th}$ is defined by complete oxidation, even if the actual products contain CO.

## Enthalpy of formation, HHV and LHV

Reactants and products are different molecules, so the enthalpy zero must be **common**. The convention: **the enthalpy of formation $\bar h_f^\circ$ of each stable element (O₂, N₂, H₂, C) is zero at 25 °C and 1 atm**, and a compound's $\bar h_f^\circ$ is the heat released or absorbed forming it from the elements. The absolute enthalpy of a substance at $T$ is then

$$\bar h(T)=\bar h_f^\circ+\big(\bar h(T)-\bar h^\circ\big),\qquad \bar h^\circ=\bar h(298\ K)\ \text{(tabulated)}.$$

The **enthalpy of combustion** $\bar h_c=\sum N_P\bar h_f-\sum N_R\bar h_f$ is the heat released when a fuel burns completely at 25 °C and 1 atm and the products return to 25 °C. If the water is **liquid** it is the **HHV** (higher heating value); if it is **vapour** it is the **LHV**; they differ by the latent heat of the water formed:

$$|LHV|=|HHV|-m_wh_{fg}=|HHV|-2441.7\,m_w=|HHV|-44\,000\,N_w\quad(\text{open}),$$

and with the **internal-energy** analogue $u_{fg}=2304.3$ kJ/kg (41 523 kJ/kmol) for a constant-volume process. For methane the lib gives HHV 55 496 and LHV 50 010 kJ/kg (the table A-27 values are 55 530 and 50 050); the ratio is 1.110. His own check: for n-octane gas HHV = 48 255 kJ/kg and LHV = 44 790 kJ/kg ($\bar h_c=5\,512\,180$ and 5 116 090 kJ/kmol; the Dec-9 pass used $M=114$ and gave 44 877, slightly off).

For a **liquid fuel**: $\bar h_f(liq)=\bar h_f(gas)-M\,h_{fg}$ (propane: $-103\,850-44.097(335)=-118\,622$ kJ/kmol). His highlighted note on the 16-38 page: "liquid!!".

## The first law for reacting flows

For a **steady** combustor with no work, per kmol of fuel (Q positive into the system):

$$Q=\sum N_P\big(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ\big)_P-\sum N_R\big(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ\big)_R,$$

so the heat given to the surroundings is $q_{out}=H_R-H_P$. Note: fuel is normally at 25 °C so its enthalpy is $\bar h_f$ alone; the air may be at a different temperature. For a **closed rigid** system (a bomb calorimeter, an engine at constant volume) the same balance uses $\bar u=\bar h-P\bar v=\bar h-R_uT$ for every gas (and ≈ 0 for solids and liquids):

$$Q-W=\sum N_P\big(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ-R_uT_P\big)-\sum N_R\big(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ-R_uT_R\big).$$

## Adiabatic flame temperature

If a combustor loses no heat ($Q=0$, $W=0$), the products reach their **maximum temperature**, the **adiabatic flame temperature**: $H_P(T_{ad})=H_R$. Habashi's students are still puzzled by the idea that "enthalpy does not change": the **total** enthalpy (formation + sensible) is conserved, so as the chemical enthalpy is released (the products' $\bar h_f$ sum is much lower than the reactants'), the *sensible* enthalpy rises by the same amount. The released energy heats the products.

<AftGraphicalFigure fuel="CH4" airs={[1, 1.5, 2.5]} />

With tables, $T_{ad}$ requires iteration: guess $T_P$, evaluate $H_P$, compare with $H_R$, adjust (interpolate). The products are mostly N₂, so a good first guess uses N₂ alone. With constant specific heats it is direct. Three consequences to remember: (1) *more excess air → lower $T_{ad}$* (the same heat warms more gas); (2) **preheated air → higher $T_{ad}$** (more $H_R$); (3) the numbers are *upper bounds*, since real flames lose heat and **dissociate** (chapter 16 shows CO₂ and H₂O dissociating above ≈ 2000 K, which can lower the true stoichiometric flame temperature by 100–300 K).

## Entropy of reacting systems

The third law fixes an **absolute entropy** $\bar s^\circ$ for each substance at 25 °C and 1 atm (table A-26), and for an ideal gas at pressure $P_i$ and temperature $T$: $\bar s_i(T,P_i)=\bar s_i^\circ(T)-R_u\ln\dfrac{y_iP_m}{P_0}$ with the **partial** pressure (as in chapter 13). The second-law balance for a reactor is $S_{gen}=S_P-S_R+\dfrac{Q_{out}}{T_{surr}}$. It is what tells you, for example, that burning a fuel to heat water at 60 °C throws away most of its exergy (chapter 8).

## Habashi's traps and your own mistakes

<Trap>

- **Meaning of AFT**: "enthalpy doesn't change" means $H_P=H_R$ with formation enthalpies included, not that nothing happens. If you balance only sensible enthalpies you lose the whole point.
- **Balancing equations**: balance atoms, not moles; do O last (your note: "always balance with O₂").
- **Mol vs kmol vs kg**: the equation is per kmol of fuel; $AF$ is per kg. $Q$ per kmol times kmol/s gives kW only if $\bar h$ is in kJ/kmol.
- **% excess air**: 20 % excess = 120 % theoretical. "Stoichiometric" means complete combustion with *no* free O₂; "complete" combustion may still have excess O₂ (the marathon slides draw this distinction).
- **HHV vs LHV**: LHV = HHV − 2441.7 $m_w$ (open) or − 2304.3 $m_w$ (closed). Heat *transfer* problems use the actual product temperatures; "efficiency" problems must name the basis.
- **H₂O is gas in the products unless told otherwise**; it condenses only below the dew point (Pv = $y_{H_2O}P$).
- **Fuel at 25 °C** in the tables; the air can be at any temperature.
- **Closed (rigid) vessels**: add the $-R_uT$ terms (his reminder "fixed volume → $-R_uT$").
- **Heat of formation of a liquid fuel = gas value − $M\,h_{fg}$.**

</Trap>

2. Why is the LHV efficiency of a condensing boiler allowed to exceed 100 %?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The LHV is defined as if the water in the products leaves as vapour; a condensing boiler cools the flue gas below its dew point and recovers that latent heat, which was never counted in the LHV. On the HHV basis the efficiency stays below 100 %. For methane the ratio is HHV/LHV = 1.11, so 90 % HHV = 100 % LHV.

</details>

3. Why does adding air lower the adiabatic flame temperature?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The heat release is fixed by the fuel; more air means more moles of N₂ and O₂ in the products to share it. $T_{ad}$ falls from ≈ 2330 K at stoichiometric methane to ≈ 1480 K at 200 % theoretical air.

</details>

4. A fuel is burned in a rigid vessel instead of a steady-flow combustor. What changes in the energy balance?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use internal energy: for every gas $\bar u=\bar h-R_uT$, so each term $N(\bar h_f+\bar h-\bar h^\circ)$ gets $-N R_uT$ (initial and final temperatures). Solids and liquids need no correction.

5. The stoichiometric equation uses theoretical O₂ even if the problem says 15 % of the carbon burns to CO. Why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$a_{th}$ is *defined* as the O₂ for complete combustion and sets the percent theoretical air. The actual air is $\lambda a_{th}$, and the *product* balance (with CO and leftover O₂) is then done with the actual O₂.

</details>
