# Psychrometrics and air conditioning

<div className="lede">

Atmospheric air is a mixture of **dry air** and **water vapour**, and the vapour is the part that changes: it condenses on a cold coil, evaporates in a cooling tower, and sets how muggy a room feels. Because the vapour is at a very low pressure it is treated as an ideal gas, so this chapter is chapter 13 with one new twist: **the vapour's partial pressure cannot exceed the saturation pressure at the air temperature**. Everything follows from that ceiling. You need five numbers for a state ($T$, $\varphi$, $\omega$, $h$, $T_{dp}$ or $T_{wb}$), and two give you the rest.

</div>

<Machine name="a rooftop HVAC unit and a power-plant cooling tower">

Approximate or typical (≈):

- **Comfort band**: **≈ 22–24 °C and 40–60 % relative humidity**. Above 60 % the sweat does not evaporate; below 30 % the air dries the nose.
- **Rooftop unit**: outside air (e.g. 35 °C, 45 %) is mixed with return air, pulled across a **cooling coil** at ≈ 7–12 °C (colder than the mixed air's dew point, so it dehumidifies), then often **reheated** a few degrees. In the figure below the coil removes ≈ 21 kJ per kg of dry air and 2.2 g/kg of water; the reheat adds ≈ 4 kJ/kg to bring the supply air from 100 % to ≈ 77 % relative humidity. Sizing is in **tons**: 1 ton = 211 kJ/min = 3.517 kW.
- **Natural-draft cooling tower**: the concrete hyperbolic towers of power stations are **≈ 100–200 m tall**; the chimney effect of the warm, moist, light air draws the cooling air without a fan. They evaporate ≈ **1–3 % of the circulating flow** as make-up water: in the class example below 1.8 kg/s evaporates from 100 kg/s, and that latent heat is ≈ 80 % of the heat removed.
- **Why it matters for thermodynamics**: the condenser of a Rankine cycle (chapter 10) at 5–10 kPa needs water at ≈ 25–35 °C, and the tower is how that water is re-cooled to within a few kelvin of the **wet-bulb temperature**.

</Machine>

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="Moist air in one box">

1. $P=P_a+P_v$ (Dalton). $P_v=\varphi P_g$ with $P_g=P_{sat}(T)$ from the steam table. $T_{dp}=T_{sat}$ at $P_v$.
2. **Humidity ratio** $\omega=\dfrac{m_v}{m_a}=\dfrac{0.622\,P_v}{P-P_v}=\dfrac{0.622\,\varphi P_g}{P-\varphi P_g}$. **Relative humidity** $\varphi=\dfrac{\omega P}{(0.622+\omega)P_g}$.
3. **Enthalpy per kg of dry air**: $h=h_a+\omega h_v\approx1.005\,T+\omega(2501.3+1.82\,T)$ ($T$ in °C; $h_v\approx h_g$ at low $P$).
4. **Volume per kg dry air**: $v=\dfrac{R_a T}{P-P_v}$ ($R_a=0.287$). **Masses**: $m_a=\dfrac{P_aV}{R_aT}$, $m_v=\omega m_a$.
5. **Adiabatic saturation / wet bulb**: $\omega_1=\dfrac{c_p(T_2-T_1)+\omega_2h_{fg2}}{h_{g1}-h_{f2}}$, with $T_2=T_{wb}$ and state 2 saturated: $\omega_2=\dfrac{0.622P_{g2}}{P-P_{g2}}$.
6. **Always (the golden rule)**: $\dot m_{a1}=\dot m_{a2}$; $\dot m_{a}\omega_1+\dot m_w=\dot m_a\omega_2$; $\dot m_ah_1+\dot m_wh_w=\dot m_ah_2$ (plus $Q$, $W$).
7. **Adiabatic mixing**: $\dfrac{\dot m_{a1}}{\dot m_{a2}}=\dfrac{\omega_2-\omega_3}{\omega_3-\omega_1}=\dfrac{h_2-h_3}{h_3-h_1}$: the mixed state is on the straight line between the two.
8. **Cooling tower**: $\dot m_a=\dfrac{\dot m_3(h_3-h_4)}{(h_2-h_1)-(\omega_2-\omega_1)h_4}$; make-up $=\dot m_a(\omega_2-\omega_1)$.

</Idea>

## Humidity, dew point and wet bulb

**Relative humidity** $\varphi$ is the vapour pressure as a fraction of what the air could hold at that temperature. It *changes with temperature even if the water content does not*: warm the air and $\varphi$ falls, because $P_g$ grows. **Humidity ratio** $\omega$ is the actual water content (kg water per kg *dry* air) and does not change unless water is added or removed. That is why $\omega$ is the quantity that balances: two states connected by pure heating have the same $\omega$ and different $\varphi$.

The **dew point** is the temperature at which air with a given $\omega$ becomes saturated when cooled at constant pressure: $T_{dp}=T_{sat}$ at the actual vapour pressure $P_v=\varphi P_g$. Cool a surface below it and the water on it condenses (window fogging, a cold glass). The **wet-bulb temperature** is the temperature of a wet wick in an air stream: evaporation cools it until the heat from the air balances the heat of vaporisation. It is also the **adiabatic saturation temperature**, the equilibrium temperature of air blown over water in an insulated duct. Habashi uses the two relations of item 5 above; at high humidity the wet bulb approaches the dry bulb.

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Use the right pressure">

The formulas contain the **total pressure** $P$, which is not always 101.325 kPa (the course problems use 95 and 100 kPa; 14-32 is at 95 kPa; the 2021 final at 100 kPa). Always check the statement. The lib's psychrometric functions all take $P$ as an explicit argument for that reason.

</Callout>

<Statement>

<Think>

The standard opener. Tables: A-4 (saturation) for $P_g$ at 25 °C. The partial pressure of the dry air is the total minus the vapour, and the masses come from the ideal-gas law *with the partial pressure of each component*: $m_a=P_aV/(R_aT)$, not $PV/(R_aT)$.

</Think>

<Plan>

</Step>
<Step title="3. Enthalpy">

</Step>

$V=75$ m³, $R_a=0.287$. $m_a=\dfrac{97.62(75)}{0.287(298.15)}=\mathbf{85.6}$ kg; $m_v=\omega m_a=0.01515(85.6)=\mathbf{1.30}$ kg (his: 85.6 kg and 1.30 kg).

</Step>
</Steps>

## The psychrometric chart

The chart plots $\omega$ against dry-bulb $T$ with curves of constant $\varphi$ (the top one, 100 %, is the saturation curve and the ceiling of the chart), constant $h$ and $T_{wb}$ (nearly parallel slanted lines), and volume $v$. Any two properties fix a point. Every process in this chapter is a line on it, which is the reason to learn to *see* it before computing:

| Process | Direction on the chart | $\omega$ | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensible heating | to the right, horizontal | constant | $q=h_2-h_1$ |
| Sensible cooling | to the left, horizontal, until saturation | constant | $q=h_1-h_2$ |
| Cooling below $T_{dp}$ | left along $\omega$ then down the saturation curve | falls (condensate) | $q=h_1-h_2-(\omega_1-\omega_2)h_f$ |
| Humidification by steam | nearly straight up | rises | steam enthalpy added |
| Evaporative cooling | up and left along constant $T_{wb}$ (≈ constant $h$) | rises | $h_2\approx h_1$ |
| Adiabatic mixing | straight line between the states | mass average | $\dot m_ah$ adds |

<Problem id="Example 14-5" title="Heating and humidification: furnace plus a steam humidifier" tags="heating, humidification, mass and energy balance">

<Statement>

Air at 10 °C and 30 % relative humidity, 45 m³/min, 100 kPa, is heated to 22 °C and then humidified with steam to 25 °C and 60 % relative humidity. Find the heating rate and the steam flow.

</Statement>

<Think>

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. State 1">

<T>A-4: Pg at 10 °C = 1.2282 kPa</T>. $P_{v1}=0.3(1.2282)=0.3685$ kPa, $\omega_1=\dfrac{0.622(0.3685)}{100-0.3685}=\mathbf{0.0023}$ (his: $2.7\times10^{-3}$, a chart reading), $h_1=1.005(10)+0.0023(2501.3+18.2)=15.85$ kJ/kg, $v_1=\dfrac{0.287(283.15)}{99.63}=0.8156$ m³/kg.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Dry-air flow">

$\dot m_a=\dfrac{45}{0.8156}=\mathbf{55.2}$ kg/min.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Heater (ω constant)">

$h_2=1.005(22)+0.0023(2501.3+40.0)=27.96$ kJ/kg. $\dot Q=\dot m_a(h_2-h_1)=55.17(27.96-15.85)=\mathbf{668}$ kJ/min (11.1 kW). The textbook prints 673 from chart readings; the table calculation is 668.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Humidifier">

State 3: <T>A-4: Pg at 25 °C = 3.1698 kPa; Pv = 0.6 × 3.1698 = 1.902 kPa</T> gives $\omega_3=\dfrac{0.622(1.902)}{100-1.902}=0.01206$ (the same as the review example 14 value). $\dot m_w=\dot m_a(\omega_3-\omega_1)=55.17(0.01206-0.00230)=\mathbf{0.538}$ kg/min.

</Step>

## Cooling and dehumidification, and the 2021 final problem

Air conditioning **cools below the dew point**: the coil is colder than the air's $T_{dp}$, so vapour condenses and the air leaves the coil nearly saturated at the coil-exit temperature. Then a heating coil brings the temperature back up at constant $\omega$. Cooling load = (enthalpy drop) − (condensate enthalpy): $\dot Q_{out}=\dot m_a(h_1-h_2)-\dot m_wh_f$. This is the chart path of the sim above: left along constant $\omega$ until the saturation curve, then down the curve.

<Problem id="Final 2021 · P5" title="Air conditioner: cooling coil, then a heater, 11 tons" tags="dehumidification, heating coil, tons, condensate">

<Statement>

Air at 30 °C, 100 kPa and 70 % relative humidity enters a cooling coil with a capacity of 11 tons (1 ton = 211 kJ/min) and leaves saturated. It is then reheated to 25 °C and 40 % relative humidity. Find the coil exit temperature, the dry-air flow, the entering volume flow, the condensate and the heating-coil load.

</Statement>

<Think>

The exit humidity ratio is set by the *final* state: the heater does not change $\omega$, so $\omega_2=\omega_3$ fixes the saturated state 2 and hence $T_2$ (the dew point of the final air). Then the coil's energy balance gives $\dot m_a$.

</Think>

<Plan>

$\omega_1$, $h_1$, $v_1$ → $\omega_3=\omega_2$ from (25 °C, 40 %) → $P_{g2}$ → $T_2$ → $h_2$ → $\dot m_a=\dot Q/[h_1-h_2-(\omega_1-\omega_2)h_{f2}]$ → condensate, $\dot V_1$ → heater.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Inlet state (P = 100 kPa)">

$P_{g}(30\,°C)=4.2469$ kPa, $P_{v1}=2.973$ kPa, $\omega_1=\dfrac{0.622(2.973)}{100-2.973}=\mathbf{0.01906}$ (his 0.01880), $h_1=1.005(30)+0.01906(2501.3+54.6)=78.86$ kJ/kg, $v_1=\dfrac{0.287(303.15)}{97.03}=0.8967$ m³/kg.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Coil exit from the final state">

$P_{v3}=0.4(3.1698)=1.268$ kPa, $\omega_3=\omega_2=\dfrac{0.622(1.268)}{100-1.268}=\mathbf{0.007988}$. Saturated at $\omega_2$: $P_{g2}=P_{v3}$, so $T_2=T_{sat}(1.268\ kPa)=\mathbf{10.5}$ °C (his 10.2). $h_2=1.005(10.5)+0.007988(2501.3+19.1)=30.66$ kJ/kg.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Dry-air flow">

$\dot Q=11(211)=2321$ kJ/min. $h_f(10.5\,°C)=44.0$ kJ/kg, so $\dot m_a=\dfrac{2321}{78.86-30.66-(0.01906-0.007988)(44.0)}=\dfrac{2321}{47.71}=\mathbf{48.6}$ kg/min (his 48.05: he left out the condensate enthalpy term, a 1 % effect).

</Step>
<Step title="4. Volume flow, condensate, reheater">

$\dot V_1=\dot m_av_1=48.65(0.8967)=\mathbf{43.6}$ m³/min. Condensate $=\dot m_a(\omega_1-\omega_2)=48.65(0.011072)=\mathbf{0.539}$ kg/min. Heater: state 3 is (25 °C, $\omega_3$) with $h_3=1.005(25)+0.007988(2501.3+45.5)=45.47$, so $\dot Q=48.65(45.47-30.66)/60=\mathbf{12.0}$ kW.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$T_2=10.5$ °C, $\dot m_a=48.6$ kg/min, $\dot V_1=43.6$ m³/min, condensate 0.539 kg/min, heating coil 12.0 kW.

</Answer>

</RealWorld>

## Cooling towers

A **wet cooling tower** rejects the condenser's heat by letting the warm water trickle down through a rising air stream. Part of the water evaporates, taking its latent heat from the rest. The balances: dry air $\dot m_{a}$ is constant; water: $\dot m_3=\dot m_4+\dot m_{makeup}$ with $\dot m_{makeup}=\dot m_a(\omega_2-\omega_1)$; energy: $\dot m_ah_1+\dot m_3h_3=\dot m_ah_2+\dot m_4h_4$, which gives the formula in the box above (Habashi writes $h_4$ with the cold-water enthalpy $h_f$ at $T_4$). Two terms to know: **range** $T_3-T_4$, and **approach** $T_4-T_{wb,in}$, which can never be negative.

## Habashi's traps and your own mistakes

<Trap>

- **Saturated means $\varphi=100$ %** and $\omega_{sat}=0.622P_g/(P-P_g)$ at the *air temperature*.
- **Non-standard $P$** (95, 100 kPa): $\omega$, $v$ and $h_{fg}$ lines all change; the chart for 101.325 kPa cannot be used (the 14-32 answers at 95 kPa are $\omega\approx0.0094$ and $\varphi\approx47$ %, printed 47.6 %).
- **Chart reading accuracy**: your solutions of 14-84, 14-102, 14-132 agree with the formula to 1–2 %. His 14-132 shows $\dot Q=3.65$ kW vs the table-based 3.73 kW; condensate 0.072 kg/min and refrigerant flow 1.41 vs 1.44 kg/min.
- **Fogging** (Example 14-2): the dew point of the room air, $T_{dp}=T_{sat}(\varphi P_g)=15.4$ °C at 20 °C and 75 %, tells you the window temperature at which fog begins.
- **Humidity ratio per dry air, not per moist air**: $\dot m_a$ is *dry* air throughout.
- **Cooling tower**: the make-up term $(\omega_2-\omega_1)h_4$ is small but not zero.

## Check yourself

1. Air at 30 °C with $\omega=0.0150$ is cooled to 20 °C at constant pressure. What happens, and what is $T_{dp}$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P_v=\dfrac{0.015(101.325)}{0.622+0.015}=2.386$ kPa, so $T_{dp}\approx20.3$ °C. Cooling to 20 °C reaches essentially saturation: just at the dew point. Any further cooling would condense water.

</details>

2. Why does $\varphi$ fall when air is heated, although the water content is constant?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\varphi=P_v/P_g(T)$. Heating leaves $P_v$ (and $\omega$) unchanged but raises $P_g$ steeply (about 6 % per kelvin), so $\varphi$ falls.

</details>

3. In the mixing of two streams, why is the mixed state on a straight line, and where on the line?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\omega$ and $h$ are mass-weighted averages, so the mixed point divides the line between the two states in the *inverse* ratio of the dry-air flows (closer to the larger stream). Because the saturation curve is convex, the straight line can pass above it, which means fog.

</details>

4. In the cooling-coil energy balance, why is there a term for the condensate?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The condensate leaves the coil as liquid at $T_2$ carrying its own enthalpy $h_f$, so you must subtract $(\omega_1-\omega_2)h_f$. Omitting it is the 1 % error on your 2021 final page.

</details>

5. What limits how cold a cooling tower can make the water?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The wet-bulb temperature of the entering air: the water can only approach it. With the tower at 20 °C and 60 % ($T_{wb}\approx14.6$ °C) the 22 °C outlet is 7 K above it (approach 7 K).

</details>
