# Chemical and phase equilibrium

<div className="lede">

Chapter 15 assumed that combustion goes to completion. At high temperature it does not: CO₂ splits back into CO and O₂, and N₂ and O₂ combine into NO. A reacting mixture at fixed $T$ and $P$ settles where the **Gibbs function is a minimum**, and that composition is fixed by one number, the **equilibrium constant** $K_p(T)$. The chapter is therefore short on theory and long on a recipe: write the reaction, write $K_p$ in terms of the unknown amounts, solve. Habashi lists **six remarks** about $K_p$ that "some students do not easily digest"; they are in a box below and are the most examinable part.

</div>

<Machine name="NOx in engines, the catalytic converter and the Haber process">

Approximate or typical (≈):

- **NOx in engines**: the NO that comes out of an engine is mostly **thermal NO**, made in the hot burned gas by the Zeldovich chain ($\mathrm{O}+\mathrm{N_2}\to\mathrm{NO}+\mathrm{N}$, $\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{O_2}\to\mathrm{NO}+\mathrm{O}$). It is **strongly temperature-dependent**: the equilibrium table A-28 value for ½N₂ + ½O₂ ⇌ NO gives, for air at 1 atm, about **0.13 % NO at 1500 K, 0.8 % at 2000 K and 2.3 % at 2500 K** (the lib's equilibrium calculation below). Real engines make far less (≈ hundreds to a few thousand ppm) because the reaction is *slower* than the cooling of the gas, but the trend is the same: this is why NOx is cut by lowering the peak flame temperature (exhaust gas recirculation, lean burn, staged combustion, water injection).
- **Three-way catalytic converter**: at ≈ 300–800 °C a platinum–palladium–rhodium catalyst converts CO + ½O₂ → CO₂ (exothermic, so $K_p$ is huge at these temperatures), unburned HC → CO₂ + H₂O, and 2NO + 2CO → N₂ + 2CO₂ together, but only when the engine runs at $\lambda\approx1$ (chapter 15). The catalyst changes the **rate**; equilibrium decides how far the reaction *can* go, and at exhaust temperatures it favours the clean products completely.
- **Haber–Bosch (ammonia)**: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ is **exothermic** and reduces the number of moles ($\Delta\nu=-2$). Le Chatelier says *low T and high P* favour NH₃, but a low T is too slow, so plants compromise at **≈ 400–500 °C and 150–300 bar** over an iron catalyst, with unreacted gas recycled (≈ 15–25 % conversion per pass). The process feeds roughly half the world's food through fertiliser.

</Machine>

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="Equilibrium in one box">

1. **Criterion**: $(dG)_{T,P}=0$. A reaction at fixed $T,P$ can only go in the direction that *decreases* $G$; equilibrium is the minimum. For a reaction $\nu_AA+\nu_BB\rightleftharpoons\nu_CC+\nu_DD$: $\nu_C\bar g_C+\nu_D\bar g_D-\nu_A\bar g_A-\nu_B\bar g_B=0$.
2. **Equilibrium constant** (ideal gases): $K_p=\dfrac{P_C^{\nu_C}P_D^{\nu_D}}{P_A^{\nu_A}P_B^{\nu_B}}=\dfrac{N_C^{\nu_C}N_D^{\nu_D}}{N_A^{\nu_A}N_B^{\nu_B}}\left(\dfrac{P}{N_{total}}\right)^{\Delta\nu}$, with $P$ in **atm** and $\Delta\nu=(\nu_C+\nu_D)-(\nu_A+\nu_B)$ (gas phase only; pure solids and liquids drop out).
3. **From the Gibbs function**: $\ln K_p=-\dfrac{\Delta G^*(T)}{R_uT}$, with $\Delta G^*=\sum\nu\bar g^*_P-\sum\nu\bar g^*_R$ at 1 atm (table A-28 lists $\ln K_p$ for seven reactions).
4. **Van 't Hoff**: $\dfrac{d\ln K_p}{dT}=\dfrac{\bar h_R(T)}{R_uT^2}$; with $\bar h_R$ constant, $\ln\dfrac{K_{p2}}{K_{p1}}=\dfrac{\bar h_R}{R_u}\left(\dfrac1{T_1}-\dfrac1{T_2}\right)$.
5. **Six remarks**: see the box below.
6. **Phase equilibrium**: two phases of a pure substance are in equilibrium when $g_f=g_g$ (same $T$ and $P$).
7. **Recipe**: unknown coefficients $x,y,z$ from atom balances (and $K_p$), then $N_{total}$, then Kp relation, then solve; then an enthalpy balance as in chapter 15 if a heat transfer or flame temperature is wanted.

</Idea>

## Why equilibrium is a minimum of G

Consider 1 kmol of CO₂ at fixed $T$ and $P$ and let $\xi$ kmol dissociate into CO and ½O₂. The mixture's Gibbs function $G(\xi)=\sum N_i[\bar g_i^*(T)+R_uT\ln(y_iP)]$ has two competing parts: the **chemical** part (CO₂ is more stable, so $G$ rises as it dissociates) and the **mixing** part (a mixture of three gases has a lower $G$ than a pure one, so $G$ falls as the mixture forms). The sum has a minimum between 0 and 1: that is the equilibrium. The sim moves it with $T$ and $P$.

<GibbsMinimumSim />

At the minimum the slope is zero, which is the equation $\sum\nu_i\bar g_i=0$ of item 1; with the ideal-gas $\bar g_i=\bar g_i^*+R_uT\ln(P_i/P_0)$ it becomes exactly the $K_p$ relation of item 2. So $K_p=\exp(-\Delta G^*/R_uT)$ is a statement about the *standard* Gibbs functions only, and therefore **depends on $T$ alone**.

## The equilibrium constant and its six remarks

<Idea title="Habashi's six remarks about Kp">

1. **$K_p$ depends on $T$ only.** (Not on $P$, not on the amounts.)
2. $K_p$ of the **reverse** reaction is $1/K_p$.
3. **The larger $K_p$, the more complete the reaction** (products favoured); $\ln K_p>0$ means products win.
4. **Pressure** affects the equilibrium *composition* but **not** $K_p$ (through $(P/N_{total})^{\Delta\nu}$).
5. **Inert gases** affect the composition (they raise $N_{total}$) but **not** $K_p$.
6. If the stoichiometric coefficients are **doubled**, $K_p$ is **squared** (and halved coefficients give $\sqrt{K_p}$): always use the $K_p$ of the reaction *as you wrote it*.

</Idea>

Remark 4 and 5 are two views of the same fact: in $K_p=\dots(P/N_{total})^{\Delta\nu}$, raising $P$ or lowering $N_{total}$ changes the factor in the same way. If $\Delta\nu>0$ (dissociation: more moles on the right), **lower pressure or added inert favours the products**; if $\Delta\nu<0$ (synthesis), **higher pressure** does. If $\Delta\nu=0$ (like NO formation), neither matters. This is Le Chatelier's principle in equations.

## Van 't Hoff: temperature dependence

$K_p$ depends on $T$ through $\Delta G^*/R_uT$, and differentiating gives $d\ln K_p/dT=\bar h_R/R_uT^2$. The sign of the reaction enthalpy therefore decides the direction: **endothermic ($\bar h_R>0$): $K_p$ rises with $T$** (dissociation is favoured at high $T$); **exothermic: $K_p$ falls with $T$**, so combustion products are *less complete* at higher temperature. It also works backward: from two values of $K_p$ you get $\bar h_R$ without the enthalpy tables, "from a knowledge of $K_p$ which is easier to determine" (Habashi's slide). In the first sim above, $\ln K_p$ against $1/T$ is nearly straight, with slope $-\bar h_R/R_u$.

## The recipe: composition from Kp

1. Write the balanced reaction as **the A-28 reaction** you will use, with its $\ln K_p$ (or the reverse, with a sign change).
2. Write the amounts at equilibrium in terms of one unknown $x$ (the extent), from initial amounts and stoichiometry.
3. Write $N_{total}$ (include inerts, **exclude pure solids/liquids**).
4. Write $K_p=\prod N_i^{\nu_i}\,(P/N_{total})^{\Delta\nu}$ with $P$ in atm and solve (by hand or iteration).
5. If the question asks for a heat transfer or a temperature, then do an enthalpy balance with the *actual* product amounts.

## Dissociation changes the flame temperature

When CO₂ and H₂O dissociate, the reaction is endothermic, so it **absorbs** heat and lowers the flame temperature, and the products contain CO, H₂, O₂, OH. Habashi's recipe for such problems: use excess air, write the equation with CO₂, CO and O₂ in the products, write the $K_p$ relation for CO₂ ⇌ CO + ½O₂ to find the coefficients, *then* do the enthalpy balance as in chapter 15. If nothing dissociates, $Q=\dot n(h_2-h_1)$. For example (16-40): heating O₂ from 25 °C to 3000 K at 1 atm with the O₂ ⇌ 2O reaction ($\ln K_p=-4.357$) gives 0.113 kmol of O per kmol of O₂ at equilibrium; the heat transfer is 1986 kJ/min for 0.5 kg/min (your 1966), against 1533 kJ/min without dissociation (your 1532.7). The dissociated oxygen alone costs ≈ 30 % more heat.

At 4000 K, which is more likely: CO₂ → CO + ½O₂ ($\ln K_p=+1.61$) or N₂ + O₂ → 2NO (½-form: $\ln K_p=-1.21$)? The first: $K_p>1$ means it is **past 50 %** (the 2021 final problem 3, a pure comparison of $\ln K_p$ values).

## Phase equilibrium (short)

Two phases of a pure substance at the same $T$ and $P$ are in equilibrium when their specific Gibbs functions are equal, $g_f=g_g$. That is why $h_{fg}=Ts_{fg}$ on the saturation line (since $g=h-Ts$) and the foundation of the Clapeyron equation of chapter 12.

## Habashi's traps and your own mistakes

<Trap>

- **$K_p$ depends on $T$ only**: exothermic → higher $T$ → lower $K_p$ (less complete). Heating can make a product mixture *worse*.
- **16-4C** ($\mathrm{CO}+\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{CO_2}$): raising $T$ lowers $K_p$ and CO₂; raising $P$ at constant $T$ ($\Delta\nu=-\tfrac12$) *increases* CO₂.
- **16-8C** (NO/O₂/N₂ with $P$ tripled): $K_p$ unchanged and $\Delta\nu=0$, so the composition is unchanged.
- **16-52C**: combustion is more complete at 2000 K than at 3000 K: less dissociation.
- **Inert gases at constant total $P$**: they lower $P/N_{total}$, so they shift the equilibrium *exactly like a pressure decrease*, toward the side with **more moles**.
- **P in atm**: $K_p$ is defined with $P_0=1$ atm; with $P$ in kPa you must divide by 101.325.
- **Coefficients**: halving or doubling the reaction squares or roots $K_p$; the reverse reaction inverts it.
- **Liquid fuel in 16-38**: use $\bar h_f(liq)$ (your note "liquid!!"): $Q=190\,300$ kJ/kmol, 5180 kJ/min for 1.2 kg/min of propane (printed 5066: 2 % lower).

</Trap>

## Check yourself

1. For $\mathrm{N_2}+3\mathrm{H_2}\rightleftharpoons2\mathrm{NH_3}$ (exothermic), what do higher $T$ and higher $P$ do to the NH₃ yield?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Higher $T$ lowers $K_p$ (exothermic) and so lowers the yield. Higher $P$ raises it, because $\Delta\nu=-2$ and the product side has fewer moles. Industry compromises: 400–500 °C for speed, 150–300 bar to win back the yield.

</details>

2. If $\ln K_p=-5$ for a reaction, is it nearly complete?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$K_p=0.0067$, so the reactants are strongly favoured: the reaction hardly proceeds. Products are only a small fraction. The reverse reaction has $K_p=150$ and is nearly complete.

</details>

3. Why does adding N₂ at constant $T$ and $P$ shift CO₂ ⇌ CO + ½O₂ to the right?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$K_p$ is fixed; $N_{total}$ increases, so $(P/N_{total})^{1/2}$ decreases and the mole ratio $\dfrac{N_{CO}N_{O_2}^{1/2}}{N_{CO_2}}$ must increase to compensate. The side with more moles wins (Le Chatelier).

</details>

4. Doubling the reaction $\mathrm{H_2O}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{H_2}+\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}$ to $2\mathrm{H_2O}\rightleftharpoons2\mathrm{H_2}+\mathrm{O_2}$: what is the new $K_p$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$K_{p,new}=K_p^2$ (remark 6), i.e. $\ln K_{p,new}=2\ln K_p$. Using the table value without doubling it is the standard error.

</details>

5. How does the equilibrium NO from 1500 K to 2500 K change in air at 1 atm?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It rises from about 0.13 % to 2.3 %, a factor of about 18 for 1000 K (or about 3 % per 10 K near 2000 K). The reaction is endothermic ($\bar h_R=+90$ MJ/kmol), so $K_p$ and the NO fraction climb with $T$; real engines stay below this because the reaction is kinetically limited.

</details>
