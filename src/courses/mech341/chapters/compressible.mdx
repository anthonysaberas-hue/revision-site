# Compressible flow and nozzles

<div className="lede">

When a gas moves at a good fraction of the speed of sound its density changes along the flow, and the simple "incompressible" intuition fails: a converging duct *slows* a supersonic flow and a diverging one *accelerates* it. The chapter is four things: **stagnation properties** (what a probe reads), the **Mach number** (flow speed against sound speed), **isentropic flow with area change** (nozzles and diffusers), and **choking** (why a nozzle's flow rate cannot grow beyond the sonic throat). Habashi's last warning of the semester: "many students, now at the end of the semester, are still not able to make the difference between **static and total**".

</div>

<Machine name="an airliner at cruise and a rocket nozzle">

Approximate or typical (≈):

- **Airliner cruise**: **Ma ≈ 0.78–0.85 at ≈ 11 km** (36 000 ft). At 11 km the standard atmosphere is 216.65 K (−56.5 °C) and 22.6 kPa, so the speed of sound is $c=\sqrt{kRT}=295$ m/s and the flight speed is **≈ 230–251 m/s (≈ 830–900 km/h)**. A pitot-static probe sees the *stagnation* values: $T_0\approx243$–248 K (≈ −30 to −25 °C: **the skin of the nose is 26–31 K warmer than the air**) and $P_0\approx33.8$–36.3 kPa. Cruise is held *below* Ma 1 because local flow over the wing reaches Ma 1 first and wave drag rises sharply (the drag-divergence Mach number). The Airbus A340 problem (17-26): 945 km/h at −60 °C is Ma = 0.897.
- **Rocket nozzle**: the combustion chamber (≈ 10–20 MPa, $T_0\approx3500$ K, $k\approx1.2$) feeds a **converging–diverging (de Laval) nozzle**: the sonic throat sets the mass flow and the diverging bell expands the gas to **Ma ≈ 3–5**. For $k=1.2$, an area ratio $A_e/A^*=15$ gives $Ma_e=3.56$ and $77.5$ gives 4.7 (the lib's numbers). Sea-level nozzles are kept short to avoid flow separation; vacuum nozzles are long and wide. More on shocks, over- and under-expansion in the MECH 430 compressible-flow course ([MECH 430](#/mech430)).
- **Converging nozzle**: a garden hose or an aircraft engine's nozzle (turbofan exhausts such as the CFM56's are convergent and choke at high thrust). Once the nozzle pressure ratio $P_0/P_{amb}$ exceeds ≈ 1.9 the nozzle is choked.

</Machine>

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="Compressible flow in one box">

1. **Stagnation enthalpy** $h_0=h+\dfrac{V^2}{2}$. For an ideal gas: $T_0=T+\dfrac{V^2}{2c_p}$ (with $c_p$ in kJ/kg·K divide $V^2/2$ by 1000), $\dfrac{P_0}{P}=\left(\dfrac{T_0}{T}\right)^{k/(k-1)}$ (isentropic), $\dfrac{\rho_0}{\rho}=\left(\dfrac{T_0}{T}\right)^{1/(k-1)}$.
2. **Speed of sound** $c=\sqrt{kRT}$ (R in J/kg·K), **Mach number** $Ma=V/c$. $\dfrac{T_0}{T}=1+\dfrac{k-1}{2}Ma^2$.
3. **Critical (sonic) ratios**: $\dfrac{T^*}{T_0}=\dfrac{2}{k+1}$, $\dfrac{P^*}{P_0}=\left(\dfrac{2}{k+1}\right)^{k/(k-1)}$, $\dfrac{\rho^*}{\rho_0}=\left(\dfrac{2}{k+1}\right)^{1/(k-1)}$. For $k=1.4$: 0.8333, **0.5283**, 0.6339.
4. **Area change**: $\dfrac{dA}{A}=\dfrac{dP}{\rho V^2}\left(1-Ma^2\right)$.
5. **Area ratio**: $\dfrac{A}{A^*}=\dfrac{1}{Ma}\left[\dfrac{2}{k+1}\left(1+\dfrac{k-1}{2}Ma^2\right)\right]^{\frac{k+1}{2(k-1)}}$, minimum 1 at $Ma=1$.
6. **Choked flow**: $\dot m_{max}=A^*P_0\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{RT_0}}\left(\dfrac{2}{k+1}\right)^{\frac{k+1}{2(k-1)}}$; converging nozzle chokes when $P_b/P_0\le P^*/P_0$.

</Idea>

## Static versus stagnation

The **static** temperature and pressure are what a thermometer or gauge moving *with the flow* would read. The **stagnation** (total) values are what you get by bringing the fluid to rest **isentropically**: $h_0=h+V^2/2$ is the energy the flow *would have if its kinetic energy were converted into enthalpy*. They are not two different states of the fluid; they are two ways of reading one flowing state. In an adiabatic flow with no work, **$h_0$ (and so $T_0$ for an ideal gas) is constant**; in an *isentropic* flow, $P_0$ is also constant. Real friction and shocks leave $T_0$ unchanged and **lower $P_0$**, which is why $P_0$ is the measure of loss in this chapter.

</Statement>

</Think>

<Plan>

$T_0=T+V^2/(2000c_p)$ → $P_0=P(T_0/T)^{k/(k-1)}$ → $c=\sqrt{kRT}$ → $Ma=V/c$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Stagnation temperature">

$T_0=255.7+\dfrac{250^2}{2(1.005)(1000)}=255.7+31.09=\mathbf{286.8}$ K.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Stagnation pressure">

$P_0=54.05\left(\dfrac{286.8}{255.7}\right)^{3.5}=54.05(1.1216)^{3.5}=\mathbf{80.77}$ kPa. (The stagnation pressure is 49 % higher than the static: that is the *ram* effect on the compressor inlet.)

</Step>
<Step title="3. Mach number">

$c=\sqrt{1.4(287)(255.7)}=320.5$ m/s; $Ma=\dfrac{250}{320.5}=\mathbf{0.78}$ (the textbook comparison: $T_0/T=1+0.2(0.78)^2=1.122$ ✓).

</Step>

</Answer>

The airliner example above is the same calculation at 11 km.

## Mach number and the shape of the flow

The speed of sound in an ideal gas is $c=\sqrt{kRT}$, which depends only on the **static** temperature (with $R$ in J/kg·K), so the *same* flight speed is a different Mach number at different altitudes. Using $T_0/T=1+\tfrac{k-1}{2}Ma^2$ you can go from Mach number to every static-to-stagnation ratio: for $k=1.4$, $T/T_0$ drops to 0.833 and $P/P_0$ to 0.528 at $Ma=1$, and the density ratio is no longer ≈ 1 above $Ma\approx0.3$ (the incompressible limit: at $Ma=0.3$ the density change is already 4.5 %).

## How the area controls the flow

Combine continuity ($d\rho/\rho+dV/V+dA/A=0$), momentum ($dP=-\rho V\,dV$) and the isentropic relation ($dP=c^2d\rho$, so $d\rho/\rho=Ma^2\,dP/\rho V^2$):

$$\frac{dA}{A}=\frac{dP}{\rho V^2}\left(1-Ma^2\right).$$

The factor $(1-Ma^2)$ flips sign at Ma = 1 and with it every intuition:

| | **subsonic** ($Ma<1$) | **supersonic** ($Ma>1$) |
|---|---|---|
| Nozzle ($dP<0$, speed up) | **converging** ($dA<0$) | **diverging** ($dA>0$) |
| Diffuser ($dP>0$, slow down) | **diverging** | **converging** |

Why: in subsonic flow density changes little, so the speed is set by the area (continuity: smaller area, faster). In supersonic flow the density drops faster than the speed rises, so the area has to *grow* to pass the flow. At $Ma=1$ the area is a **minimum**: the **throat**. That is why a supersonic nozzle must be **converging–diverging**, with $Ma=1$ at the throat: the area ratio $A/A^*$ is the one function that tells you the Mach number once you know which branch (subsonic or supersonic) you are on.

Air at 0.5 MPa and 420 K flows at 110 m/s through a duct and then isentropically to a section where the flow is sonic. Find the stagnation temperature and pressure, the critical temperature and pressure, and the ratio of the sonic area to the initial area.

<Think>

Two ways to the answer for the area, which is a good habit on an exam: (1) the $A/A^*$ formula at the initial Mach number; (2) mass conservation between the two sections using the critical properties. The two should agree.

<Plan>

$T_0$ → $Ma_1$ → $P_0$ → $T^*=0.8333T_0$, $P^*=0.5283P_0$ → $A^*/A_1$ both ways.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Stagnation temperature and initial Mach number">

</Step>
<Step title="2. Stagnation pressure">

$P_0=500\left(\dfrac{426.0}{420}\right)^{3.5}=\mathbf{525.5}$ kPa.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Critical properties">

<T>A-32 (k = 1.4): T*/T₀ = 0.8333, P*/P₀ = 0.5283</T>. $T^*=0.8333(426.0)=\mathbf{355.0}$ K; $P^*=0.5283(525.5)=\mathbf{277.6}$ kPa.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Area ratio, method 1">

$\dfrac{A}{A^*}=\dfrac{1}{0.2678}\left[\dfrac{1}{1.2}\left(1+0.2(0.2678)^2\right)\right]^{3}=2.2553$, so $\dfrac{A^*}{A_1}=\mathbf{0.4434}$.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Area ratio, method 2 (continuity)">

</Step>
</Steps>

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="How much does the duct have to shrink?">

To take a 110 m/s stream to Mach 1 the duct must narrow to 44 % of its starting area, and the pressure drops to 55 % of the initial 0.5 MPa. Those are the proportions of a converging nozzle on a cold-gas thruster or a steam jet.

</RealWorld>

## Choking and back pressure

Take a nozzle fed from a large reservoir ($P_0,T_0$ fixed) and lower the **back pressure** $P_b$. At first the whole flow responds: the flow speeds up and the flow rate rises. The throat is the fastest point; when it reaches **Ma = 1** the flow is *choked* and $\dot m$ reaches its maximum $A^*P_0\sqrt{k/RT_0}(2/(k+1))^{(k+1)/(2(k-1))}$. Lowering $P_b$ further changes nothing upstream of the throat, because **pressure signals travel at the speed of sound and cannot propagate upstream against a sonic flow**: the flow rate, the throat state and the pressure field up to the throat are frozen. What changes is downstream:

- **Converging nozzle**: choked when $P_b/P_0\le P^*/P_0=0.528$ (for $k=1.4$); the exit pressure stays at $P^*$ and the rest of the expansion happens *outside* (an underexpanded jet). Reducing the exit area reduces $\dot m$ but the exit stays sonic: the speed does not change (his note on 17-33C, "can't go supersonic").
- **Converging–diverging nozzle**: the throat chokes at the first critical $P_b/P_0$ (the *subsonic-venturi* solution). Between the first and second critical, a **normal shock** stands in the diverging section and moves toward the exit as $P_b$ falls; at the second critical it sits at the exit; between the second and the third (the **design** value) the nozzle is *overexpanded* (oblique shocks outside); below the design value it is *underexpanded*.

The whole discussion is "why is it that in 17-48C the exit velocity, exit pressure and mass flow don't change when you lower the back pressure far below critical?" (answer: choked).

## Habashi's traps and your own mistakes

<Trap>

- **Static versus stagnation** (his closing slide): a probe facing the flow reads $T_0$, $P_0$; the *state* of the gas is $T$, $P$. Use static values in $c=\sqrt{kRT}$ and for $\rho$, stagnation in $h_0$ and $P_0$.
- **Cp unit**: $T_0=T+V^2/(2000c_p)$ (his note). In m/s and J/kg you need $c_p$ in J/kg·K.
- **Constant versus variable $c_p$** (17-18): with constant room-temperature values (k = 1.289, $c_p=0.846$) CO₂ expanding from 800 K to 400 K gives $Ma_1=0.113$, $V_2=824$ m/s, $Ma_2=2.64$. If you use $c_p(T)$ at each end ($k=1.193$ at 800 K and 1.252 at 400 K) $Ma_2$ becomes 2.82: "significant difference in Mach number" (your own comment).
- **$Ma=V/c$ with $c$ at the *static* temperature** at the same point.
- **$P^*/P_0=0.5283$ only for $k=1.4$** (air); for $k=1.33$ it is 0.540 and for $k=1.2$ it is 0.564.
- **17-39**: the lowest throat pressure of a C-D nozzle with $P_0=1200$ kPa is $P^*=0.5283(1200)=634$ kPa.
- **Choked flow does not depend on $P_b$** (17-48C), only on $P_0$, $T_0$, $A^*$.

</Trap>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ 17-18: Ma at the inlet">

Your page has $Ma_1=0.0923$ with $c_1=541$ m/s. That speed of sound corresponds to a static temperature of about 1200 K, not the 800 K inlet; with 800 K, $c_1=441$ m/s and $Ma_1=\mathbf{0.113}$ (which also matches the textbook answer). $V_2=824$ and $Ma_2=2.64$ on your page are right.

</Callout>

## Check yourself

1. A converging nozzle is fed at $P_0=500$ kPa and exhausts into 100 kPa. Is it choked? What is the exit pressure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P_b/P_0=0.2<0.528$: **choked**, exit Ma = 1, exit pressure $P^*=0.528(500)=264$ kPa, *above* the ambient 100 kPa (the rest of the expansion happens outside the nozzle).

</details>

2. Why must a supersonic nozzle diverge, while a supersonic diffuser must converge?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$dA/A=(dP/\rho V^2)(1-Ma^2)$: for $Ma>1$ the bracket is negative, so a *pressure drop* ($dP<0$, acceleration) needs $dA>0$ (diverging); a pressure rise (deceleration) needs $dA<0$ (converging).

</details>

3. An aircraft flies at 250 m/s in air at 230 K. Find $T_0$ and $Ma$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$T_0=230+\dfrac{250^2}{2010}=261.1$ K; $c=\sqrt{1.4(287)(230)}=304.0$ m/s, $Ma=0.822$.

</details>

4. What does "choked" tell you about the effect of lowering the back pressure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing changes upstream of the throat: the mass flow rate, the throat state ($Ma=1$) and the pressure along the converging part are all frozen, because signals cannot travel upstream through a sonic throat. Only the flow downstream of the throat (shocks, exit pressure) changes.

</details>

5. Why do $T_0$ and $P_0$ behave differently across a normal shock?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The shock is adiabatic with no work, so $h_0$ (and $T_0$ for an ideal gas) is **unchanged**; but it is irreversible, so entropy rises and the stagnation **pressure drops**. $P_0$ is the measure of the loss, and it is why the choked mass flow, which depends on $P_0$, shrinks after a shock.

</details>
