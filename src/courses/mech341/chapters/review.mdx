# Thermo 1 refresher, exergy and the second law

<div className="lede">

Thermo 2 is mostly *applications* of a handful of Thermo 1 ideas: the first law for a control volume, the state postulate and the property tables, the second law and entropy, and the difference between an **ideal** device and a **real** one (isentropic efficiency). If these are solid, chapters 9 to 11 (jet engines, steam plants, refrigerators) are bookkeeping on a T–s diagram. This chapter is the minimum you "absolutely should know" before starting, plus the one extension that makes the second law quantitative: **exergy**, the useful work that a stream of energy is really worth.

</div>

<Machine name="a car engine, and where its fuel goes">

A petrol car engine is the cleanest place to *feel* the second law. Typical values (all ≈, they vary with load and engine):

- brake thermal efficiency **≈ 25–35 %** at best (≈ 20 % in ordinary driving; modern hybrids' Atkinson engines reach ≈ 40 %);
- **≈ 30–35 %** of the fuel energy leaves in the exhaust (hot gas at ≈ 700–900 °C), **≈ 25–30 %** through the coolant and radiator;
- peak in-cylinder gas temperature **≈ 2500–3000 K** for a few milliseconds, yet the cycle-average temperature the metal sees is far lower (the slide asks "jet engine 2000 K vs ICE 3000 K: why?", see the traps below);
- a coal power plant: **≈ 33–40 %** thermal efficiency; a modern gas-and-steam combined cycle: **≈ 60 %**; the Carnot limit for a 2000 K source and a 300 K sink is **85 %**.

Every number on this site's later chapters is some version of this audit: how much of the energy that enters leaves as work, and where the rest goes.

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="What you must be able to do">

1. **Closed system**: $\Delta E=Q_{in}-W_{by}$ (in a closed system with no motion, $\Delta E=\Delta U=m\,\Delta u$).
2. **Steady-flow device** (turbine, compressor, pump, nozzle, boiler, condenser, mixing chamber, heat exchanger): $\dot Q-\dot W=\sum\dot m_{out}\left(h+\tfrac{V^2}{2}+gz\right)-\sum\dot m_{in}\left(h+\tfrac{V^2}{2}+gz\right)$. Mass: $\sum\dot m_{in}=\sum\dot m_{out}$. Kinetic and potential terms are dropped unless the problem is a nozzle or a jet.
3. **State postulate**: any state of a simple compressible substance is fixed by two independent intensive properties. You *measure* $P,v,T$; you *look up or compute* $u,h,s$.
4. **Pick the table by the region**: compressed liquid, saturated mixture (inside the dome), superheated vapour.
5. **Second law**: $s_{gen}\ge0$ ($=0$ reversible). For an adiabatic steady device, $s_{out}\ge s_{in}$; "isentropic" is the **ideal** limit.
6. **Real devices**: $\eta_T=\dfrac{w_a}{w_s}=\dfrac{h_1-h_{2a}}{h_1-h_{2s}}$ for a turbine and $\eta_C=\dfrac{w_s}{w_a}=\dfrac{h_{2s}-h_1}{h_{2a}-h_1}$ for a compressor or pump.
7. **Exergy** (this chapter's extension): the reversible work between two states and an environment at $T_0$; **destroyed** exergy is $T_0\,s_{gen}$.

</Idea>

## Energy, heat, work and efficiency

Energy crosses a boundary only as **heat** $Q$, **work** $W$, or with **mass flow** (carrying $h$). Properties ($u,h,s,P,v,T$) are *point functions*: they depend only on the state, not on the path. Heat and work are *path functions*: their value depends on how you went from 1 to 2. This is why you can compute $\Delta h$ or $\Delta s$ with *any* convenient path, but $Q$ and $W$ only with the actual process.

Habashi insists on separating three words that students blur:

- **Thermal efficiency** is the efficiency of an entire **cycle**, $\eta_{th}=\dfrac{w_{net}}{q_{in}}=1-\dfrac{q_{out}}{q_{in}}$, even if the cycle is ideal (all components isentropic).
- **Isentropic efficiency** is the efficiency of a **single component** (one process 1 to 2) because of friction or heat loss.
- **No isentropic process exists in real life.** We define isentropic efficiency just to *measure how far from ideal* a real turbine or compressor is.

Cycle efficiency is bounded by Carnot: $\eta_{th}<1-\dfrac{T_L}{T_H}$ (always; a cycle with 100 % thermal efficiency would violate Kelvin–Planck).

## Properties: tables, the state postulate and the ideal gas

### Which region am I in?

For water (tables A-4 to A-7) or R-134a (A-11 to A-13):

| Given | Look at | Region |
|---|---|---|
| $T,P$ | compare $T$ with $T_{sat}(P)$ | $T<T_{sat}$: compressed liquid; $T>T_{sat}$: superheated vapour |
| $P,v$ | compare $v$ with $v_f,v_g$ at $P$ | $v<v_f$ liquid; $v_f<v<v_g$ mixture; $v>v_g$ vapour |
| $P,s$ or $P,h$ | compare with $s_f,s_g$ (or $h_f,h_g$) | same idea |
| $T,x$ or $P,x$ | quality | inside the dome |

Inside the dome, any extensive-per-mass property is a **linear mix** of the saturated values: $v=v_f+x\,v_{fg}$, $u=u_f+x\,u_{fg}$, $h=h_f+x\,h_{fg}$, $s=s_f+x\,s_{fg}$ with $x=\dfrac{m_g}{m_{total}}$. The temperature and the pressure are tied together ($T=T_{sat}(P)$) and are **not** independent there; that is why you need $x$ as the second property.

**Compressed liquid** has no table in most problems; use $v\approx v_f(T)$, $h\approx h_f(T)+v_f(P-P_{sat})$, $s\approx s_f(T)$. For a **pump** this is exactly $w=v\,\Delta P$.

### Ideal gas

$Pv=RT$, with $R=R_u/M$ and $R_u=8.314$ kJ/kmol·K (air: $R=0.287$ kJ/kg·K). $\Delta u=\int c_v\,dT$, $\Delta h=\int c_p\,dT$, $c_p-c_v=R$, $k=c_p/c_v$ ($k_{air}=1.4$).

- **Perfect gas** (constant $c_p$): $\Delta h=c_p\Delta T$. Use when the problem says "cold-air standard" or does not give the air tables.
- **Semi-perfect gas** ($c_p=c_p(T)$): use the air table (A-17) with $h(T)$, $P_r(T)$, $v_r(T)$.

<Trap title="Never interpolate a specific volume linearly across a wide pressure gap">

For a vapour, $v\propto1/P$ is far from linear. Interpolate $Pv$ (nearly constant) or $1/v$, or use a closer table row. This is the trap in Anthony's own Assignment 1, problem 4-9 below: a linear interpolation between 0.1 and 0.2 MPa gave $v=1.6264$ m³/kg (and $W=350.28$ kJ) where the true value is 1.4445 m³/kg (213.8 kJ), an error of 12 % in $v$.

</Trap>

## Entropy and the second law

**Statements.** Kelvin–Planck: no cyclic device can take heat from a single reservoir and deliver an equal amount of work. Clausius: heat cannot pass from cold to hot with no other effect. The two are equivalent. **Carnot principles**: (1) an irreversible engine is less efficient than a reversible one between the same two reservoirs; (2) all reversible engines between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, $1-T_L/T_H$.

**Entropy.** $ds\ge\dfrac{\delta q}{T}$, equality for a reversible process. For an adiabatic device the entropy can only stay the same or increase: **$s_{gen}=s_2-s_1\ge0$** (per unit mass, steady flow). Entropy is "a measure of the loss of energy available to do work": every irreversibility (friction, mixing, heat transfer across a finite $\Delta T$) increases it.

**Ideal-gas $\Delta s$**:

$$s_2-s_1=c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}-R\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}=c_v\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}+R\ln\frac{v_2}{v_1}\qquad(\text{perfect gas}).$$

For a semi-perfect gas, $s_2-s_1=s^\circ(T_2)-s^\circ(T_1)-R\ln\dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$ with $s^\circ$ from the table. **Isentropic** ($\Delta s=0$) for a perfect gas: $\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}=\left(\dfrac{P_2}{P_1}\right)^{(k-1)/k}=\left(\dfrac{v_1}{v_2}\right)^{k-1}$; with the table: $\dfrac{P_{r2}}{P_{r1}}=\dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$. **Liquids and vapours** have no such formula: use the tables with $s_2=s_1$.

**The T–s diagram** is the language of Thermo 2: for an internally reversible process, $q=\int T\,ds$, the **area under the path** is the heat; for a cycle, the **enclosed area is the net work** ($w_{net}=q_{in}-q_{out}$). The most common confusion (Habashi says "many students have trouble to the end of the semester" with this): the area under a T–s path is **heat**, not work. Work is the *enclosed* area of the cycle, or $\Delta h$ across a turbine.

## Isentropic efficiency of a turbine, compressor and pump

<Remember title="The three formulas (the same idea each time: actual versus ideal for the SAME inlet and the SAME exit pressure)">

$$\eta_T=\frac{w_a}{w_s}=\frac{h_1-h_{2a}}{h_1-h_{2s}},\qquad \eta_C=\frac{w_s}{w_a}=\frac{h_{2s}-h_1}{h_{2a}-h_1},\qquad \eta_P=\frac{w_s}{w_a}=\frac{v\,(P_2-P_1)}{h_{2a}-h_1}.$$

A device that *produces* work: actual over ideal (the real output is smaller). A device that *consumes* work: ideal over actual (the real input is larger). To get $h_{2a}$ from $\eta_T$: $h_{2a}=h_1-\eta_T(h_1-h_{2s})$, then look up the state at $(P_2,h_{2a})$.

</Remember>

**A subtle difference that Habashi stresses** (slide on compressors): in a *pump* we put in a certain amount of work and, with losses, get to a **lower pressure**; in a *compressor* we must reach a **specified pressure**, so a real device does **more work** to get there. That is why $\eta_C$ uses the work needed for a given pressure ratio, and why the exit temperature of a real compressor is higher than the ideal one: $T_{2a}=T_1+\dfrac{T_{2s}-T_1}{\eta_C}$ (perfect gas).

## Exergy: what work is really worth

The first law counts energy; it does not distinguish a kilojoule of electricity from a kilojoule of lukewarm water. The second law does, through **exergy** (also called availability): the maximum useful work obtainable as a system comes to equilibrium with the **environment** at $T_0$ (usually 25 °C = 298.15 K, 101.3 kPa).

For two states with the surroundings at $T_0$, the **reversible work** of a steady-flow device exchanging heat only with the environment is

$$w_{rev}=(h_1-h_2)-T_0\,(s_1-s_2)\qquad(\text{ignoring } V^2\text{ and } z).$$

The **actual** work of an adiabatic device is $w_a=h_1-h_2$, so

$$\boxed{\,w_{rev}-w_a=T_0\,(s_2-s_1)=T_0\,s_{gen}=x_{dest}\,}$$

**Exergy destroyed equals $T_0$ times entropy generated.** The **second-law efficiency** of a work-producing device is $\eta_{II}=\dfrac{w_a}{w_{rev}}$. Two further facts:

- **Exergy of heat** $Q$ available at temperature $T$: $X_Q=Q\left(1-\dfrac{T_0}{T}\right)$, the Carnot factor. Heat at 2000 K is worth 85 %; heat at 350 K only 15 %.
- A **flow stream** has specific flow exergy $\psi=(h-h_0)-T_0(s-s_0)+\tfrac{V^2}{2}+gz$. For a mixing chamber or feedwater heater the destruction is $x_{dest}=T_0\left[\sum\dot m_{out}s_{out}-\sum\dot m_{in}s_{in}\right]/\dot m$. This is what the Tutorial uses on the open feedwater heater (chapter 10): $i=T_0\left[s_3-y\,s_6-(1-y)\,s_2\right]$.

## Habashi's traps

<Trap title="Concepts students keep getting wrong (from the instructor's review)">

1. **Thermal efficiency is a cycle quantity; isentropic efficiency is a component quantity.** A cycle can be ideal (all components isentropic) and still have a thermal efficiency far below 1 (the Carnot bound).
2. **The area under the T–s curve is heat, not work.** Net work is the enclosed area.
3. **Pump versus compressor**: a pump gets less pressure for its work; a compressor must reach the pressure and so does more work. Both are handled by $\eta=w_s/w_a$.
4. **"Why 2000 K in a jet engine but 3000 K in a piston engine?"** The piston engine reaches 3000 K only for an instant in each cycle and the walls are cooled; the turbine blades see the hot gas **continuously**, so the allowable steady temperature (the material limit, helped by blade cooling) is lower than the engine's peak flame temperature.
5. The perfect-gas assumption ($c_p$ constant) is a *subset* of the ideal gas, the same way "incompressible" is a subset of compressible. If a problem gives you the air table, do not use $T_2/T_1=(P_2/P_1)^{(k-1)/k}$; use $P_{r}$.

</Trap>

## Check yourself

1. A steam turbine has $\eta_T=0.80$ and an ideal specific work of 1000 kJ/kg. What are the real work and the exit enthalpy relative to the ideal exit?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$w_a=0.80(1000)=800$ kJ/kg, so the real exit enthalpy is 200 kJ/kg **higher** than $h_{2s}$ (the real exit is hotter and drier or more superheated). Never apply $\eta$ to the exit temperature of a vapour.

</details>

2. A compressor must raise air from 100 to 800 kPa. Is the exit temperature of the real (adiabatic) compressor higher or lower than the isentropic exit temperature? Why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Higher. The real machine needs more work for the same pressure ($w_a=w_s/\eta_C$), all of which is converted to enthalpy of the gas ($q=0$): $T_{2a}=T_1+(T_{2s}-T_1)/\eta_C$.

</details>

3. Heat of 1000 kJ is available at 600 K. The environment is at 300 K. What is the largest amount of work you could get from it, and what if the heat were at 400 K?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X=1000(1-300/600)=500$ kJ at 600 K; $1000(1-300/400)=250$ kJ at 400 K. Heat at lower temperature is worth less work.

</details>

4. In the turbine problem, why is $s_{gen}$ positive but $s_{2a}-s_{2s}$ also positive? Are they the same number?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Yes: both exits are at the same pressure, $s_{2s}=s_1$, so $s_{2a}-s_{2s}=s_{2a}-s_1=s_{gen}=0.503$ kJ/kg·K. The isentropic exit is the reference with no generation.

</details>
