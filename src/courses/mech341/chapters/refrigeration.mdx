# Refrigeration and heat pumps

<div className="lede">

A refrigerator is a heat engine run backwards: it *spends* work to move heat from a cold space to a warm one. A heat pump is the same machine with the objective reversed (deliver the heat to the warm space). The performance measure is the **coefficient of performance**, COP, which is almost always greater than 1; a COP of 3 means 1 kW of electricity moves 3 kW of heat from the cold side. The hardware is four components (compressor, condenser, expansion valve, evaporator), and the exam is a T–s or P–h diagram plus a table lookup in R-134a. The only thing that changes between problems is the *variation*: superheat and subcooling, compressor efficiency, two stages with a flash chamber or a cascade, several evaporators, or air as the refrigerant.

</div>

<Machine name="a house heat pump, a fridge, a supermarket and an airliner">

All typical or approximate (≈):

- **Home heat pump**: seasonal **COP ≈ 2.5–4** (about 3 on a mild winter day, about 2 at −15 °C outdoors), because the COP falls as the temperature difference grows. 1 kW of electricity delivers ≈ 3 kW of heat: three times better than a resistance heater.
- **Household fridge / freezer**: evaporator at **≈ −20 to −25 °C** (freezer) or ≈ −5 °C (fresh-food), condenser at ≈ 35–45 °C; **COP ≈ 2–3**, using R-134a, R-600a (isobutane) or R-290 depending on the market. A fridge's compressor draws about 100 W to remove about 250 W of heat. The numbers of the class example below (−25 °C evaporator) are a freezer.
- **Supermarket cascade**: a low-temperature loop (CO₂ or R-134a) at ≈ −35 °C cools the freezer cases, and rejects its heat into the evaporator of a second loop (ammonia or another refrigerant) that rejects outdoors. Two loops, one heat exchanger: it is the cascade of this chapter.
- **Aircraft air-cycle pack**: engine bleed air (≈ 200–250 °C) is cooled, compressed, cooled again and expanded through a turbine to well below zero: a *gas* refrigeration (reversed Brayton) cycle. Its COP is well below 1 but the "refrigerant" is the cabin air itself, weightless and leak-proof; the course's gas-refrigeration problem (11-79) has COP 0.48.
- **Units**: 1 *ton of refrigeration* = 211 kJ/min = 3.517 kW (the cooling of melting a US ton of ice per day).

</Machine>

## The 30-second version

<Idea title="What to remember">

1. **COP**: refrigerator $COP_R=\dfrac{q_L}{w_{in}}=\dfrac{h_1-h_4}{h_2-h_1}$; heat pump $COP_{HP}=\dfrac{q_H}{w_{in}}=\dfrac{h_2-h_3}{h_2-h_1}=COP_R+1$.
2. **Carnot (reversible) limits**: $COP_{R,rev}=\dfrac{1}{T_H/T_L-1}$, $COP_{HP,rev}=\dfrac{1}{1-T_L/T_H}$ (kelvin!). The bigger the "lift" $T_H-T_L$, the worse.
3. **Ideal vapor-compression cycle**: 1 = **saturated vapour** at $P_L$ ($h_1=h_g$), 1→2 isentropic compression to $P_H$, 2→3 condensation to **saturated liquid** ($h_3=h_f$), 3→4 throttle ($h_4=h_3$, **constant enthalpy**), 4→1 evaporation.
4. **Actual cycle**: compressor $\eta_C=\dfrac{h_{2s}-h_1}{h_{2a}-h_1}$, superheat at the evaporator exit, subcooling at the condenser exit, pressure drops. COP decreases.
5. **Mass flow**: $\dot m=\dfrac{\dot Q_L}{h_1-h_4}$; power $\dot W=\dot m(h_2-h_1)$.
6. **Two-stage, cascade, multi-evaporator**: each mixing point or heat exchanger is one energy balance $\sum\dot m_{in}h=\sum\dot m_{out}h$ (isolated: no heat, no work).
7. **Gas refrigeration**: $COP=\dfrac{q_L}{w_C-w_T}$, with a regenerator effectiveness $\varepsilon=\dfrac{T_3-T_4}{T_3-T_6}$.

</Idea>

## Carnot refrigerators and heat pumps

Energy conservation on a cycle that takes $Q_L$ from the cold reservoir and rejects $Q_H$ to the hot one: $Q_H=Q_L+W_{in}$, so $W_{in}=Q_H-Q_L$. For a reversible cycle $Q_H/Q_L=T_H/T_L$, which gives

$$COP_{R}=\frac{Q_L}{W_{in}}=\frac{Q_L}{Q_H-Q_L}=\frac{1}{Q_H/Q_L-1}\ \to\ \frac{1}{T_H/T_L-1},\qquad COP_{HP}=\frac{Q_H}{W_{in}}=\frac{1}{1-Q_L/Q_H}\ \to\ \frac{1}{1-T_L/T_H}.$$

Two consequences worth feeling. If $T_H\to T_L$ (a fridge in a room the same temperature as its contents) you need no work, $COP\to\infty$. If $T_L\to0$ K the COP goes to zero: infinite work to reach absolute zero. A **heat pump always has $COP_{HP}>1$** (the heat delivered includes the work); a refrigerator can have $COP_R<1$ (gas refrigeration).

The **reversed Carnot cycle** (isothermal heat transfer, isentropic compression and expansion) is the ideal limit but is impractical: (1) compressing a *wet* mixture (state 1 inside the dome) damages the compressor, (2) the isentropic "turbine" that would recover work in the expansion process produces little work in a liquid or low-quality mixture and is expensive. So the real cycle *completely vaporises* the refrigerant before the compressor (state 1 on the saturated-vapour line: compress a gas) and **replaces the turbine by a throttle valve** (an **isenthalpic** process, $h_4=h_3$, instead of isentropic).

## The ideal vapor-compression cycle

<VcrPhFigure />

States (P–h diagram, log pressure): **1** saturated vapour at $P_L$; **2** superheated vapour at $P_H$ with $s_2=s_1$; **3** saturated liquid at $P_H$; **4** throttled mixture at $P_L$ with $h_4=h_3$. The two shortcuts that give the table lookups (the notebook's boxed lines):

$$h_1=h_g@P_1,\qquad h_3=h_f@P_3=h_4.$$

The throttle is the only irreversible step in the ideal cycle: entropy is generated there ($s_4>s_3$) and no work is recovered. On the P–h diagram the throttle is a **vertical line** and the evaporator and condenser are horizontal lines; on the T–s diagram the isenthalpic step has no nice shape: that is why the P–h diagram is the working diagram of refrigeration.

$$q_L=h_1-h_4,\quad q_H=h_2-h_3,\quad w_{in}=h_2-h_1,\quad COP_R=\frac{h_1-h_4}{h_2-h_1},\quad \dot m=\frac{\dot Q_L}{q_L},\quad \dot W=\dot m\,w_{in}.$$

For a refrigeration load given in **tons**: $\dot Q_L=211\,N$ kJ/min, so $\dot m=\dfrac{3.5168\,N}{h_1-h_4}$ kg/s. To find the **compressor displacement** (the theoretical piston displacement per unit time, $\dot V=\dot m\,v_1$) and relate it to bore, stroke, cylinders and rpm: $\dot V=\dfrac{\pi D^2}{4}L\,N_{cyl}\,\text{rpm}$.

The sim shows how steeply the COP drops with the lift. It also shows why the ideal VCR (solid) sits below the Carnot curve (dashed): the throttle wastes the expansion work and the compressor discharges superheated vapour, so heat is rejected over a range of temperatures, not at $T_H$.

## The actual vapor-compression cycle

The real cycle differs from the ideal one by (his list): **friction** (pressure drops in the pipes, condenser and evaporator), **heat losses**, **non-isentropic compression**, **superheated vapour** at the evaporator exit (to protect the compressor from liquid droplets), and **subcooled liquid** at the condenser exit (to make sure only liquid reaches the valve, and to raise $q_L$). The COP decreases.

Habashi's remark on the compressor exit state $2'$: **friction increases entropy while heat loss decreases it**, so the real end point lies to the right of the isentropic point *or* may sit to its left if heat loss dominates. In an exam, you are given either $\eta_C$ (and compute $h_{2a}=h_1+\dfrac{h_{2s}-h_1}{\eta_C}$) or the actual exit temperature (look up $h_2$ at $P_H$, $T_2$). The superheat and subcooling are given as temperatures: look up $h_1$ at $(P_L,T_1)$ in the superheated table and $h_3\approx h_f@T_3$ (compressed-liquid approximation).

For the class-example pressures below (evaporator −25 °C, condenser 800 kPa): ideal COP 3.34; with $\eta_C=0.80$, 5 K superheat and 5 K subcooling the COP is **2.81** (−16 %) and the cooling capacity per kg/s of refrigerant rises to 151 from 140 kJ/kg (subcooling and superheat add refrigeration effect, but the compressor work rises faster).

## Heat pumps

The same cycle with the *useful* effect on the other side: $COP_{HP}=\dfrac{q_H}{w}=\dfrac{h_2-h_3}{h_2-h_1}=COP_R+1$. A heat pump warms a house in winter and cools it in summer (a reversing valve swaps the roles of the two coils); an air-source unit's COP falls on the coldest days because the lift grows and the evaporator coil frosts. The comparison to resistance heating is the point of the exercise: any COP above 1 beats a resistance heater, which converts 1 kW of electricity to 1 kW of heat.

## Cascade and multistage systems

When the temperature range is large (a −40 °C freezer rejecting at 40 °C), a single stage has a poor COP (high lift) and demands a very high compressor ratio. Two answers:

**Cascade (2 compressors, 2 loops, same or different refrigerants).** The low-temperature loop B rejects its heat in a heat exchanger that is the *evaporator* of the high-temperature loop A. The heat exchanger is isolated, so $\dot m_A(h_5-h_8)=\dot m_B(h_2-h_3)$ and

$$\dot m_A=\dot m_B\,\frac{h_2-h_3}{h_5-h_8},\qquad COP_{cascade}=\frac{\dot Q_L}{\dot W_{net}}=\frac{\dot m_B(h_1-h_4)}{\dot m_B(h_2-h_1)+\dot m_A(h_6-h_5)}.$$

A convenient **intermediate pressure** is the geometric mean $P_i=\sqrt{P_LP_H}$ (equal pressure ratio on the two compressors).

**Two-stage compression with a flash chamber or separator (one loop).** The liquid leaving the condenser (state 5) is throttled (state 6) to an intermediate pressure $P_m$ into a **flash chamber**. The vapour fraction $x_6=\dfrac{h_5-h_7}{h_3-h_7}$ (with 3 the saturated vapour and 7 the saturated liquid in the chamber, both at $P_m$) goes to the high-pressure compressor; the saturated liquid is throttled to $P_L$ (state 8) and does the evaporating. **Only the liquid fraction $1-x_6$ passes through the low-pressure compressor and the evaporator**, so $\dot m_H=\dfrac{\dot m_L}{1-x_6}$. In the energy balance of the mixing chamber before the high-pressure compressor you use the enthalpy of the **saturated vapour** from the chamber, not of the mixture state 6 right after the valve (his highlighted warning, "$h_3$ not $h_6$"): $x_6\,h_3+(1-x_6)\,h_2=h_9$, where $h_2$ is the low-pressure compressor exit and $h_9$ the mixed stream entering the second compressor. In the separator layout of Fig. P11-60 the low-pressure discharge is bubbled through the separator and the isolated balance is $\dot m_H h_5+\dot m_L h_2=\dot m_H h_3+\dot m_L h_7$.

**Several evaporators, one compressor.** Route all evaporator outlets to a single compressor, mixing the streams first with an energy balance (the mixing chamber is adiabatic and does no work), then compress once.

## Gas refrigeration cycles

When the **refrigerant is a gas that never condenses** (air), the cycle is a reversed Brayton cycle: 1→2 compressor, 2→3 heat rejection at constant pressure (cooling the compressed air to near ambient), 3→4 a regenerator (hot side), 4→5 a **turbine** (expansion, recovering work and dropping the temperature far below the sink), 5→6 the refrigerated space, 6→1 the regenerator (cold side). With air and $c_p$ constant,

$$COP=\frac{q_L}{w_C-w_T}=\frac{T_6-T_5}{(T_2-T_1)-(T_4-T_5)},\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{T_3-T_4}{T_3-T_6},$$

(heat balance on the regenerator: $T_3-T_4=T_1-T_6$ for equal $c_p$ and flow). The gas cycle has a low COP (the heat is removed over a temperature *range*, not at constant temperature), but it is the right choice for aircraft cabins and cryogenic liquefiers because the working fluid is harmless, light, and works at very low temperature.

## Habashi's traps

<Trap title="What the instructor and the notebook warn about in chapter 11">

1. **COP depends on the temperature lift**; the Carnot COP uses kelvin. Always check your answer against $COP_{rev}$: if it is larger, you made an error.
2. **State 1 is saturated vapour; state 3 is saturated liquid** (ideal); the throttle is **isenthalpic**, not isentropic. State 4 is a mixture with a quality you may need only to answer a question.
3. **Do not recover work from the throttle** and do not use an isentropic efficiency for it.
4. **Superheat and subcooling add to the capacity** but raise the compressor work; do not "correct" the ideal answer by the same factor.
5. **Flash chamber**: the vapour fraction $x_6$ comes from the *throttle* state; the mixing-chamber balance uses the **vapour** enthalpy (his reminder: "$h_3$ not $h_6$").
6. **Cascade versus flash**: the cascade's two loops have *different* mass flows tied by the heat-exchanger balance; the single-loop two-stage plant has $\dot m_H=\dot m_L/(1-x_6)$ in the flash layout.
7. **Refrigerator versus heat pump**: same cycle; only the objective changes. $COP_{HP}=COP_R+1$ holds for the same cycle.

</Trap>

## Check yourself

1. A refrigerator holds −5 °C in a 25 °C room. What is the best possible COP, and what does a real one of 3 tell you?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$COP_{rev}=\dfrac{1}{298.15/268.15-1}=8.94$. A real COP of 3 is about a third of Carnot, typical for a household unit (the throttle and the superheated compressor discharge account for most of the gap).

</details>

2. In the ideal cycle, why is $h_4=h_3$, and what does the quality at state 4 mean physically?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A throttling valve has no heat or work and negligible kinetic energy change, so $h$ is constant. The quality of state 4 is the vapour mass fraction produced by the sudden pressure drop ("flash"): that liquid already evaporated and cannot absorb heat in the evaporator, so a lower $x_4$ means a higher refrigeration effect $h_1-h_4$ (the single-stage example: $x_4=0.35$).

</details>

3. A heat pump has $COP_{HP}=4.2$. What is the $COP_R$ of the same machine, and how many kW of heat does it take from outdoors per kW of electricity?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$COP_R=3.2$. For 1 kW of electricity it delivers 4.2 kW of heat and takes 3.2 kW from outdoors.

</details>

4. Why does two-stage compression with a flash chamber improve the COP, and where do you put the intermediate pressure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(i) The two compressions each have a lower pressure ratio and the gas going to the second compressor is cooled (the cold flash vapour), so less work. (ii) Flashing at $P_m$ removes the vapour that would otherwise be produced by the throttle at $P_L$, so less refrigerant has to be pumped through the cold, low-pressure part. The optimum is near $P_m=\sqrt{P_LP_H}$ (the sim's curve is flat there).

</details>

5. In the gas-refrigeration cycle, why is a turbine used instead of a throttle valve, and what does the regenerator do?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A gas does not condense, and throttling an ideal gas does not change its temperature at all (chapter 12): to cool it you must extract work in a turbine. The regenerator uses the cold air leaving the refrigerated space to pre-cool the air entering the turbine, so the turbine can reach a much lower temperature for the same pressure ratio.

</details>
