<Callout kind="note" title="The six remarks about Kp that these problems exercise (from the review session)">

1. $K_p$ depends on **temperature only**. 2. $K_p$ of the reverse reaction is $1/K_p$. 3. The larger $K_p$, the more complete the reaction. 4. The mixture **pressure** changes the equilibrium *composition* but not $K_p$ (through $(P/N_{total})^{\Delta\nu}$). 5. **Inert** gases change the composition (through $N_{total}$) but not $K_p$. 6. Doubling the stoichiometric coefficients **squares** $K_p$. Habashi writes "some students do not easily digest these conclusions". Tutorial 16-8C is remarks 1 and 4, 16-52C is remark 3 with the van 't Hoff equation, 16-40 is the quantitative routine with $N_{total}$.

</Callout>

<Problem id="16-8C" title="NO, O2 and N2 in equilibrium: the pressure is tripled" tags="Kp, delta nu = 0, pressure independence">

<Statement>

A mixture of NO, O₂ and N₂ is in equilibrium at a given temperature and pressure; the pressure is now tripled. (a) Does the equilibrium constant $K_p$ change? (b) Do the numbers of moles of NO, O₂ and N₂ change, and how?

</Statement>

<Think>

Two separate questions, two separate rules. (a) $K_p$ is a function of $T$ only. (b) The pressure acts on the composition **only through the factor $(P/N_{total})^{\Delta\nu}$**, so the question is the value of $\Delta\nu$ for $\mathrm{N_2+O_2\rightleftharpoons2NO}$: $\Delta\nu=2-1-1=0$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. (a) Kp">

No: $K_p$ depends on temperature only ($\ln K_p=-\Delta G^\circ(T)/R_uT$), and the temperature has not changed.

</Step>
<Step title="2. (b) Composition">

$K_p=\dfrac{N_{NO}^2}{N_{N_2}N_{O_2}}\left(\dfrac{P}{N_{total}}\right)^{0}=\dfrac{N_{NO}^2}{N_{N_2}N_{O_2}}$: the pressure has dropped out, so tripling it changes **nothing**: the mole numbers of NO, O₂ and N₂ stay the same (the total number of moles does not change in the reaction, so Le Chatelier's principle has no side to prefer).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Shown with numbers">

Air (3.76 N₂ + 1 O₂) at 3000 K: $\ln K_p(\tfrac12\mathrm{N_2}+\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}\to\mathrm{NO})=-2.1035$, so for $\mathrm{N_2+O_2\to2NO}$: $\ln K_p=-4.207$, $K_p=0.0149$. Solving for the extent gives **0.2199 kmol NO** (N₂ 3.650, O₂ 0.890; $y_{NO}=0.2199/4.76=4.62$ %) at **1 atm and also at 3 atm**, to 9 digits. Contrast with a reaction that has $\Delta\nu\ne0$: CO₂ ⇌ CO + ½O₂ at 3000 K dissociates 43.9 % at 1 atm and 24.2 % at 10 atm.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) **No**, $K_p$ depends on $T$ only. (b) **No change** in the moles of NO, O₂ and N₂, because $\Delta\nu=2-1-1=0$: the pressure does not enter the equilibrium relation of this reaction.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="The printed solution matches, and your page 1 of assignment 9">

The instructor's page gives the same two answers with the same exponent "$2-1-1=0$". Your notebook (assignment 9) reaches the same conclusion; see <Asg n="09">16-8C</Asg>.

</Callout>

<RealWorld title="NO in an engine does not care about boost pressure (to first order)">

Because the NO-forming equilibrium has $\Delta\nu=0$, the equilibrium NO fraction of an engine's burned gas depends on the **temperature** (the Zeldovich route), not on the cylinder pressure; turbo-charging raises NO only through the higher flame temperature it causes (typical).

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- "More pressure shifts toward fewer moles" is the rule for $\Delta\nu\ne0$; when the mole count does not change, there is no shift.
- $K_p$ never changes with $P$; do not mix up $K_p$ with the composition.

</Traps>

<Problem id="16-52C" title="Will a fuel burn more completely at 2000 K or at 2500 K?" tags="exothermic, van't Hoff, dissociation, Kp">

<Statement>

Will a fuel burn more completely at 2000 K or at 2500 K?

</Statement>

<Think>

A one-line conceptual question with the **van 't Hoff** equation behind it: $\dfrac{d\ln K_p}{dT}=\dfrac{\bar h_R}{R_uT^2}$. Combustion is exothermic ($\bar h_R<0$), so $K_p$ of the *forming* reaction **falls** as $T$ rises: the reaction is less complete at the higher temperature. The answer is **2000 K**.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. The argument">

Write the burning step as $\mathrm{CO+\tfrac12O_2\to CO_2}$ (or $\mathrm{H_2+\tfrac12O_2\to H_2O}$): $\bar h_R<0$. Van 't Hoff: $K_p$ decreases with $T$, and "the larger $K_p$, the more complete". So the fuel burns more completely at the **lower** temperature, 2000 K.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Shown with numbers (CO₂ at 1 atm)">

For the reverse (dissociation) reaction CO₂ → CO + ½O₂: $\ln K_p=-6.629$ at 2000 K and $-3.301$ at 2500 K (the lib; table A-28 values agree to 0.5 %). Starting from 1 kmol CO₂ at 1 atm the extent is 0.0151 at 2000 K and **0.1298 at 2500 K**: 1.5 % against 13 % of the carbon is left as CO. Van 't Hoff from the two $K_p$ values: $\bar h_R=\dfrac{R_u\ln(K_2/K_1)}{1/T_1-1/T_2}=\dfrac{8.314(3.328)}{1/2000-1/2500}=+276\,700$ kJ/kmol, positive for the dissociation (so negative, $-277$ MJ per kmol of CO₂ formed, for the combustion step), in agreement with the lib's enthalpy of reaction at 2250 K (276 600).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**At 2000 K.** Combustion is exothermic, and exothermic reactions are more complete at lower temperatures ($K_p$ of the forming reaction decreases with $T$): for CO₂ at 1 atm, 1.5 % dissociated at 2000 K against 13 % at 2500 K.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="The printed solution and a caution">

The instructor's printed answer is the same one-sentence argument. One caution for a full-marks answer: raising the pressure pushes every dissociation back toward the products (at 10 atm the CO₂ extent at 2500 K is 6.3 %, half of the 1-atm value), so "more completely" holds *at the same pressure*.

</Callout>

<RealWorld title="Why the hottest flame is not the 'cleanest'">

Peak flame temperatures of 2500–3000 K are above the temperature at which CO₂ and H₂O dissociate appreciably, which is why dilution (excess air, exhaust-gas recirculation, steam) lowers CO and also NO, and why gas-turbine combustors burn lean (typical).

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- Decide the direction with the **sign of $\bar h_R$**, not with $\Delta\nu$; $\Delta\nu$ is for pressure questions.
- State the reaction you mean: "burns more completely" is the *forming* reaction (exothermic); the dissociation is its reverse.

</Traps>

<Problem id="16-40" title="Oxygen heated at 1 atm from 298 K to 3000 K at 0.5 kg/min: heat supplied with and without dissociation" tags="dissociation, O2 to 2O, steady flow, Q">

<Statement>

Oxygen (O₂) is heated in a steady-flow process at 1 atm from 298 K to 3000 K at a rate of 0.5 kg/min. Determine the rate of heat supply needed (a) if some O₂ dissociates into O, and (b) if no dissociation takes place.

</Statement>

<Think>

Three tasks in order: **composition** at 3000 K and 1 atm from $K_p$ of $\mathrm{O_2\rightleftharpoons2O}$; **steady-flow energy balance** with the formation enthalpy of atomic oxygen (the dissociation heat is hidden in $\bar h_f(\mathrm O)$); **convert** per kmol to the kg/min flow. Part (b) is the plain sensible heating of O₂.

</Think>

<Plan>

$x$ from $K_p$ → products $x\mathrm{O_2}+2(1-x)\mathrm{O}$ → $Q=\sum N_P\bar h_P-\bar h_{O_2}(298)$ → $\times\dot N$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Equilibrium composition">

$\mathrm{O_2\to xO_2+2(1-x)O}$, $N_{total}=2-x$, $\Delta\nu=2-1=1$. <T>A-28: ln K_p = −4.357 at 3000 K for O₂ ⇌ 2O</T>, $K_p=0.01282$: $0.01282=\dfrac{[2(1-x)]^2}{x}\left(\dfrac{1}{2-x}\right)$, i.e. $4.0128x^2-8.0256x+4=0$, so $x=\dfrac{8.0256-\sqrt{0.2057}}{2(4.0128)}=\mathbf{0.943}$ (lib: 0.9435): the products are $0.943\,\mathrm{O_2}+0.114\,\mathrm{O}$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. (a) Energy balance (W = 0)">

<T>Table A-26 and A-18/A-19: h̄f(O) = 249 190 kJ/kmol; h̄(O, 298) = 6852, h̄(O, 3000) = 63 425; h̄(O₂, 298) = 8682, h̄(O₂, 3000) = 106 780</T>. $\bar Q_{in}=0.943(0+106\,780-8682)+0.114(249\,190+63\,425-6852)-0=92\,500+34\,860=\mathbf{127\,360}$ kJ/kmol of O₂ (lib: 127 137).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Flow and rate">

$\dot N=\dfrac{0.5}{32}=0.01563$ kmol/min. (a) $\dot Q=0.01563(127\,360)=\mathbf{1990}$ kJ/min (lib, exact extent: **1986.6**).

</Step>
<Step title="4. (b) No dissociation">

$\dot Q=\dot N(\bar h_2-\bar h_1)=0.01563(106\,780-8682)=\mathbf{1533}$ kJ/min (lib 1533.5).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) **1990 kJ/min** (1986.6 with the unrounded extent; the printed solution gives 1990). (b) **1533 kJ/min**. Dissociation costs 30 % more heat (29 300 kJ per kmol of O₂ fed), although 94 % of the oxygen molecules survive.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Why hot air takes more heat than the tables of an ideal gas say">

Above about 2500 K, dissociation of O₂ and N₂ makes air absorb extra heat at the same temperature: that is why the specific heat of a re-entry shock layer or of a rocket-chamber gas at 3000 K is effectively much larger than the ideal-gas value (typical), and why the real flame temperature is below the no-dissociation one.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- Write the reaction **as in table A-28** ($\mathrm{O_2\to2O}$, $\Delta\nu=+1$) and put the unknown on the *products*: $x\mathrm{O_2}+2(1-x)\mathrm{O}$, $N_{total}=2-x$.
- The heat is the *change of absolute enthalpy*: use $\bar h_f(\mathrm O)=249\,190$, not the sensible rows alone.
- Per kmol → per minute: $\dot N=\dot m/M$ with $M=32$ kg/kmol; the reaction is per kmol of O₂ fed, not per kmol of products.

</Traps>
