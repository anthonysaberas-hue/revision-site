<Callout kind="note" title="What this assignment teaches, in one paragraph">

(1) For a reaction $\nu_A A+\nu_B B\rightleftharpoons\nu_C C+\nu_D D$ the equilibrium constant is $K_p=\dfrac{N_C^{\nu_C}N_D^{\nu_D}}{N_A^{\nu_A}N_B^{\nu_B}}\left(\dfrac{P}{N_{total}}\right)^{\Delta\nu}$ with $P$ in **atm**, $\Delta\nu=\nu_C+\nu_D-\nu_A-\nu_B$ and $N_{total}$ the moles of **gas** at equilibrium (a pure solid appears in neither). (2) $K_p$ depends on **temperature only** (Table A-28 gives $\ln K_p$); the *composition* responds to $P$, to inerts and to $T$ through the factor $(P/N_{total})^{\Delta\nu}$ and through $K_p(T)$. (3) The recipe: unknown extent $x$ → amounts → $N_{total}$ → $K_p$ relation → solve. (4) The heat transfer of a dissociating stream is $Q=\sum N_{out}\bar h-\sum N_{in}\bar h$ with the **equilibrium** amounts, and a liquid fuel enters with $\bar h_f(liq)$ (your note "liquid!!"). (5) Van 't Hoff: $\ln\dfrac{K_{p2}}{K_{p1}}=\dfrac{\bar h_R}{R_u}\left(\dfrac1{T_1}-\dfrac1{T_2}\right)$ links $K_p$ and the reaction enthalpy.

</Callout>

<Problem id="16-4C" title="CO2, CO and O2 in equilibrium: the effect of temperature and of pressure on CO2" tags="Le Chatelier, Kp, delta nu, exothermic">

<Statement>

A reaction chamber holds CO₂, CO and O₂ in equilibrium at a given temperature and pressure. How do (a) a temperature increase at constant pressure and (b) a pressure increase at constant temperature change the number of moles of CO₂?

</Statement>

<Think>

A conceptual problem with two separate arguments, one for each variable. Temperature acts through $K_p(T)$, so you need the **sign of the reaction enthalpy** (combustion releases heat, so the formation of CO₂ is exothermic). Pressure leaves $K_p$ unchanged and acts through the factor $(P/N_{total})^{\Delta\nu}$, so you need the sign of $\Delta\nu$. This is the first place in the course where a $K_p$ expression is used as a *rule*, not a formula to compute with.

</Think>

<Plan>

Write the reaction in the direction that produces CO₂ → write $K_p$ with $\Delta\nu$ → (a) how $K_p$ moves with $T$ → (b) hold $K_p$ fixed and let $P$ change.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. The reaction and its Kp">

$\mathrm{CO}+\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{CO_2}$, so $K_p=\dfrac{N_{CO_2}}{N_{CO}\,N_{O_2}^{1/2}}\left(\dfrac{P}{N_{total}}\right)^{\Delta\nu}$ with $\Delta\nu=1-1-\tfrac12=\mathbf{-\tfrac12}$ (your page: $\Delta\nu=1-1-0.5=-0.5$ ✔).

</Step>
<Step title="2. (a) Temperature up at constant P">

$K_p$ depends on $T$ only, and for this exothermic reaction ($\bar h_R<0$) the van 't Hoff equation says it **decreases** as $T$ rises. A smaller $K_p$ means less CO₂ in the numerator: the number of moles of CO₂ **decreases** and CO and O₂ increase. Your page: "$\ln K_p$ decreases with the increase of $T$ … the number of moles of CO₂ decreases and rises of CO and O₂" ✔.

</Step>
<Step title="3. (b) Pressure up at constant T">

$K_p$ is fixed. With $\Delta\nu=-\tfrac12$ the factor $(P/N_{total})^{-1/2}$ **falls** when $P$ rises, so the mole ratio $N_{CO_2}/(N_{CO}N_{O_2}^{1/2})$ must **rise** to keep $K_p$ constant: CO₂ **increases**. (The system moves toward the side with fewer moles.) Your v1 page: "negative $\Delta\nu$ … with $P\uparrow$ the number of moles of CO₂ will rise" ✔.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) CO₂ **decreases** (higher $T$ lowers $K_p$ of an exothermic reaction); (b) CO₂ **increases** (higher $P$ favors the side with fewer moles, $\Delta\nu=-\tfrac12$). Your pages ✔ (both copies).

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Why combustion is more complete at moderate temperature and high pressure">

At ordinary flame temperatures the CO₂ ⇌ CO + ½O₂ equilibrium lies far to the CO₂ side; it moves toward CO above about 2000 K. This is why a gas turbine or a diesel burning at high pressure produces *less* CO from dissociation than the same flame at 1 atm, and why a very hot flame can be less "complete" than a cooler one.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- $K_p$ does **not** change with pressure; only the composition does.
- The sign of $\Delta\nu$ depends on how the reaction is written (CO₂ → CO + ½O₂ has $\Delta\nu=+\tfrac12$). The conclusion must be the same for both ways of writing it.
- Temperature: decide with the **sign of $\bar h_R$** (exothermic or endothermic), not with $\Delta\nu$.

</Traps>

<Problem id="16-6C" title="Adding inert nitrogen at constant T and P to a CO2 / CO / O2 equilibrium" tags="inert gas, Le Chatelier, N_total, delta nu">

<Statement>

A reaction chamber holds CO₂, CO and O₂ in equilibrium at a given temperature and pressure. Nitrogen is added while the temperature **and the total pressure** are kept constant. Does this change the number of moles of O₂, and how?

</Statement>

<Think>

The new idea: an inert gas takes part in the equilibrium only through $N_{total}$. At **constant total pressure**, adding N₂ raises $N_{total}$ and so **lowers** $P/N_{total}$: it acts exactly like a pressure decrease. The reaction then shifts toward the side with *more* moles.

</Think>

<Plan>

$K_p$ for CO₂ ⇌ CO + ½O₂ with $\Delta\nu=+\tfrac12$ → $K_p$ fixed, $(P/N_{total})^{1/2}$ smaller → the mole ratio must grow.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Kp for the dissociation">

$K_p=\dfrac{N_{CO}\,N_{O_2}^{1/2}}{N_{CO_2}}\left(\dfrac{P}{N_{total}}\right)^{1/2}$, with $\Delta\nu=1+\tfrac12-1=+\tfrac12$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Effect of the inert">

$K_p$ depends only on $T$ and $T$ is constant. Adding N₂ makes $N_{total}$ larger at constant $P$, so $(P/N_{total})^{1/2}$ is smaller; therefore $N_{CO}N_{O_2}^{1/2}/N_{CO_2}$ must be **larger**: the equilibrium shifts to the right, CO₂ decreases, **CO and O₂ increase**.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Your v2 re-solve (18 Dec) says 'O₂ will decrease': it increases">

Your Chapter 16 v2 page begins "the total number of moles will change, it will go higher, so the ratio is higher …" and ends, legibly, "O₂ will decrease". The reasoning "$N_{total}$ goes up" is right but the conclusion is the wrong way round. Your 17 Dec first pass wrote $N_T\uparrow$, CO₂ ↓, and "(CO and O₂) ↑" in red, which is correct. The mistake to avoid is to think "more moles in the box, so it must favor the side with fewer moles": that is the effect of raising the **pressure**. An inert at constant $P$ is the opposite, a pressure *decrease* for the reacting gases. (Errata N10.)

</Callout>

<Answer>

**O₂ increases** (and CO increases, CO₂ decreases): the inert at constant $T$ and $P$ lowers $P/N_{total}$ and favors the side with more moles. Your 17 Dec page ✔; your 18 Dec page ✗.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Diluting a furnace atmosphere">

The same argument says that a dilute, very hot atmosphere of CO₂ with inert N₂ (flue gas at 1 atm) dissociates more than pure CO₂ at the same $T$ and $P$, which is why dissociation corrections matter for flames in air but are negligible in oxy-fuel flames at high pressure.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- Inert **at constant volume** raises neither the partial pressures nor the equilibrium shift; inert **at constant total pressure** is the effect asked here.
- The inert enters $K_p$ only through $N_{total}$.
- Check with the limiting case: infinite dilution is the low-pressure limit, where dissociation is complete.

</Traps>

<Problem id="16-40" title="Oxygen heated from 298 K to 3000 K at 1 atm: heat with and without dissociation" tags="O2 dissociation, Kp, steady flow, first law">

<Statement>

Oxygen is heated at 1 atm from 298 K to 3000 K in a steady-flow process at 0.5 kg/min. Find the heat supply rate needed if (a) some O₂ dissociates into O and (b) no dissociation occurs.

</Statement>

<Think>

The cleanest example of "dissociation absorbs heat": the same stream needs **more** heat when part of it becomes atoms, because breaking the bond (the enthalpy of formation of O, 249 190 kJ/kmol) is paid for by the heater. New: you compute the composition first, then the first law with the **equilibrium mixture** at the outlet.

</Think>

<Plan>

$\mathrm{O_2}\rightleftharpoons2\mathrm{O}$ at 3000 K → amount of O from $K_p$ → $Q=\sum N\bar h(3000)-\bar h_{O_2}(298)$ per kmol of O₂ fed → $\dot Q=\dot N\,Q$ → compare with no dissociation.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Amounts">

Per kmol of O₂ fed, let $x$ kmol of O atoms form: O₂ $=1-\tfrac x2$, O $=x$, $N_{total}=1+\tfrac x2$ (your page: "$2-x=2y$" for the balance written on the 2-atom basis).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Kp relation">

<T>A-28 at 3000 K: ln Kp (O₂ ⇌ 2O) = −4.357</T> (your page ✔). $e^{-4.357}=\dfrac{x^2}{1-0.5x}\left(\dfrac{1}{1+0.5x}\right)^{1}$, so **$x=0.113$** kmol O (your page ✔: $x=0.113$; the lib gives 0.1130), O₂ $=0.9435$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Heat per kmol of O₂ (a)">

<T>A-19 and A-26 (lib values; the printed table differs by 0.05 %): h̄ of O₂ at 3000 K = 98 142 kJ/kmol; h̄ of O at 3000 K = 249 190 + 56 577 = 305 767 kJ/kmol; h̄ of O₂ at 298 K = 0 (formation datum)</T>. $Q=0.9435(98\,142)+0.113(305\,767)-0=\mathbf{127\,107}$ kJ/kmol of O₂ (your page: 127 106.686 ✔).

</Step>
<Step title="4. Rate (a)">

$\dot N=\dfrac{0.5}{32}=0.015625$ kmol/min; $\dot Q=0.015625(127\,107)=\mathbf{1986}$ kJ/min (33.1 kW).

</Step>
<Step title="5. No dissociation (b)">

$Q=\bar h_{O_2}(3000)-\bar h_{O_2}(298)=98\,142$ kJ/kmol and $\dot Q=\dot N(h_2-h_1)=0.015625(98\,142)=\mathbf{1533}$ kJ/min (your page: 1532.7 ✔).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="note" title="Check your page: 1966 or 1986?">

Your v2 page writes the rate in kJ/min right after the Q per kmol, and the digits read "1966" (and a kW value that looks like 33.1 or 32.8). With your own $Q=127\,106.7$ kJ/kmol and $\dot N=0.5/32$, the product is **1986** kJ/min, so if the page says 1966 it is a slip of 20 kJ/min (1 %); the third digit of that number is ambiguous in the scan, so no error is asserted.

</Callout>

<Answer>

(a) **$\dot Q\approx1986$ kJ/min** (33.1 kW), with 0.113 kmol of O atoms per kmol of O₂ fed; (b) **1533 kJ/min** (25.6 kW) ✔ (your 1532.7). Dissociation raises the heat requirement by about 30 %.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Arc heaters and re-entry">

Heating air or oxygen to 3000 K in an arc-jet or behind a re-entry shock dissociates a few percent of the molecules and the dissociation absorbs about a quarter of the energy supplied; the energy is released again when the atoms recombine on the vehicle's surface, a major part of the heat load on a re-entry capsule.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- $\bar h$ of the **atom** includes its formation enthalpy (249 190 kJ/kmol), which is what makes dissociation costly.
- The outlet has fewer O₂ molecules than the inlet: weight each species by its **equilibrium** amount.
- Convert kg/min to kmol/min with $M=32$.

</Traps>

<Problem id="16-52C" title="Does a fuel burn more completely at 2000 K or at 2500 K?" tags="dissociation, Kp, Le Chatelier, conceptual">

<Statement>

Will a fuel burn more completely at 2000 K or at 2500 K?

</Statement>

<Think>

A one-line application of the temperature rule from 16-4C: combustion products (CO₂, H₂O) are **stable** at lower $T$ and **dissociate** at higher $T$ (dissociation is endothermic, $K_p$ of CO₂ ⇌ CO + ½O₂ increases with $T$). At 2500 K more CO and O₂ (and OH, H₂, H, O) appear, so the burn is *less* complete there.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="The argument">

For CO₂ ⇌ CO + ½O₂ at $P=1$ atm: <T>A-28: ln Kp = −6.635 at 2000 K, −3.860 at 2400 K, and about −3.30 at 2500 K (lib)</T>, so the dissociation constant grows by a factor of about 28 between 2000 and 2500 K and a much larger fraction of the CO₂ is dissociated. The page of your v2 copy writes only "2000" as the answer ✔.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**2000 K**: the lower temperature, because the dissociation reactions are endothermic and their $K_p$ rises with $T$; at 2500 K the products contain appreciable CO, O₂, H₂, OH. Your page ✔ ("2000").

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Why the hottest flame is not the most efficient">

Burner designs deliberately keep peak flame temperature below about 2000 K with excess air or recirculated exhaust: higher temperatures dissociate the products (energy locked in CO and H₂), and they also make NO exponentially (16-31).

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- "Complete combustion" in chapter 15 assumes CO₂ and H₂O; chapter 16 shows how far that fails above 2000 K.
- Don't answer with the *adiabatic flame temperature*: a higher flame temperature is the result of burning more completely at a given $T$-independent heat release, not the other way round.

</Traps>

<Problem id="16-56" title="Enthalpy of reaction of CO2 → CO + ½O2 at 2200 K, from enthalpy data and from Kp" tags="van 't Hoff, enthalpy of reaction, Kp, consistency check">

<Statement>

<Think>

The reaction enthalpy is a property of the reaction, so the two routes must agree: the first uses the **first law** (enthalpy tables), the second uses the **second law** through $K_p$ and van 't Hoff, $\dfrac{d\ln K_p}{dT}=\dfrac{\bar h_R}{R_uT^2}$, integrated with $\bar h_R$ constant between 2000 and 2400 K. </Plan>
