<Problem id="4-9" title="Constant-pressure heating of steam: the interpolation trap" tags="boundary work, superheated table, interpolation, P·v">

<Statement>

A piston–cylinder device holds 5 kg of saturated water vapor at 150 kPa. It is heated at constant pressure until the temperature reaches 200 °C. Find the work done by the steam. (Printed answer: 214 kJ.)

</Statement>

<Think>

Constant pressure means $W_b=P(V_2-V_1)=mP(v_2-v_1)$. State 1 is saturated vapor at 150 kPa. State 2 is at 150 kPa and 200 °C; the superheated table (A-6) lists 100 kPa and 200 kPa but **not** 150 kPa, so you must interpolate in pressure. That is the whole problem, and it teaches the first rule of this course's property work: **interpolate the quantity that varies linearly**, here $Pv\approx$ constant, not $v\propto1/P$.

</Think>

<Plan>

$v_1=v_g$ at 150 kPa $\to$ $v_2$ at (150 kPa, 200 °C) by interpolating $Pv$ between the 100 kPa and 200 kPa rows $\to$ $W_b=mP(v_2-v_1)$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. State 1">

<T>A-5 at 150 kPa, saturated vapor: v₁ = v_g = 1.1594 m³/kg</T>. Your page: $v_1=1.15941$ ✔.

</Step>
<Step title="2. State 2, your way (linear in v)">

<T>A-6 at 200 °C: v(100 kPa) = 2.17245, v(200 kPa) = 1.08049 m³/kg</T>. You averaged the two values: $v_2=\dfrac{2.17245+1.08049}{2}=1.6264$ m³/kg. Then $W=150\times5\times(1.6264-1.15941)=\mathbf{350.3\ kJ}$ (your page: $W=350.28$ kJ).

</Step>
<Step title="3. State 2, the right way (linear in P·v)">

$P_1v_1=100(2.17245)=217.25$ and $P_2v_2=200(1.08049)=216.10$ kJ/kg; their average at 150 kPa is $216.67$, so $v_2=\dfrac{216.67}{150}=\mathbf{1.4445}$ m³/kg (direct evaluation: 1.4445). The two numbers differ by 12.6 %.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Work">

$W=mP(v_2-v_1)=5(150)(1.4445-1.1594)=\mathbf{213.8\ kJ}$, which is the printed 214 kJ.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Statement>

1 m³ of saturated liquid water at 200 °C is expanded isothermally in a closed system until its quality is 80 %. Find the total work produced, in kJ.

</Statement>

<Think>

Inside the vapor dome temperature fixes pressure: isothermal means isobaric at $P_{sat}(200\ °C)$, so $W=P\,\Delta V$ with the pressure constant. What is new: the **mass** comes from the initial *liquid* volume, $m=V_1/v_f$, and the final volume from the mixture formula $v=v_f+xv_{fg}$.

</Think>

</Step>

</Step>

$W=P(V_2-V_1)=1554.9\,(88.15-1)=\mathbf{135\,523\ kJ}$. Your page: $W=135\,522.77$ kJ.

</Steps>

<Problem id="4-15" title="Polytropic expansion, PVⁿ = constant with n = 1.5" tags="polytropic, boundary work">

<Statement>

A gas expands in a piston–cylinder device with $PV^n=C$ from 350 kPa and 0.03 m³ to 0.2 m³, with $n=1.5$. Find the work.

</Statement>

<Think>

For $PV^n=C$ the work integral is $\int P\,dV=\dfrac{P_2V_2-P_1V_1}{1-n}$ (for $n\ne1$). First find $P_2$ from the constant.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Final pressure">

$P_2=P_1\left(\dfrac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^n=350\left(\dfrac{0.03}{0.2}\right)^{1.5}=\mathbf{20.33\ kPa}$ (your page: 20.33).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Work">

$W=\dfrac{P_2V_2-P_1V_1}{1-n}=\dfrac{20.33(0.2)-350(0.03)}{1-1.5}=\dfrac{4.066-10.5}{-0.5}=\mathbf{12.87\ kJ}$ (your page: 12.9 kJ ✔).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$P_2=20.3$ kPa, $W=12.9$ kJ** (positive: the gas does work).

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Real compressors and engines are polytropic">

The exponent $n$ of a real compression or expansion lies between 1 (isothermal, perfect cooling) and $k$ (adiabatic): about 1.2–1.35 for a cooled compressor or an engine cylinder. The formula above is the area under that curve.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- $n=1$ is the isothermal case with its own formula, $W=P_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1)$; the formula above divides by zero there.

<Problem id="7-26" title="Paddle-wheel work on an ideal gas held at constant temperature" tags="entropy, ideal gas, rigid tank">

<Statement>

A rigid tank contains an ideal gas at 40 °C that is stirred by a paddle wheel doing 200 kJ of work on it. The gas temperature stays constant because heat flows to the surroundings at 30 °C. Find the entropy change of the ideal gas. (This statement is in the 9th-edition textbook as 7-26; your page header gives only "ideal gas, W = 200 kJ, surroundings 30 °C".)

</Statement>

<Think>

An entropy-change question about an *ideal gas* is answered by the property formula, not by $Q/T$: $s_2-s_1=c_v\ln\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}+R\ln\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}$. Here the tank is rigid ($v$ constant) and the temperature is constant, so both terms vanish. The 200 kJ is a distraction for *this* question, and exactly what the *surroundings* and the generation need.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Gas">

$T_2=T_1$ and $v_2=v_1$: $\Delta S_{gas}=m\left[c_v\ln1+R\ln1\right]=\mathbf{0}$. Your page ✔ ("$\Delta S=0$: no change in entropy").

</Step>
<Step title="2. For completeness: surroundings and generation">

First law for the gas: $\Delta U=0=W_{in}-Q_{out}$, so $Q_{out}=200$ kJ. The surroundings at $30\ °C=303.15$ K receive it: $\Delta S_{surr}=\dfrac{200}{303.15}=+0.660$ kJ/K, so $S_{gen}=0+0.660=\mathbf{0.660\ kJ/K}>0$: stirring is irreversible (work turned into heat).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$\Delta S_{gas}=0$** ✔ (and $S_{gen}=0.660$ kJ/K for the whole process).

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Every brake, stirrer and resistor does this">

Mechanical or electrical work turned into heat at constant temperature produces entropy $Q/T_{surr}$ in the surroundings and none in the gas: that is why a heater has $\eta_{II}\ll1$ even though its first-law efficiency is 100 %.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- Writing $\Delta S=Q/T$ for the gas: that is the *reversible* formula and the process is not reversible.
- Your header reads the gas temperature as 400 °C; the textbook says 40 °C. It cancels in the answer.

</Traps>

<Problem id="9-95" title="Simple Brayton cycle with component efficiencies and a given turbine exit temperature" tags="Brayton, constant cp, ηC, ηT, back-work ratio">

<Statement>

A gas-turbine plant runs a simple Brayton cycle between 100 and 1600 kPa. Air enters the compressor at 40 °C at 850 m³/min and leaves the turbine at 650 °C. $\eta_C=0.85$, $\eta_T=0.88$. Use $c_v=0.821$, $c_p=1.108$ kJ/kg·K, $k=1.35$. Find (a) the net power, (b) the back-work ratio, (c) the thermal efficiency. (Printed: 6488 kW, 0.511, 37.8 %.)

</Statement>

<Think>

The twist is that the **turbine inlet temperature is not given**; the turbine *exit* temperature is. So the turbine efficiency is used backwards: $\eta_T=\dfrac{T_3-T_4}{T_3-T_{4s}}$ with $T_{4s}=T_3/r_p^{(k-1)/k}$ is one equation in $T_3$. Everything else is the standard chain: compressor, turbine, mass flow from the ideal-gas law.

</Think>

<Plan>

$T_{2s}$ from the pressure ratio $\to$ $T_2$ with $\eta_C$ $\to$ solve $T_3$ from $\eta_T$ and the known $T_4$ $\to$ $\dot m=PV̇/RT_1$ $\to$ $\dot W_{net}$, $r_{bw}$, $\eta$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Compressor">

$T_{2s}=T_1r_p^{(k-1)/k}=313.15(16)^{0.35/1.35}=642.6$ K (your page: 642.28 with 313 K). $T_2=T_1+\dfrac{T_{2s}-T_1}{\eta_C}=313.15+\dfrac{642.6-313.15}{0.85}=\mathbf{700.7}$ K (your page: 700).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Turbine inlet temperature from the given exit">

$T_{4s}=\dfrac{T_3}{16^{0.2593}}=0.4872\,T_3$, $T_4=923.15$ K. $0.88=\dfrac{T_3-923.15}{T_3(1-0.4872)}\Rightarrow T_3=\mathbf{1682}$ K (your page: 1682 K ✔).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Mass flow">

$\dot m=\dfrac{P_1\dot V}{RT_1}=\dfrac{100(850/60)}{0.287(313.15)}=\mathbf{15.76}$ kg/s (your page: 15.77 with 313 K).

</Step>
<Step title="4. Net power, back-work ratio, efficiency">

$\dot W_{net}=\dot m\,c_p\left[(T_3-T_4)-(T_2-T_1)\right]=15.76(1.108)\left[(1682-923.2)-(700.7-313.15)\right]=15.76(1.108)(371.3)=\mathbf{6484}$ kW. $r_{bw}=\dfrac{T_2-T_1}{T_3-T_4}=\dfrac{387.6}{758.8}=\mathbf{0.511}$. $q_{in}=c_p(T_3-T_2)=1.108(1681.98-700.72)=1087.2$ kJ/kg, so $\eta=\dfrac{411.4}{1087.2}=\mathbf{0.378}$ and $\dot Q_{in}=15.76(1087.2)=17\,140$ kW.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Problem id="9-109" title="GE 7FA: back-solve the component efficiencies, then add a 65 % regenerator" tags="Brayton, air table, efficiency, regenerator">

<Statement>

The GE 7FA gas turbine is reported to have 35.9 % efficiency in simple-cycle mode and 159 MW net, with pressure ratio 14.7, turbine inlet 1288 °C and 1 536 000 kg/h through the turbine, ambient 30 °C and 100 kPa. Find the isentropic efficiencies of the turbine and the compressor, and the thermal efficiency if a regenerator of effectiveness 65 % is added.

</Statement>

<Think>

Two unknown efficiencies, but the numbers given are **net work and cycle efficiency**. Your method is a clean linear trick: $q_{in}=w_{net}/\eta$ gives the actual compressor exit $h_{2a}=h_3-q_{in}$ directly; then $q_{out}=q_{in}-w_{net}$ gives the actual turbine exit $h_{4a}=h_1+q_{out}$. Only then are the efficiencies computed, no simultaneous equations. The air table (variable $c_p$) is needed because 1561 K is far from room temperature.

</Think>

<Plan>

Ideal states from the table ($P_r$ method) $\to$ $w_{net}$ and $q_{in}$ $\to$ $h_{2a}$ and $h_{4a}$ $\to$ $\eta_C,\eta_T$ $\to$ regenerator.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Ideal states from the air table">

<T>A-17 at 303 K: h₁ = 303.21 kJ/kg, P_r1 = 1.4356 (interpolated)</T>. $P_{r2}=14.7(1.4356)=21.10\to$ <T>h₂s = 652.4 kJ/kg</T>. <T>A-17 at 1561 K: h₃ = 1710 kJ/kg, P_r3 = 719.5</T>; $P_{r4}=\dfrac{719.5}{14.7}=48.94\to$ <T>h₄s = 825.2 kJ/kg</T>.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Specific work and heat">

$\dot m=\dfrac{1\,536\,000}{3600}=426.7$ kg/s; $w_{net}=\dfrac{159\,000}{426.7}=\mathbf{372.66}$ kJ/kg. $q_{in}=\dfrac{w_{net}}{\eta}=\dfrac{372.66}{0.359}=\mathbf{1038}$ kJ/kg.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Actual compressor and turbine exits">

$q_{in}=h_3-h_{2a}\Rightarrow h_{2a}=1710-1038=672.0$ kJ/kg. $q_{out}=q_{in}-w_{net}=665.34$ kJ/kg and $q_{out}=h_{4a}-h_1\Rightarrow h_{4a}=665.34+303.21=968.55$ kJ/kg.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Efficiencies">

$\eta_T=\dfrac{h_3-h_{4a}}{h_3-h_{4s}}=\dfrac{1710-968.55}{1710-825.2}=\mathbf{0.838}$. $\eta_C=\dfrac{h_{2s}-h_1}{h_{2a}-h_1}=\dfrac{652.4-303.2}{672.0-303.2}=\mathbf{0.947}$ (your page prints 0.948: its numerator uses a slightly larger $h_{2s}$ than the 652.4 above).

</Step>
<Step title="5. Add a regenerator, ε = 0.65">

$q_{regen}=\varepsilon(h_{4a}-h_{2a})=0.65(968.55-672.0)=192.8$ kJ/kg, so $q_{in,new}=1038-192.8=845.2$ and $\eta=\dfrac{372.66}{845.2}=\mathbf{0.441}$.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$\eta_T=83.8\ \%$, $\eta_C=94.8\ \%$ (94.7–94.9 by table reading), regenerated $\eta=44.1\ \%$.** Recomputed with the lib's smooth air table and $T_1=303.15$ K: $\eta_C=95.2\ \%$, $\eta_T=83.7\ \%$, regenerated 44.1 %.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="GE's 7FA, real and idealized">

The 7FA is a frame-7 heavy-duty industrial turbine (about 60 Hz, 3600 rpm) whose published simple-cycle rating is about 36 % at roughly 160–200 MW, with a turbine inlet around 1300 °C (as the problem states). A combined-cycle plant that sends the 600 °C exhaust through a steam bottoming cycle reaches typically 55–60 %, which is why nobody adds a regenerator to a 7FA: the exhaust is more valuable making steam.

</RealWorld>

<Problem id="9-126" title="Two-stage compression with intercooling and two-stage expansion with reheat, with and without a regenerator" tags="Brayton, intercooling, reheat, regeneration, air table">

<Statement>

A gas-turbine cycle has two compression stages and two expansion stages; the pressure ratio per stage is 3. Air enters each compressor stage at 300 K and each turbine stage at 1200 K. With $\eta_C=0.86$ per compressor stage and $\eta_T=0.90$ per turbine stage, find the back-work ratio and the thermal efficiency (a) without a regenerator and (b) with a regenerator of effectiveness 75 %. Use variable specific heats. (This is 9-126; its ideal version is 9-125.)

</Statement>

<Think>

Because the stages are identical, the work is just twice one stage: $w_C=2(h_{2a}-h_1)$ and $w_T=2(h_5-h_{6a})$. Heat is added **twice** (before turbine 1 and again at the reheater): $q_{in}=(h_5-h_4)+(h_7-h_{6a})$. The regenerator takes heat from the last turbine exit ($h_{6a}$ equals the final exhaust) and gives it to the air leaving the second compressor ($h_4=h_{2a}$).

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Compressor stage">

<T>A-17 at 300 K: h₁ = 300.19, P_r1 = 1.386</T>. $P_{r2}=3(1.386)=4.158\to T_{2s}=410.1$ K, <T>h₂s = 411.26</T> (your page 411.26). $h_{2a}=h_1+\dfrac{h_{2s}-h_1}{0.86}=300.19+\dfrac{111.07}{0.86}=\mathbf{429.34}$ kJ/kg. $w_C=2(429.34-300.19)=\mathbf{258.3}$ kJ/kg.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Turbine stage">

<T>A-17 at 1200 K: h₅ = 1277.79, P_r5 = 238.0</T>. $P_{r6}=\dfrac{238.0}{3}=79.33\to T_{6s}=911.95$ K, <T>h₆s = 946.35</T>. $h_{6a}=h_5-0.90(h_5-h_{6s})=1277.79-0.90(331.44)=\mathbf{979.49}$ kJ/kg. $w_T=2(1277.79-979.49)=\mathbf{596.6}$ kJ/kg.

</Step>
<Step title="3. (a) No regenerator">

$r_{bw}=\dfrac{258.3}{596.6}=\mathbf{0.433}$ (your page 0.432). $q_{in}=(1277.79-429.34)+(1277.79-979.49)=\mathbf{1146.75}$; $\eta=\dfrac{596.6-258.3}{1146.75}=\mathbf{0.295}$.

</Step>
<Step title="4. (b) With ε = 0.75">

$q_{regen}=0.75(h_{6a}-h_4)=0.75(979.49-429.34)=412.6$; $q_{in,new}=1146.75-412.6=734.1$; $\eta=\dfrac{338.3}{734.1}=\mathbf{0.461}$ (46.0 % with unrounded table values; the answer on your page is 46.1 %).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) **$r_{bw}=0.433$, $\eta=29.5\ \%$**; (b) **$\eta=46.1\ \%$** with the 75 % regenerator ✔. For the ideal version (9-125) the same method gives $r_{bw}=0.335$, $\eta=36.8\ \%$, and 55 % with the regenerator.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Intercooling and reheat in practice">

Reheat ("sequential combustion") is used in the GT24/GT26 family: two combustors with a turbine between them. Intercooling appears in the aeroderivative GE LMS100. Both add **work** per kilogram of air (here +80 % compared with the simple cycle), but lower the *un-regenerated* efficiency, so they are used where fuel is cheap and size matters, or together with a regenerator or a steam bottoming cycle.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- Intercooling returns the air to the **first** inlet temperature (300 K), reheat to the turbine inlet temperature (1200 K): these are the two statements that make the stages identical.
- Two heat-addition terms in $q_{in}$ (heater and reheater).
- Regenerator heat uses $h_{6a}$ and $h_4$: the *second* compressor's exit (it has already been intercooled and re-compressed), not $h_{2a}$.

</Traps>

<Problem id="9-136" title="Turbojet at 9150 m: velocity, propulsive power and fuel flow (ideal components)" tags="turbojet, ram, thrust, fuel flow">

<Statement>

A turbojet flies at 280 m/s at 9150 m where the ambient state is 32 kPa and −32 °C. $r_c=12$, turbine inlet 1100 K, 50 kg/s of air, fuel heating value 42 700 kJ/kg. Assuming ideal components and constant $c_p$ at room temperature ($c_p=1.005$, $k=1.4$), find (a) the exhaust velocity, (b) the propulsive power, (c) the fuel flow.

</Statement>

<Think>

The complete jet-engine chain with flight speed. New compared with a test stand: the **ram compression** in the inlet (stagnation values), then $w_T=w_C$, and a nozzle that expands to ambient. Chain of states: 1 free stream → 2 after the diffuser → 3 compressor exit → 4 turbine inlet → 5 turbine exit → 6 nozzle exit.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Inlet (ram)">

$T_2=T_1+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2c_p}=241.15+\dfrac{280^2}{2(1005)}=\mathbf{280.2}$ K (your page: 280). $P_2=P_1\left(\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{k/(k-1)}=32\left(\dfrac{280.2}{241.15}\right)^{3.5}=\mathbf{54.1}$ kPa (your page 54.1 ✔).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Compressor">

$P_3=12(54.1)=649.2$ kPa (your page ✔), $T_3=T_2(12)^{0.2857}=\mathbf{569.8}$ K (your page 569.5).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Turbine and nozzle">

$c_p(T_3-T_2)=c_p(T_4-T_5)\Rightarrow T_5=T_4-T_3+T_2=1100-569.8+280.2=\mathbf{810.4}$ K (your page 810.5). $T_6=T_4\left(\dfrac{P_6}{P_3}\right)^{(k-1)/k}=1100\left(\dfrac{32}{649.2}\right)^{0.2857}=\mathbf{465.5}$ K. Energy balance on the nozzle: $V_6=\sqrt{2000\,c_p(T_5-T_6)}=\sqrt{2000(1.005)(810.4-465.5)}=\mathbf{832.5}$ m/s (your page 832.7).

</Step>
<Step title="4. Propulsive power and fuel">

$\dot W_P=\dot m(V_6-V_1)V_1=50(832.5-280)(280)\times10^{-3}=\mathbf{7735}$ kW (your page 7738). $\dot Q_{in}=\dot mc_p(T_4-T_3)=50(1.005)(1100-569.8)=26\,640$ kW and $\dot m_f=\dfrac{26\,640}{42\,700}=\mathbf{0.624}$ kg/s (your page: $\dot Q=26\,657$, $\dot m_f=0.6243$ ✔).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) **$V_6=832.5$ m/s**, (b) **$\dot W_P=7735$ kW**, (c) **$\dot m_f=0.624$ kg/s** ✔ (your page 832.7 / 7738 / 0.6243; the 0.03 % differences are rounding of $T_1=241$ vs 241.15 K).

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Ram effect at cruise">

At 280 m/s and 32 kPa the ram ratio is only 1.69, yet it already provides the compressor with an inlet 39 K warmer and 22 kPa higher than ambient. At Mach 2+ the ram does most of the compression, which is why a ramjet has no compressor at all.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- Use the **stagnation** $T_2$ as the compressor inlet, not the static $T_1$.
- Thrust is $\dot m(V_6-V_1)$: the captured air already carries momentum $\dot mV_1$.
- Fuel flow comes from the heat added in the combustor, $\dot m c_p(T_4-T_3)$, divided by the heating value.

</Traps>
