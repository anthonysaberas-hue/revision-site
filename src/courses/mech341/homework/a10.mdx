<Callout kind="note" title="What this assignment teaches, in one paragraph">

(1) **Static versus stagnation**: $h_0=h+V^2/2$, so $T_0=T+\dfrac{V^2}{2c_p}$ (with $c_p$ in kJ/kg·K, $V$ in m/s, divide by 1000), and for an isentropic flow $\dfrac{P_0}{P}=\left(\dfrac{T_0}{T}\right)^{k/(k-1)}$. (2) **Mach number** $Ma=V/c$ with the speed of sound $c=\sqrt{kRT}$ at the **static** temperature; $\dfrac{T_0}{T}=1+\dfrac{k-1}{2}Ma^2$. (3) **Sonic ("critical") state**: $\dfrac{T^*}{T_0}=\dfrac{2}{k+1}=0.8333$ and $\dfrac{P^*}{P_0}=\left(\dfrac{2}{k+1}\right)^{k/(k-1)}=0.5283$ for $k=1.4$. (4) **Area ratio** $\dfrac{A}{A^*}=\dfrac1{Ma}\left[\dfrac{2}{k+1}\left(1+\dfrac{k-1}2Ma^2\right)\right]^{\frac{k+1}{2(k-1)}}$. (5) **Choking**: a converging nozzle cannot go beyond $Ma=1$ at its exit; once the exit is sonic, lowering the back pressure changes neither the exit velocity nor the flow rate. (6) With variable specific heats, use the enthalpy table, not $c_p$ at one end.

</Callout>

<Problem id="17-33C" title="Converging nozzle with a sonic exit: what happens when the exit area is reduced?" tags="choking, converging nozzle, mass flow, conceptual">

<Statement>

A converging nozzle has sonic speed at its exit plane. The exit area is now reduced while the inlet conditions are held constant. What happens to (a) the exit velocity and (b) the mass flow rate through the nozzle?

</Statement>

<Think>

Two separate facts: a converging nozzle can accelerate the flow only up to $Ma=1$ (the sonic speed at the **throat**, which for a converging nozzle is the exit); and the mass flow rate is $\dot m=\rho VA$, which at the sonic state equals $A^*P_0\sqrt{k/(RT_0)}\left(\dfrac{2}{k+1}\right)^{\frac{k+1}{2(k-1)}}$, proportional to the exit area for fixed $P_0$ and $T_0$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. (a) Exit velocity">

The velocity at a sonic exit is $V=c^*=\sqrt{kRT^*}$ with $T^*=0.8333T_0$: it depends only on $T_0$. With $T_0$ held constant, the exit velocity **does not change** and stays at the sonic value (your Ass12 page: "for a converging nozzle the maximum velocity is the sonic velocity, so the exit speed remains constant at it" ✔; your first pass: "converging nozzle can't go supersonic").

</Step>
<Step title="2. (b) Mass flow rate">

$\dot m_{max}\propto A^*$ at fixed $P_0$ and $T_0$, so a **smaller** exit area gives a **smaller** mass flow rate (your page: "we will get a reduced mass flow rate since the decrease in flow area will cause a decrease in mass flow rate" ✔).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) The exit velocity stays at the sonic value (**unchanged**); (b) the mass flow rate **decreases** in proportion to the exit area. Your Ass12 page ✔.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Choked flow sizes every relief valve">

A safety or relief valve discharging to the atmosphere is sized from exactly this relation: once the upstream pressure is more than about twice the downstream, the flow is choked and the capacity is simply proportional to the valve's smallest area times the inlet pressure; the same formula governs a gas-pipeline rupture.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- A smaller exit area does not make the jet faster: it makes it **smaller**; speed is capped by $c^*$.
- "Sonic exit" means $P_{exit}=P^*$ is **above** the back pressure; the jet continues to expand outside the nozzle.

</Traps>

</Step>

$P_{01}=P_1\left(\dfrac{T_{01}}{T_1}\right)^{k/(k-1)}=54.05\left(\dfrac{286.8}{255.7}\right)^{3.5}=\mathbf{80.77}$ kPa (your page ✔: $P_{01}=80.77$; textbook 80.77).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Example 17-2: speed of sound and Mach number">

$c=\sqrt{kRT}=\sqrt{1.4(287)(303)}=\mathbf{349}$ m/s (your page ✔) and $Ma=\dfrac{200}{349}=\mathbf{0.573}$ (your page: 0.57 ✔). The inlet is subsonic, so a diverging duct *decelerates* it, which is why a subsonic diffuser diverges.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

17-1: **$T_{01}=286.8$ K, $P_{01}=80.77$ kPa**; 17-2: **$c=349$ m/s, $Ma=0.573$** ✔ your page and the textbook.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Ram rise at 250 m/s">

Ram compression at 250 m/s raises the air temperature by 31 K and the pressure by 49 % before the compressor even turns: that free pressure ratio (1.49) is why a turbojet's overall pressure ratio is larger in flight than on the test stand.

</RealWorld>

- $P_{01}$ needs the exponent $k/(k-1)=3.5$, not $k$.

</Traps>
