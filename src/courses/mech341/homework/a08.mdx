<Callout kind="note" title="What this assignment teaches, in one paragraph">

(1) **Balance** with $\mathrm{O_2+3.76N_2}$ in the air; with a given product analysis, balance **N₂ first** (it fixes the air), then C, H, O. (2) Air-fuel ratio $AF=\dfrac{N_{air}(4.76)\,(29)}{M_{fuel}}$; percent theoretical air is the ratio of actual to theoretical **moles** of air. (3) The **dew point** is the saturation temperature at the water-vapor partial pressure $P_v=y_{H_2O}P$. (4) First law for reactants and products: $Q_{out}=\sum N_R\bar h_R-\sum N_P\bar h_P$ with $\bar h=\bar h_f^\circ+(\bar h-\bar h^\circ)$; for a **closed rigid** system subtract $R_uT$ from every gas: $\bar u=\bar h-R_uT$. (5) **Adiabatic flame temperature**: $H_R=H_P$ (less any heat loss), solved by two trials and a linear interpolation. (6) Heating values: HHV (water as liquid) against LHV (water as vapor); a liquid fuel needs $\bar h_f(liq)=\bar h_f(gas)-Mh_{fg}$.

</Callout>

<Problem id="15-79" title="Acetylene with 30 % excess air and heat loss: adiabatic flame temperature" tags="flame temperature, heat loss, iteration, interpolation">

<Statement>

Acetylene at 25 °C is burned in a steady-flow process with 30 % excess air at 27 °C; 75 000 kJ of heat is lost to the surroundings per kmol of acetylene. Assuming complete combustion, find the exit temperature of the products. (Printed: 2301 K.)

</Statement>

<Think>

The energy balance $H_R=H_P+Q_{out}$ with the formation enthalpies: all the unknown temperature is in the products' $\bar h$. Guess a temperature from the **N₂ equivalent** (the product mixture behaves like 17 kmol of N₂: $\dfrac{\text{energy}}{\text{kmol}}$ → table T), evaluate $H_P$ at two temperatures that bracket the target and interpolate linearly. It is the same method as the 2021 final's decane problem. This problem is worked in <Ch slug="combustion">chapter 15</Ch>.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Balance">

$\mathrm{C_2H_2}+3.25(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\mathrm{N_2})\to2\mathrm{CO_2}+\mathrm{H_2O}+0.75\mathrm{O_2}+12.22\mathrm{N_2}$ (theoretical $a_{th}=2.5$, 130 %: $3.25$).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Energy balance">

$-75\,000=\sum N_P(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ)_P-\sum N_R(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ)_R$. The reactants carry $\bar h_f(\mathrm{C_2H_2})=226\,730$ and the air at 300 K ($\bar h_{300}$: O₂ 8736, N₂ 8723 against $\bar h_{298}$: 8682, 8669). Moving every known term to the right, what remains on the left is the table enthalpy of the products at the unknown temperature: $2\bar h_{CO_2}+\bar h_{H_2O}+0.75\bar h_{O_2}+12.22\bar h_{N_2}=\mathbf{1\,321\,784}$ kJ (your page ✔).

</Step>
<Step title="3. First trial: the N₂ equivalent">

$\dfrac{1\,321\,784}{2+1+0.75+12.22}=\dfrac{1\,321\,784}{15.97}\approx82\,700$ kJ per kmol of products: the N₂ table gives about 2750 K for that value, and because CO₂ and H₂O absorb more energy per kelvin than N₂ the real temperature is **lower**, so you try 2500 K. At 2500 K: $2(\bar h_{CO_2})+\bar h_{H_2O}+0.75\bar h_{O_2}+12.22\bar h_{N_2}=\mathbf{1\,352\,961}$ kJ, higher than the target.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Second trial and interpolation">

At 2300 K: $=\mathbf{1\,320\,517}$ kJ, just below the target, so the answer is "close enough" at 2300 K on your assignment page. In the 18 Dec re-solve you interpolate between 2300 K and 2350 K: $\dfrac{T-2300}{2350-2300}=\dfrac{1\,321\,784-1\,320\,517}{1\,352\,961-1\,320\,517}\Rightarrow T=\mathbf{2302}$ K.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$T_P\approx2300$ K** (assignment page), **2302 K** (interpolated, your v3) ✔; printed 2301 K, recomputed 2303 K with the lib's NASA-polynomial enthalpies.

</Answer>

<Problem id="15-26" title="Butane in 200 % theoretical air: how much water to spray to make the dew point 60 °C" tags="dew point, water injection, mole fraction">

<Statement>

Butane is burned in 200 % theoretical air. For complete combustion, how many kmol of water must be sprayed into the combustion chamber per kmol of fuel if the products are to have a dew point of 60 °C at 100 kPa? (Your pages: 9.77.)

</Statement>

<Think>

The extra water $x$ adds to the H₂O **and** to the total products: $\dfrac{5+x}{N_P+x}=\dfrac{P_g(60\,°C)}{P}$ with $P_g=19.947$ kPa.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Balance with 200 % theoretical air">

$\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}+13(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\mathrm{N_2})+x\mathrm{H_2O}\to4\mathrm{CO_2}+(5+x)\mathrm{H_2O}+6.5\mathrm{O_2}+48.88\mathrm{N_2}$, so $N_P=4+5+6.5+48.88+x=64.38+x$ (your page writes 64.3).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Solve for x">

<T>A-4: P_g at 60 °C = 19.947 kPa</T>. $\dfrac{5+x}{64.38+x}=0.19947\Rightarrow x=\dfrac{0.19947(64.38)-5}{1-0.19947}=\mathbf{9.80}$ kmol (your page: 9.77 with 64.3).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$x=9.8$ kmol of water per kmol of butane** ✔ (your 9.77 differs only by the rounding of $N_P$).

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Water injection in a gas turbine">

Gas turbines inject water or steam into the combustor to lower the flame temperature (less NOx) and to raise the turbine mass flow (more power). The 9.8 kmol here is 176 kg of water per 58 kg of butane: three times the fuel mass.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- The sprayed water is **added to numerator and denominator**.
- Use the 200 % **theoretical** air (13 kmol O₂ per kmol of C₄H₁₀ is $2\times6.5$), not 200 % excess.

</Traps>

<Problem id="15-35" title="Octane burned with 60 % excess air, 15 % of the carbon to CO: product fractions and dew point" tags="incomplete combustion, mole fractions, dew point">

<Statement>

n-Octane is burned with 60 % excess air with 15 % of the carbon in the fuel forming CO. Find the mole fractions of the products and the dew-point temperature at 1 atm. (Printed: CO₂ 0.0678, CO 0.0120, H₂O 0.0897, O₂ 0.0808, N₂ 0.7498, 44.0 °C.) In your notebook this problem is numbered 15-35.

</Statement>

<Think>

The novelty is **partial combustion**: of the 8 C, 6.8 go to CO₂ and 1.2 to CO; the CO takes less O₂, so the O₂ balance is written last and **leaves the extra O₂ in the products**. Your highlighted note: "when you calculate the stoichiometric coefficient you assume perfect combustion".

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Air and balance">

$a_{th}=8+\dfrac{18}{4}=12.5$; with 60 % excess, $a=1.6(12.5)=20$. $\mathrm{C_8H_{18}}+20(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\mathrm{N_2})\to6.8\mathrm{CO_2}+1.2\mathrm{CO}+9\mathrm{H_2O}+8.1\mathrm{O_2}+75.2\mathrm{N_2}$ (your page ✔; O balance: $40=13.6+1.2+9+16.2$).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Mole fractions">

$N_P=6.8+1.2+9+8.1+75.2=100.3$ kmol: $y_{CO_2}=0.0678$, $y_{CO}=0.0120$, $y_{H_2O}=0.0897$, $y_{O_2}=0.0808$, $y_{N_2}=0.7498$ (your page: 0.0677, 0.012, 0.09, 0.0808, 0.7499 ✔).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Dew point">

$P_v=0.0897(101.325)=9.09$ kPa; $T_{dp}=T_{sat}(9.09\ \mathrm{kPa})=\mathbf{44.0}$ °C (your page: ≈ 44 °C).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$y_{CO_2}=0.0678$, $y_{CO}=0.0120$, $y_{H_2O}=0.0897$, $y_{O_2}=0.0808$, $y_{N_2}=0.7498$; $T_{dp}=44.0$ °C** ✔ (printed, your page; $AF=24.1$).

</Answer>

<MoleBarFigure title="Exhaust of the octane engine (mole fractions)" parts={[{ label: 'CO₂', y: 0.0678 }, { label: 'CO', y: 0.012 }, { label: 'H₂O', y: 0.0897 }, { label: 'O₂', y: 0.0808 }, { label: 'N₂', y: 0.7498 }]} caption="15-35: products with 15 % of the carbon in CO." />

<RealWorld title="The wet exhaust of a cold gasoline engine">

A petrol engine's exhaust has a dew point of about 44 °C, so on a cold start the water condenses in the exhaust pipe (the drip from the tailpipe) until the pipe passes 44 °C. The 1.2 % CO is what the catalytic converter oxidizes.

</RealWorld>

<DewPointFigure Pv={9.09} label="15-35 exhaust: 9.09 kPa" caption="15-35: Pv = 9.09 kPa gives a dew point of 44 °C." />

- Balance O₂ **last**, counting the CO's single oxygen.
- Atoms are conserved, moles are not: $N_P=100.3$ is not 20×4.76.

</Traps>

<Problem id="15-52" title="HHV and LHV of gaseous n-octane" tags="heating value, enthalpy of formation, h_fg">

<Statement>

Calculate the higher and lower heating values of gaseous n-octane and compare them with Table A-27.

</Statement>

<Think>

HHV is the magnitude of the enthalpy of combustion with the water as liquid, LHV with the water as vapor; the difference is $N_{H_2O}M_{H_2O}h_{fg}=9\times44\,010$ kJ/kmol.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Enthalpy of combustion">

$\mathrm{C_8H_{18}(g)}+12.5(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\mathrm{N_2})\to8\mathrm{CO_2}+9\mathrm{H_2O}+47\mathrm{N_2}$. <T>A-26: h̄f° C₈H₁₈(g) = −208 450; CO₂ −393 520; H₂O(l) −285 830; H₂O(g) −241 820</T>.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Per kmol and per kg">

HHV: $|8(-393\,520)+9(-285\,830)-(-208\,450)|=\mathbf{5\,512\,180}$ kJ/kmol; LHV: $|8(-393\,520)+9(-241\,820)+208\,450|=\mathbf{5\,116\,090}$ kJ/kmol (your page ✔). With $M=114.23$: **48 255** and **44 787** kJ/kg (your slides page divides by 114: 48 352 and 44 877; the 0.2 % comes from $M$ = 114 instead of 114.23).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**HHV = 5 512 180 kJ/kmol = 48 255 kJ/kg; LHV = 5 116 090 kJ/kmol = 44 787 kJ/kg** ✔. Your note: for the table we need to add the enthalpy of vaporization: Table A-27 lists octane as a **liquid**, whose heating value is smaller by $h_{fg}\approx363$ kJ/kg.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Gasoline energy content">

Petrol is about 44 MJ/kg LHV (the figure here, 44.8, is for pure gaseous octane); the 7 % HHV−LHV gap is the water that leaves the tailpipe as vapor, which is why internal-combustion engines are always rated on the LHV.

</RealWorld>

<Problem id="15-59" title="Propane burned at AF = 25, products cooled to the dew point: heat per kg of fuel" tags="dew point, steady-flow heat, percent theoretical air">

<Statement>

Propane is burned with an air-fuel ratio of 25 in an atmospheric furnace. Find the heat transfer per kilogram of fuel when the products are at the temperature at which liquid water just begins to form.

</Statement>

<Think>

Two steps in a row: first the dew point (so the products' state), then $Q_{out}$ with the products at the dew point and the reactants at 25 °C. The air-fuel ratio gives the excess air: $AF_{th}=\dfrac{5(4.76)(29)}{44}=15.68$, so $25/15.68=1.6$: **160 % theoretical air**.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Balance">

$\mathrm{C_3H_8}+8(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\mathrm{N_2})\to3\mathrm{CO_2}+4\mathrm{H_2O}+3\mathrm{O_2}+30.08\mathrm{N_2}$, $N_P=40.08$ (your page: $30.8$, i.e. 30.08, as 8×3.76).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Dew point">

$P_v=\dfrac{4}{40.08}(101.3)=\mathbf{10.12}$ kPa (your page: 10.13), $T_{dp}=T_{sat}(10.12)=\mathbf{46.0}$ °C. Your Dec-9 page reads 46.1 °C ≈ 319 K, the Dec-16 page uses the 10 kPa table row (45.81 °C): the difference is a table-row rounding.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Heat transfer per kmol of fuel">

With the products at 318–320 K and the reactants at 25 °C, your Dec-9 slides page writes $-Q_{out}=3(-393\,520+10\,786-9364)+4(-241\,820+10\,639-9904)+3(9325-8682)+30(9306-8669)-(-103\,850)$ (the products' $\bar h$ are the 318 K table values) and obtains **$-Q_{out}=-2\,017\,510$ kJ/kmol**, that is $Q_{out}=2.0175\times10^{6}$ kJ/kmol $=\dfrac{2.0175\times10^{6}}{44.1}=\mathbf{45\,780}$ kJ/kg of fuel (recomputed: 2 018 510 kJ/kmol, 45 774 kJ/kg).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Re-solve of 16 Dec: the last line reads −2 297 xxx kJ/kmol (more than the fuel's HHV)">

The 16 Dec re-solve ends with a number that reads about "$-2\,297\,460$" (or $-2\,297\,160$) kJ/kmol, ticked in blue. It cannot be right: the **entire higher heating value** of propane is $2.22\times10^{6}$ kJ/kmol (50 350 kJ/kg × 44.1), and a furnace whose products are only cooled to 46 °C recovers *less* than the HHV: 2.02×10⁶. The earlier Dec-9 page ($-2\,017\,510$) and the recomputation (2.0185×10⁶) agree; the digits of the Dec-16 line are partly ambiguous in the scan, so this is listed in the errata as an **uncertain reading** with the correct value.

</Callout>

<Answer>

**$T_{dp}=46.0$ °C; $Q_{out}=2.018\times10^{6}$ kJ/kmol $=45\,770$ kJ/kg of propane** (Dec-9 page ✔: 2 017 510). The Dec-16 value ≈ 2.30×10⁶ is wrong.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="How much of the HHV a furnace can capture">

Cooling the products only to 46 °C recovers $2.018/2.220=91$ % of the HHV: the unrecovered 9 % is the latent heat of the 4 kmol of water (4×44 010 = 176 000 kJ/kmol) that is **still vapor** at the dew point. A condensing furnace goes below 46 °C and condenses it: that is the whole 91 %→ 100 % story.

</RealWorld>

<DewPointFigure Pv={10.12} label="15-59 products: 10.12 kPa" caption="15-59: Pv = 10.12 kPa gives T_dp = 46 °C." />

<Traps>

- The excess-air percentage comes from $AF/AF_{th}$ (1.60): not 25/15 or 25/17.
- Use the **vapor** $\bar h_f$ for the water ($-241\,820$): liquid just begins to form, so all the water is still vapor.
- Heat out per kg of fuel: divide per-kmol by 44.1.

</Traps>

<Problem id="15-68" title="Methane and oxygen burned in a rigid tank: final pressure and heat transfer" tags="closed system, constant volume, ideal gas, u = h − R_uT">

<Statement>

A rigid tank contains 120 g of methane and 600 g of O₂ at 25 °C and 200 kPa. The mixture is ignited, the methane burns completely, and the final temperature is 1200 K. Find (a) the final pressure and (b) the heat transfer.

</Statement>

<Think>

Constant volume, no work: $Q_{out}=U_R-U_P$ with $\bar u=\bar h-R_uT$ for every ideal gas. The pressure follows from the **mole count** of the products: $P_2=\dfrac{N_PR_uT_2}{V}$. The new points are the $-R_uT$ terms and the mole counts: $N_R=0.0075+0.01875$, $N_P=0.0075+0.015+0.00375$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Moles and balance">

$N_{CH_4}=\dfrac{0.12}{16}=0.0075$ kmol, $N_{O_2}=\dfrac{0.6}{32}=0.01875$ kmol: $0.0075\mathrm{CH_4}+0.01875\mathrm{O_2}\to0.0075\mathrm{CO_2}+0.015\mathrm{H_2O}+0.00375\mathrm{O_2}$ (your page ✔: oxygen in excess by 0.00375).

</Step>
<Step title="2. (a) Volume and final pressure">

$V=\dfrac{N_RR_uT_1}{P_1}=\dfrac{0.02625(8.314)(298)}{200}=\mathbf{0.325}$ m³ (your page ✔), $P_2=\dfrac{N_PR_uT_2}{V}=\dfrac{0.02625(8.314)(1200)}{0.3253}=\mathbf{805}$ kPa (your page: 805.82 ✔; recomputed 805.0).

</Step>
<Step title="3. (b) Heat transfer">

$-Q_{out}=\sum N_P(\bar h_f^\circ+\bar h-\bar h^\circ-R_uT_2)-\sum N_R(\bar h_f^\circ-R_uT_1)$ (your page: "closed system", $-R_uT$). Products at 1200 K: <T>CO₂ 53 848, H₂O 44 380, O₂ 38 447 kJ/kmol (A-20, A-23, A-19)</T>; the energy balance gives **$Q_{out}=5251$ kJ** (recomputed).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="note" title="Check your page: 5951 or 5251 in the v3 re-solve">

The v3 copy ends "Q = 5 9 5 1 kJ", with the digits for 2 and 9 written with the same stroke. The recomputation gives 5251 kJ; 5951 would be 13 % higher. The value is listed in the errata as an uncertain reading.

</Callout>

<Answer>

(a) **$P_2=805$ kPa**, (b) **$Q_{out}=5251$ kJ** (your v2 page for P₂ ✔; the v3 heat reads 5951 or 5251: **Check your page**).

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="A confined methane explosion">

Pressure rising from 200 to 805 kPa (×4) in a closed vessel is why gas explosions burst weak enclosures: the temperature ratio 1200/298 = 4.0 drives it (the mole count is unchanged). A real stoichiometric methane–air deflagration reaches about 8 bar (gauge) from atmospheric pressure, the figure vented-explosion design uses.

</RealWorld>

<Traps>

- Closed rigid system: **subtract $R_uT$** from every $\bar h$ (reactants at 298 K, products at 1200 K).
- The mole count is not conserved in general: here it is (3 kmol → 3 kmol for CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O), which is why $P_2/P_1=T_2/T_1$.

</Traps>
