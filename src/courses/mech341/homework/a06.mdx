<Callout kind="note" title="What this assignment teaches, in one paragraph">

(1) A mixture is described by **mole fractions** for volume, pressure and $M_m$, and by **mass fractions** for $c_p$, $c_v$ and $R$: the two bases are linked by $y_i=\dfrac{mf_i/M_i}{\sum mf_j/M_j}$. (2) Dalton and Amagat give the same result for ideal gases: $P_i=y_iP_m$ and $V_i=y_iV_m$. (3) A closed mixing process with no constant pressure is solved with **internal energy**: $Q=\sum m_ic_{v,i}\Delta T$, never $c_p$. (4) Every entropy term of a mixture uses the **partial pressure**: $\Delta s_i=c_{p,i}\ln\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}-R_i\ln\dfrac{y_iP_{m,2}}{P_{i,1}}$. (5) Departure charts (13-72) enter mixtures through Amagat's law and Kay's rule: each component gets its own $Z_h$ at the **mixture** temperature and pressure.

</Callout>

<Statement>

</Statement>

<Think>

</Think>

</Step>
</Steps>

<Problem id="13-56" title="Oxygen and nitrogen mixed in an insulated and an uninsulated tank: pressure, heat, entropy generation" tags="mixing, closed system, Q = ΔU, entropy generation, partial pressure">

</Plan>

<Step title="3. Work and heat">

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Pipeline compressor stations">

Natural-gas pipelines recompress every 100–200 km. The isothermal limit $RT\ln r_p$ is the minimum work; real stations use intercooling to approach it. For a mixture that is 20 % heavier hydrocarbons the specific work per kg is **lower** than for pure methane ($R=0.52$ kJ/kg·K).

</RealWorld>

<Problem id="13-62" title="Equimolar helium–argon turbine: isentropic work per kg" tags="mixture k, isentropic expansion, turbine work">

<Statement>

An equimolar mixture of helium and argon is the working fluid of a closed gas-turbine cycle. It enters the turbine at 2.5 MPa and 1300 K and expands isentropically to 200 kPa. Find the turbine work per unit mass.

</Statement>

<Think>

An isentropic expansion of a (constant-$c_p$) ideal gas needs the mixture's $k=c_p/c_v$, so first the mixture's $c_p$ and $R$. New here: the equimolar basis → mass fractions (He 2/22, Ar 20/22 on a 1 kmol basis) → $c_p=\sum mf_ic_{p,i}$, $R=R_u/M_m$, $c_v=c_p-R$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Mixture properties">

On 1 kmol: $m_{He}=0.5(4)=2$ kg, $m_{Ar}=0.5(40)=20$ kg, $M_m=22$. $c_p=\dfrac{2}{22}(5.1926)+\dfrac{20}{22}(0.5203)=\mathbf{0.9450}$ kJ/kg·K, $R=\dfrac{8.314}{22}=0.3779$, $c_v=c_p-R=0.5671$, $k=\dfrac{0.9450}{0.5671}=\mathbf{1.667}$ (your pages ✔: $c_p=0.9450$, $R=0.3779$, $c_v=0.5671$, $k=1.67$).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Exit temperature">

</Step>
<Step title="3. Work">

$w=c_p(T_1-T_2)=0.9450(1300-473.2)=\mathbf{781.3}$ kJ/kg (recomputed with 473.4: **781.9** kJ/kg).

</Step>
</Steps>

<RealWorld title="Closed-cycle helium–argon turbines">

Closed-loop Brayton cycles for high-temperature reactors and space power use He–Ar mixtures because a mixture with $M_m\approx22$–40 gives a blade-friendly speed of sound: pure helium (M = 4) would need many more compressor stages for the same pressure ratio, pure argon (M = 40) too few. A 20 K change in $T_2$ is a 2 % change in the work: precision in $k$ matters.

</RealWorld>

<Problem id="13-72" title="Hydrogen–nitrogen heated at constant pressure: ideal gas versus Amagat and departure charts" tags="Amagat, Kay, departure chart, enthalpy change">

<Statement>

A piston–cylinder device holds 6 kg of H₂ and 21 kg of N₂ at 160 K and 5 MPa. Heat is added at constant pressure until the temperature is 200 K. Find the heat transfer treating the mixture (a) as an ideal gas and (b) as a non-ideal gas by Amagat's law. (Printed: 4273 kJ and 4745 kJ.)

</Statement>

<Think>

Constant pressure: $Q=\Delta H$. (a) Ideal gas: $\Delta H=\sum N_i\Delta\bar h_i$ with the molar enthalpy table. (b) Amagat's law: **add each component's real enthalpy change**, each with its own reduced state at the **mixture** $T$ and $P$: $\bar h_2-\bar h_1=(\bar h_2-\bar h_1)_{ideal}-R_uT_{cr}(Z_{h2}-Z_{h1})$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Moles">

$N_{H_2}=6/2=3$ kmol, $N_{N_2}=21/28=0.75$ kmol.

</Step>
<Step title="2. (a) Ideal gas">

<T>A-22, A-18: h̄(H₂) 4535.4 → 5669.2; h̄(N₂) 4648 → 5810 kJ/kmol</T>. $Q=3(5669.2-4535.4)+0.75(5810-4648)=3(1133.8)+0.75(1162)=\mathbf{4273}$ kJ. On *Gen3ral revision* (p. 5) you did it with constant specific heats at room temperature, $21(1.039)(40)+6(14.307)(40)=\mathbf{4306}$ kJ: 0.8 % higher than the table value.

</Step>
<Step title="3. (b) Departure corrections at the mixture's T and P">

<T>Fig. A-29: H₂ T_R = 160/33.3 = 4.80, P_R = 5/1.30 = 3.85, Z_h ≈ 0 (both states); N₂ T_R = 160/126.2 = 1.27 (P_R = 5/3.39 = 1.47), Z_h1 = 1.3; at 200 K T_R = 1.58, Z_h2 = 0.7</T>. So H₂ is an ideal gas, and for N₂: $\Delta\bar h=1162+R_uT_{cr}(Z_{h1}-Z_{h2})=1162+8.314(126.2)(1.3-0.7)=\mathbf{1791.5}$ kJ/kmol. $Q=3(1133.8)+0.75(1791.5)=\mathbf{4745}$ kJ (the solution you pasted into your notes).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) **$Q=4273$ kJ** (table; 4306 with constant $c_p$), (b) **$Q=4745$ kJ** from chart readings; the Lee–Kesler functions ($Z_{h1}=1.18$, $Z_{h2}=0.67$) give 4672 kJ. Your page ✔ for (a); for (b) you pasted the manual's solution.

</Answer>

<RealWorld title="Cryogenic hydrogen and nitrogen">

At 160 K and 5 MPa nitrogen is near its critical point (126 K, 3.4 MPa), so the departure from ideal behavior adds **40 %** to its enthalpy change. The same effect makes heat exchangers in air separation and LNG plants be sized with real-gas models, not ideal ones.

</RealWorld>

- $R_u$ (not $R$) with $\bar h$ in kJ/kmol; the units must match.
- Hydrogen at $T_R=4.8$: the correction is zero, so only the nitrogen term changes.

</Traps>
