<Problem id="HW10 Q1" title="PI control of 400/[s(s+40)], K_I = 1: choose K_P for about 20 % overshoot; settling time" tags="PI, closed-loop zero, root locus">

<Statement>

Negative unity feedback, process $\dfrac{400}{s(s+40)}$, PI controller $G_c=K_P+K_I/s$. (a) Set $K_I=1$ and find $K_P$ for about 20% overshoot. (b) Approximate 2% settling time?

</Statement>

<Think>

Put the unknown $K_P$ where the root locus wants it: as a **gain in front of a fraction** $1+K\dfrac{N}{D}=0$. Then pick the point on the locus where the dominant pair gives roughly the wanted overshoot, **but check the zero**: the closed-loop transfer function has a zero at $-K_I/K_P$, and a zero near the poles inflates the overshoot.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Characteristic equation">

$1+\Big(K_P+\dfrac1s\Big)\dfrac{400}{s(s+40)}=0\Rightarrow s^2(s+40)+400K_Ps+400=0\Rightarrow s^3+40s^2+400K_Ps+400=0.$

</Step>
<Step title="2. Standard form for the root locus in K_P">

Move the $K_P$ term out: $1+K_P\dfrac{400s}{s^3+40s^2+400}=0$ (open-loop zero at the origin, poles from $s^3+40s^2+400$).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Which K_P?">

20% overshoot needs $\zeta=0.45$ for a pure second-order system; because of the closed-loop zero a point slightly inside that ray is chosen. Take $K_P=0.5$: $s^3+40s^2+200s+400=0$ has roots $-34.55$ and $-2.73\pm j2.04$ ✔.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Check by simulation">

Step response: **OS 20.7%**, 2% settling **1.52 s** ✔ (the exact $K_P$ for 20.0% overshoot is 0.515, poles $-34.3$, $-2.83\pm j1.90$).

</Step>
<Step title="5. Settling time">

$t_s\approx4/\sigma=4/2.73=1.47$ s, using the real part $\sigma=2.73$ of the dominant pair (not $4/(\zeta\omega_n)$ with $\zeta=0.45$).

</Step>
<Step title="6. A sweep (how K_P moves things)">

$K_P=0.6$: OS 16.5%; $0.8$: 11%; $1.0$: 8% (real poles $-24,-14.8,-1.12$, slowed by the pole near the zero); $1.5$: 5.4% (poles $-19.7\pm j13.7$, $-0.70$).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$K_P\approx0.5$ (0.515 exact); $t_s(2\%)\approx1.5$ s.

*Real-world:* a motor loop where the integral action removes offset and its zero is the price.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="Why a pair with ζ = 0.8 gives 20 % overshoot">

The poles $-2.73\pm j2.04$ have $\zeta=0.80$, which alone gives only about 1.5% overshoot. But the closed-loop transfer function has a **zero at $s=-K_I/K_P=-2$**, almost on top of the pole pair. A zero near the poles adds a derivative-like component to the response and inflates the overshoot to 20%. **When you choose poles for an overshoot, always check where the zeros lie.**

</Callout>

<Traps>

- Using the pole damping only; the PI zero matters.
- The parameter appears in the $s$-term, not as a simple gain: rewrite the characteristic equation to put it in standard form.
- $t_s=4/(\zeta\omega_n)$ with $\zeta=0.45$ would be wrong: use $\sigma=2.73$.

</Traps>

<Problem id="HW10 Q3" title="Lead-lag compensator for 100/[s(s+5)(s+10)]: K_v = 100, GM ≥ 10 dB, PM ≥ 40°" tags="lead-lag, K_v, missing gain">

<Statement>

Chemical-process plant $G=\dfrac{100}{s(s+5)(s+10)}$; small ramp error means $K_v=100$; margins $GM\ge10$ dB, $PM\ge40^\circ$. Find a lead-lag compensator (unity feedback) and draw the canonical block diagram.

</Statement>

<Think>

Look first at what $K_v$ the plant has: $K_v=\dfrac{100}{5\cdot10}=2$. So the loop needs **an extra gain of 50** just to meet the accuracy spec, and with that gain the loop is **unstable**. The lag part cuts the gain at crossover (while keeping it large at low frequency), and the lead part restores phase.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. The accuracy fixes the gain">

$K_v=\lim_{s\to0}sG_cG=100\Rightarrow$ with $G_c(0)=K$ we need $K\cdot2=100\Rightarrow K=50$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. The uncompensated loop at K = 50">

$50G$ is **unstable** (PM $-40.5^\circ$ at 15.9 rad/s, GM $-16.5$ dB): the compensator must provide both gain reduction at crossover and phase.

</Step>
<Step title="3. The manual's compensator">

$$G_c(s)=\frac{(s+2.5)(s+0.9871)}{(s+36.54)(s+0.0675)}$$

Lead: zero 2.5, pole 36.54. Lag: zero 0.9871, pole 0.0675 (ratios both 14.6). Dorf reports GM 12.35 dB, PM 41.8°, $K_v=100$.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Why it works (hand check at ω = 9 rad/s)">

Lead: $\tan^{-1}\dfrac{9}{2.5}-\tan^{-1}\dfrac{9}{36.54}=74.5^\circ-13.8^\circ=+60.7^\circ$. Lag: $\tan^{-1}\dfrac{9}{0.987}-\tan^{-1}\dfrac{9}{0.0675}=83.7^\circ-89.6^\circ=-5.9^\circ$. Net $+54.8^\circ$. Plant (with the 50): $-90^\circ-\tan^{-1}\dfrac95-\tan^{-1}\dfrac9{10}=-90^\circ-61^\circ-42^\circ=-193^\circ$. Loop phase $=-138^\circ\Rightarrow PM\approx42^\circ$ ✔. The lag has gain about 1 at $\omega=9$ but 14.6 at low frequency, so it cuts the crossover gain while keeping $K_v$; the lead pole at 36.5 limits the noise gain.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Recipe to reproduce the design">

(1) $K=50$ for $K_v$. (2) Read the phase of $50G$ at the desired crossover ($\approx9$ rad/s): $-193^\circ$, so about $+55^\circ$ is needed (40° + 15° safety). (3) Lead $\alpha=\dfrac{1-\sin55^\circ}{1+\sin55^\circ}\approx0.1$, or split the phase between lead ($\approx60^\circ$) and lag ($-6^\circ$). (4) Lag zero and pole a decade or more below the crossover (0.987 and 0.0675). (5) Check the margins.

</Step>
<Step title="6. Block diagram">

$R\to\oplus\to[50]\to\Big[\dfrac{s+2.5}{s+36.54}\Big]\to\Big[\dfrac{s+0.9871}{s+0.0675}\Big]\to\Big[\dfrac{100}{s(s+5)(s+10)}\Big]\to Y$, unity feedback.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$G_c(s)=50\dfrac{(s+2.5)(s+0.9871)}{(s+36.54)(s+0.0675)}$: $K_v=100$, $GM=12.35$ dB, $PM=41.8^\circ$ ($\omega_{gc}=9.01$, $\omega_{pc}=20.87$ rad/s).

*Real-world:* a chemical-process loop where the operator wants tight ramp tracking yet comfortable stability margins: a lag-lead is the classical compromise.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Official (Dorf AP10.9) error: the printed compensator is missing a gain of 50">

With $G_c$ exactly as printed (DC gain 1.0005) the loop has **$K_v=2.0$, $PM=32.6^\circ$, $GM=46.3$ dB**: it does not meet the specs. With the missing **factor 50** the numbers are exactly Dorf's ($\omega_{gc}=9.01$, $PM=41.8^\circ$, $\omega_{pc}=20.87$, $GM=12.35$ dB, $K_v=100.05$). Either the compensator should include the gain 50 or the manual's plant is $5000/[s(s+5)(s+10)]$.

</Callout>

<Traps>

- $K_v$ is a property of the **whole loop**: scale the compensator DC gain accordingly (the printed one forgot).
- Always compute the margins of the compensated loop; never trust a printed result.
- A lag-lead has two corner pairs; the lag pole must be well below the crossover.

</Traps>

<Source>Dorf AP10.9 ✘ as printed; ✔ with $K=50$ (digest recomputation). No Anthony version.</Source>

<Problem id="HW10 Q5" title="Nyquist plots and maximum K for K/[s(s²+s+6)] and K(s+1)/[s²(s²+6)]" tags="Nyquist, wrong L(s) in manual">

<Statement>

Nyquist plots and stability of (a) $L=\dfrac{K}{s(s^2+s+6)}$ and (b) $L=\dfrac{K(s+1)}{s^2(s^2+6)}$ (the same as <Hw n="8">HW8 A3</Hw>).

</Statement>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) Manual (Dorf P9.2) and the check">

Real-axis crossing at $\omega=\sqrt6$ where $\mathrm{Re}=-K/6$: stable for $K<6$ ✔ (at $K=6$ the poles are $\pm j\sqrt6$ and $-1$).

</Step>
<Step title="(b) The sheet's L(s)">

Closed-loop polynomial $s^4+6s^2+Ks+K$ has no $s^3$ term, so it is **unstable for every $K$** ($N=2$, $Z=2$; for $K=1$ the roots are $0.088\pm j2.42$ and $-0.088\pm j0.40$; the roots sum to zero because there is no $s^3$ term ✔).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) stable for $K<6$. (b) unstable for all $K$ (for the sheet's $L$).

</Answer>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ The manual's (b) answers a different L(s)">

Dorf prints $L=\dfrac{K(s+1)}{s^2(s+6)}$ and concludes "the polar plot never encircles $-1$, so the system is stable for all $K$". HW10 and HW8-2024 print $L=\dfrac{K(s+1)}{s^2(s^2+6)}$ (a lightly damped pair at $\pm j\sqrt6$), which is **unstable for every $K$**. The handwritten HW8 solution handles the sheet's version correctly. Use the textbook's "stable for all $K$" only for $L=K(s+1)/[s^2(s+6)]$. Other manual pages in the file: P9.1, P9.3, P9.4.

</Callout>

<Source>Dorf P9.2 (a) ✔; (b) ✔ for the sheet's $L$ by root computation, ✘ for the printed conclusion.</Source>
