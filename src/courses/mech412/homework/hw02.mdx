<Problem id="HW2 Q1" title="Second-order system with an extra zero: step response and overshoot" tags="zero, step response, overshoot">

<Statement>

$H(s)=\dfrac{\omega_n^2(s+z)}{z\,(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)}$. (a) Show the unit-step response has the form $y(t)=1\pm Ce^{-\sigma t}\cos(\omega_dt+\beta)$ with $C=\dfrac{\sqrt{1-2\zeta\omega_n/z+\omega_n^2/z^2}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ and $\beta=\tan^{-1}\dfrac{-\zeta+\omega_n/z}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$. (b) Derive the overshoot $M_p$. (c) Given $M_p$, how are $\zeta,\omega_n$ found?

</Statement>

<Think>

A zero multiplies by $(s+z)/z=1+s/z$, and multiplying a transform by $s$ **differentiates** the time response. So the response is *the plain 2nd-order step response plus $1/z$ times its derivative*. The common student error is to differentiate the **impulse** response (the transfer function itself) instead of the **step** response — then the "1 −" and the cosine form never appear.

</Think>

<Plan>

$y=y_1+\dfrac1z\dot y_1$ where $y_1$ is the step response of $\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$ → combine sine and cosine into one cosine → set $\dot y=0$ for the peak.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Split the transfer function">

$H=\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}+\dfrac1z\,\dfrac{s\,\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$. With $Y=H/s$ the second term is $s\times$(first term's step transform), i.e. the *derivative* of the first step response.

</Step>
<Step title="2. The standard step response">

$y_1=1-\dfrac{e^{-\sigma t}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\cos(\omega_dt-\beta_0)$, $\tan\beta_0=\dfrac\zeta{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$, so $\cos\beta_0=\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, $\sin\beta_0=\zeta$. Its derivative: $\dot y_1=\dfrac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}e^{-\sigma t}\sin\omega_dt$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Combine">

$y=1-\dfrac{e^{-\sigma t}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\Big[\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\cos\omega_dt+\big(\zeta-\tfrac{\omega_n}z\big)\sin\omega_dt\Big]$. The bracket has amplitude $M=\sqrt{1-2\zeta\omega_n/z+\omega_n^2/z^2}$, so

$$y(t)=1-C\,e^{-\sigma t}\cos(\omega_dt+\beta),\qquad C=\frac M{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}},$$

which is the printed form **with a minus sign in front of $C$**.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Peak time">

$\dot y=0$ first at $\omega_dt_p=\pi-\operatorname{atan2}\big(\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2},\,z-\zeta\omega_n\big)$.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Overshoot">

At $t_p$, $\cos(\omega_dt_p+\beta)=-\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, so

$$M_p=C\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\,e^{-\sigma t_p}=\frac1z\sqrt{z^2-2\zeta\omega_nz+\omega_n^2}\;e^{-\sigma t_p}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="6. (c) Given M_p">

$M_p$ depends only on $\zeta$ and $\omega_n/z$. One spec does not fix both $\zeta$ and $\omega_n$: with $z$ known, a second spec (peak time, settling time) is needed and the two equations are solved numerically. As $z\to\infty$: $M_p=e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ (the standard result). A left-half-plane zero always **increases overshoot** and shortens the rise.

</Step>
<Step title="7. Numerical check">

$\zeta=0.5$, $\omega_n=1$, $z=2$: the simulation peaks at $t\approx3.0$ s with $M_p=0.191$; the formulas give $t_p=3.023$ s, $M_p=0.1910$ ✔. (Without the zero the overshoot is 16.3 %: the zero pushes it to 19.1 %.)

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$y=1-Ce^{-\sigma t}\cos(\omega_dt+\beta)$; $t_p=\dfrac{\pi-\operatorname{atan2}(\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2},z-\zeta\omega_n)}{\omega_d}$; $M_p=\dfrac1z\sqrt{z^2-2\zeta\omega_nz+\omega_n^2}\,e^{-\sigma t_p}$.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Errors in the printed statement and the official solution">

- The printed statement has "$1+Ce^{-\sigma t}\cos(\cdot)$": that gives $y(0)=2$, impossible. It must be $1-Ce^{-\sigma t}\cos(\cdot)$ (then $y(0)=0$, and $y(2)=1.059$ matches the simulation).
- The official final $y(t)$ has the wrong sign and is missing a factor $z$ (dimensional mismatch).
- The official $t_p=\tfrac1{\omega_d}\big[\tan^{-1}\tfrac{z-\zeta\omega_n}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}+\tfrac32\pi\big]$ gives 6.65 s for the example: one half-period late (it lands on the next minimum). The official $M_p$ formula has a missing factor 2 ($z^2-z\zeta\omega_n+\omega_n^2$ instead of $z^2-2\zeta\omega_nz+\omega_n^2$).
- **Anthony:** treated $F(s)=\omega_n^2/(\cdots)$ as the step response; its inverse $f=\tfrac{\omega_d}{1-\zeta^2}e^{-\sigma t}\sin\omega_dt$ is the *impulse* response. He also wrote OS $=100e^{+\pi\sigma/\omega_d}$ (missing minus sign), valid only without the zero.

</Callout>

<Traps>

- Differentiate the *step* response, not the transfer function.
- $M_p$ is measured against the final value $H(0)=1$.
- When $z\gg\omega_n$ the zero hardly matters.

</Traps>

<Source>official solution (Wang, problem 4) with the corrections above; digest-author derivation checked with the step-response simulator.</Source>

</Problem>

<Problem id="HW2 Q2" title="Pinned bar driven through a slider by a spring and a dashpot" tags="rotation, small angle, 2nd-order TF with s in the numerator">

<Statement>

A uniform bar (mass $m=3$ kg, length $L=0.1$ m) rotates frictionlessly about a pin at its right end. A frictionless slider in a slot, at distance $L$ from the pin, links it to a spring $k=12$ kN/m (to the ground) and a dashpot $b=400$ N·s/m whose upper end is the input $x$ (a 1 mm step). Small oscillations. Find (a) the EOM, (b) the Laplace form, (c) $\omega_n,\zeta$, the final value, (d) the step response.

</Statement>

<Think>

Rotation about a fixed pin ⇒ **moments**. The spring and dashpot act at the slider, an arm $L$ from the pin, so their coefficients get multiplied by $L^2$. The dashpot sees the **relative** velocity of its two ends.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Kinematics and forces (down positive)">

Slider displacement $y=L\theta$. Force on the bar at the slider: spring $-kL\theta$; dashpot $b(\dot x-L\dot\theta)$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Moments about the pin">

$I\ddot\theta=L\big[b(\dot x-L\dot\theta)-kL\theta\big]$, with $I=\tfrac13mL^2=3(0.1)^2/3=0.01$ kg·m². So

$$\frac{mL^2}3\ddot\theta+bL^2\dot\theta+kL^2\theta=bL\dot x .$$

</Step>
<Step title="3. Numbers and transfer function">

$0.01\ddot\theta+4\dot\theta+120\theta=40\dot x$ ⇒ $\dfrac\Theta X=\dfrac{40s}{0.01s^2+4s+120}=\dfrac{4000\,s}{s^2+400s+12000}$.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Specs">

$\omega_n^2=12000\Rightarrow\omega_n=109.5$ rad/s (17.4 Hz); $2\zeta\omega_n=400\Rightarrow\zeta=1.83$ (**overdamped**). Poles $-200\pm20\sqrt{70}=-32.67$ and $-367.3$.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Step of 1 mm">

$X=0.001/s$, so $\Theta=\dfrac{4}{s^2+400s+12000}=\dfrac{4}{(s+32.67)(s+367.3)}$. Residues: $\theta(t)=0.01195\big(e^{-32.67t}-e^{-367.3t}\big)$ rad.

</Step>
<Step title="6. Final value and peak">

$\lim_{s\to0}s\Theta=0$ (the numerator has a zero at $s=0$). Peak at $t=\ln(367.3/32.67)/334.7=7.2$ ms, $\theta_{max}=8.6$ mrad $=0.49^\circ$. Decays in about $0.15$ s (time constant 31 ms).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

EOM $\dfrac{mL^2}3\ddot\theta+bL^2\dot\theta+kL^2\theta=bL\dot x$; $\dfrac\Theta X=\dfrac{4000s}{s^2+400s+12000}$; $\omega_n=109.5$ rad/s, $\zeta=1.83$, $\theta(\infty)=0$, $\theta(t)=0.01195(e^{-32.67t}-e^{-367.3t})$ rad.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- The input is the *position* of the damper top, so the TF has an $s$ in the numerator: the final value is **0**, not a multiple of $x$.
- Units of $x$ (mm vs m) and **rad/s vs Hz**: Anthony wrote "hz" for $\omega_n$ and plotted the 1 **m** step (peak 8.6 rad instead of 8.6 mrad).
- *Physical:* the damper can shove the bar for a moment but cannot hold it: a high-pass behaviour.

</Traps>

<Source>no official solution (the file does not contain it). Digest-author solution, poles verified with the library. Anthony's EOM and TF are right; his tablet version uses the energy method $T=\tfrac12I\dot\theta^2$, $V=\tfrac12kL^2\theta^2$.</Source>

</Problem>

<Problem id="HW2 Q3" title="Disk on a cable with a spring and a torsional damper" tags="rolling without slipping, instantaneous centre, factor 2">

<Statement>

A cable is fixed to a wall at B, wrapped around a homogeneous disk (mass $m=50$ kg, radius $R=0.6$ m) and leaves horizontally at A, where $F(t)=200\,u(t)$ N pulls. A spring $k=250$ N/m joins the disk centre O to the ground. The disk does not slip on the cable; torsional damping $c_t=18$ N·m·s. Start from rest. (a) EOM, (b) coefficients of the equation, (c) poles and zeros, (d) final position of the wheel.

</Statement>

<Think>

The bottom contact point C is an *instantaneous centre of zero velocity*: $v_O=R\omega$ and the top point (where the cable leaves) moves at $2v_O$. So the force $F$ at the top does work at twice the rate of $F$ on the centre: effectively a force $2F$ on the centre, and the disk's effective inertia about C is $\tfrac32mR^2$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Equation of motion">

Newton for the centre plus moments about O (or energy): $T=\tfrac34m\dot x^2$ (kinetic energy about C), dissipation $D=\tfrac12c_t\dot x^2/R^2$, $V=\tfrac12kx^2$, generalised force $2F$ (point A moves twice as far as O):

$$\tfrac{3m}2\ddot x+\frac{c_t}{R^2}\dot x+kx=2F .$$

</Step>
<Step title="2. Numbers">

$75\ddot x+50\dot x+250x=400\,u(t)$; in $\theta$ ($x=R\theta$): $27\ddot\theta+18\dot\theta+90\theta=240u$, i.e. $\ddot\theta+\tfrac23\dot\theta+\tfrac{10}3\theta=\tfrac{80}9u$. Transfer function $\dfrac XF=\dfrac2{75s^2+50s+250}$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Poles and zeros">

$s^2+\tfrac23s+\tfrac{10}3=0\Rightarrow s=-\tfrac13\pm j\tfrac{\sqrt{29}}3=-0.333\pm j1.795$. **No finite zeros.** $\omega_n=1.826$ rad/s, $\zeta=0.183$ (lightly damped: overshoot 55.8 %).

</Step>
<Step title="4. Final position">

At rest the damper carries no force: $kx=2F\Rightarrow x(\infty)=\dfrac{400}{250}=1.6$ m (simulation: final 1.60, peak 2.49 m, ringing at 1.8 rad/s).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$75\ddot x+50\dot x+250x=400u$; poles $-0.333\pm j1.795$, no zeros; $\omega_n=1.826$, $\zeta=0.183$; $x(\infty)=1.6$ m.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- **The factor 2**: Anthony's first version used $F=kx$ and got $x=0.8$ m; his second version ($2F=kx$, $x=1.6$ m) is right.
- The damping is *torsional*: the equivalent translational damping is $c_t/R^2=50$ N·s/m, not 18.
- *Real-world:* a winch: a 200 N pull stretches the spring 1.6 m but the light damping lets it overshoot to about 2.5 m and ring with a 3.5 s period.

</Traps>

<Source>no official solution; digest-author solution (Newton + Lagrange agree), verified with the library. Anthony: version a ✘ (0.8 m), version b ✔.</Source>

</Problem>
