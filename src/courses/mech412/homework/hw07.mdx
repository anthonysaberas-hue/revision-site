<Problem id="HW7 Q1" title="Routh: how many roots in the right half-plane?" tags="Routh array, sign changes">

<Statement>

(a) $s^4+8s^3+32s^2+80s+100=0$; (b) $s^4+2s^3+7s^2-2s+8=0$. How many roots have positive real part?

</Statement>

<Think>

Routh's array counts right-half-plane roots **without solving**: build the table from the coefficients, then count **sign changes in the first column**. Each sign change = one root in the RHP.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) Build the first two rows">

Row $s^4$: coefficients of $s^4,s^2,s^0$: $1,\;32,\;100$. Row $s^3$: coefficients of $s^3,s^1$: $8,\;80$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) Each next entry = (last pivot × entry above-right − pivot above × entry right) ÷ last pivot">

Row $s^2$: $\dfrac{8\cdot32-1\cdot80}{8}=\dfrac{176}{8}=22$ and $\dfrac{8\cdot100-1\cdot0}{8}=100$.

Row $s^1$: $\dfrac{22\cdot80-8\cdot100}{22}=\dfrac{1760-800}{22}=\dfrac{480}{11}=43.6$.

Row $s^0$: $100$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) Read the first column">

$1,\;8,\;22,\;43.6,\;100$: all positive, **no sign change, 0 roots in the RHP: stable.** Numeric check: the roots are $-1\pm3j$ and $-3\pm1j$ ✔.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Build the table">

Row $s^4$: $1,\;7,\;8$. Row $s^3$: $2,\;-2$ (then pad with 0). Row $s^2$: $\dfrac{2\cdot7-1\cdot(-2)}{2}=8$ and $\dfrac{2\cdot8-0}{2}=8$. Row $s^1$: $\dfrac{8\cdot(-2)-2\cdot8}{8}=\dfrac{-32}{8}=-4$. Row $s^0$: $8$.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Read the first column">

$1,\;2,\;8,\;-4,\;8$: signs $+,+,+,-,+$, **two sign changes (+ to −, then − to +), so 2 roots in the RHP.** Numeric check: roots $0.314\pm0.977j$ (RHP) and $-1.314\pm2.423j$ ✔.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) 0 roots in the RHP (stable). (b) 2 roots in the RHP (unstable).

*Real-world:* (a) is a well-damped fourth-order system; (b) is an oscillation growing like $e^{0.31t}$.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Missing coefficient: in (b) the third column of the $s^3$ row is padded with 0.
- Count **sign changes** (both + to − and − to +), here two.

</Traps>

<Problem id="HW7 Q2" title="Range of k for stability of s⁵+5s⁴+10s³+10s²+5s+k" tags="Routh with a parameter">

<Statement>

Find all $k$ for which every root of $s^5+5s^4+10s^3+10s^2+5s+k=0$ lies in the open left half-plane.

</Statement>

<Think>

Routh with a symbol in it: build the table, then demand that **every first-column entry is positive**, because any sign change means an unstable root. Each entry gives an inequality in $k$; the answer is their intersection.

</Think>

<Plan>

Table: rows $s^5\ldots s^0$; entries that contain $k$ give the conditions.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. First two rows">

$s^5$: $1,\;10,\;5$. $s^4$: $5,\;10,\;k$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Row s³">

$\dfrac{5\cdot10-1\cdot10}{5}=8$ and $\dfrac{5\cdot5-1\cdot k}{5}=\dfrac{25-k}{5}$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Row s²">

$\dfrac{8\cdot10-5\cdot\frac{25-k}{5}}{8}=\dfrac{80-25+k}{8}=\dfrac{55+k}{8}$ and $\dfrac{8\cdot k-5\cdot0}{8}=k$.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Row s¹">

$$\frac{\frac{55+k}{8}\cdot\frac{25-k}{5}-8k}{\frac{55+k}{8}}=\frac{(55+k)(25-k)-320k}{5(55+k)}=\frac{1375-30k-k^2-320k}{5(55+k)}=\frac{1375-350k-k^2}{5(55+k)}.$$

(Multiply numerator and denominator by $40$ to clear the fractions: $(55+k)(25-k)=1375-30k-k^2$, and $8k\cdot40=320k$.)

</Step>
<Step title="5. Row s⁰">

$k$.

</Step>
<Step title="6. Conditions">

First column: $1,\;5,\;8,\;\dfrac{55+k}{8},\;\dfrac{1375-350k-k^2}{5(55+k)},\;k$. We need: $k>0$ (row $s^0$); $55+k>0$ (row $s^2$, automatic when $k>0$); and the $s^1$ entry $>0$. For $k>0$ the denominator is positive, so we need $1375-350k-k^2>0$, i.e. $k^2+350k-1375<0$, i.e. $k<-175+80\sqrt5=3.885$.

</Step>
<Step title="7. Numeric check">

Largest real part of any root: $-0.006$ at $k=3.8$, $\approx0$ at $k=3.885$, $+0.001$ at $k=3.9$ ✔. At $k=3.885$ the roots include a pure imaginary pair (marginal stability).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$0<k<-175+80\sqrt5\approx3.885$.

*Real-world:* $k$ is a loop gain: up to about 3.9 the loop is stable; above it one pair of poles crosses into the RHP and the system oscillates with growing amplitude.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Anthony's arithmetic slip">

His $s^1$ numerator reads $-k^2-350k+1325$; it should be $+1375$ ($25\cdot55=1375$). His final range "0 < k < 3.9" is right only because the slip is small (1325 would give 3.76). The solution file has the correct $K^2+350K-1375<0$ and the exact form $]0,\,-175+80\sqrt5[$.

</Callout>

<Traps>

- A single mis-multiplied constant shifts the bound: recheck $(55+k)(25-k)=1375-30k-k^2$.
- "Open left half-plane" excludes marginal stability, so the inequality is strict.

</Traps>

<Problem id="HW7 Q3" title="PI controller on 1/[s(s+15)(s+25)] for ζ = 0.707; t_p, t_s; steady-state errors" tags="pole placement, dominant poles, error constants">

<Statement>

Unity feedback, controller $K_p+K_I/s$, plant $1/[s(s+15)(s+25)]$. Design the gains so the dominant roots have $\zeta=0.707$. Find the peak time and 2% settling time for a step input. Calculate the position, velocity and acceleration errors.

</Statement>

<Think>

Think of the PI as "P gives speed, I removes steady-state error". Closing the loop gives a **fourth-order** characteristic polynomial; we want it to look like (slow real pole) × (fast real pole) × (a nice $\zeta=0.707$ pair). **Two unknowns ($K_p,K_I$) but only one requirement ($\zeta$)**: so we must add a design choice. Standard: put the PI zero close to the origin ($K_I/K_p$ small) so that it only removes the offset and barely touches the transient.

</Think>

<Plan>

Closed-loop polynomial, then match $(s+a)(s+b)(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)$, choose $K_p=10K_I$, solve, then time-response estimates and error constants.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Closed loop">

$G=\dfrac{K_ps+K_I}{s}\cdot\dfrac{1}{s(s+15)(s+25)}$. Since $s^2(s+15)(s+25)=s^2(s^2+40s+375)=s^4+40s^3+375s^2$:

$$T(s)=\frac{K_ps+K_I}{s^4+40s^3+375s^2+K_ps+K_I}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="2. Match coefficients">

$(s+a)(s+b)(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)$ gives, by powers of $s$:

- $s^3$: $a+b+2\zeta\omega_n=40$
- $s^2$: $\omega_n^2+2\zeta\omega_n(a+b)+ab=375$
- $s^1$: $\omega_n^2(a+b)+2\zeta\omega_nab=K_p$
- $s^0$: $\omega_n^2ab=K_I$

Four equations, five unknowns ($a,b,\omega_n,K_p,K_I$): exactly the missing choice.

</Step>
<Step title="3. The design choice">

Put the closed-loop PI zero at $-K_I/K_p=-0.1$ and let the slow pole $b\approx0.1$ sit right next to it (the pair almost **cancels**, so the transient is that of the remaining three poles). With $\zeta=0.707$, $2\zeta\omega_n=1.414\,\omega_n$.

From $s^3$: $a=39.9-1.414\omega_n$. Put in $s^2$ with $b=0.1$:
$$\omega_n^2+1.414\omega_n(40-1.414\omega_n)+0.1(39.9-1.414\omega_n)=375\;\Rightarrow\;-\omega_n^2+56.43\,\omega_n-371=0.$$
$\omega_n=\dfrac{56.43-\sqrt{56.43^2-4\cdot371}}{2}=\dfrac{56.43-41.2}{2}\approx7.6$ rad/s (the other root, 48.8, would make $a<0$). Then $a\approx29.15$.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Gains">

$K_I=\omega_n^2ab\approx172$ (with the exact $b=0.1023$) and $K_p=10K_I\approx1720$. Check by the $s^1$ equation: $57.8\cdot29.25+10.75\cdot2.98=1721$ ✔. **Closed-loop poles:** $-29.15,\;-5.37\pm j5.37,\;-0.102$ ✔ ($\zeta=5.37/7.6=0.707$).

</Step>
<Step title="5. Time response (dominant-pair estimate)">

$\sigma=\zeta\omega_n=5.37$, $\omega_d=5.37$: $t_p=\pi/\omega_d=0.585$ s; $t_s(2\%)=4/\sigma=0.745$ s; $OS=100e^{-\pi}=4.3\%$.

</Step>
<Step title="6. Time response (actual simulation)">

The pole at $-0.102$ and the third pole at $-29$ shift these slightly: **OS 6.4%, $t_p=0.63$ s, 2% settling 1.09 s.** The formulas are for a *pure* second-order system; always say they are estimates.

</Step>
<Step title="7. Steady-state errors">

The loop is **type 2** (the plant integrator + the PI integrator). Use the error constants of $G(s)=\dfrac{K_ps+K_I}{s^2(s+15)(s+25)}$:

- Position (step): $K_p^{err}=\infty\Rightarrow e_{ss}=0$.
- Velocity (ramp): $K_v=\lim sG=\infty\Rightarrow e_{ss}=0$.
- Acceleration (parabola $1/s^3$): $K_a=\lim s^2G=\dfrac{K_I}{15\cdot25}=\dfrac{K_I}{375}\Rightarrow e_{ss}=\dfrac1{K_a}=\dfrac{375}{K_I}=\dfrac{375}{172}=2.18$ (for the unit parabola $1/s^3$; for $r=\tfrac12t^2$ it is $1.09$).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

Design (one choice): $K_p\approx1720$, $K_I\approx172$ (PI zero at $-0.1$). $t_p\approx0.6$ s, $t_s\approx1.1$ s (dominant-pair formulas: 0.585 s, 0.745 s). $e_{position}=0$, $e_{velocity}=0$, $e_{acceleration}=375/K_I=2.18\neq0$.

*Real-world:* a servo whose plant is an integrator with fast poles at 15 and 25 rad/s. The PI guarantees zero offset; the acceleration error of 2.18 means a constant-acceleration command is followed with a constant lag, like cruise control tracking a steady acceleration.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Official solution error: acceleration error is NOT zero">

The solution file ends with "lim e = lim e_v = lim e_acc = 0". It defines $e_v=sE(s)$ and $e_{acc}=s^2E(s)$ for a **unit step** input; those are derivatives of the error signal, not the velocity and acceleration errors, and they vanish only because of the $s^2$ factor in $E$. With a parabola input a type-2 loop has a **finite, non-zero error** $375/K_I$. Jad's version repeats the same mistake; the typed classmate also says "= 0".

</Callout>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Other submissions">

Typed classmate: assumed $\omega_n=0.1$ rad/s, giving $K_p=52.6$, $K_I=3.69$, 21% overshoot and $t_s=49$ s: a valid $\zeta$ but an absurdly slow system. Anthony: wrote the correct coefficient matching and stopped (no extra condition, no numbers, no errors).

</Callout>

<Traps>

- The problem has a **free parameter**: state what you chose.
- Dominant-pole formulas assume a clean second-order response; the PI zero and extra poles move the real numbers.
- Only the parabola gives a finite non-zero error for a type-2 loop.

</Traps>

<Problem id="HW7 Q4" title="Paper machine e^{−2s}/(3s+1): Ziegler–Nichols PID, two methods" tags="Ziegler–Nichols, delay">

<Statement>

(a) Find the PID parameters with the Ziegler–Nichols **step-response** rules. (b) The system is marginally stable for $K_u=3.044$; find the PID parameters with the Z–N **ultimate-gain** rules.

</Statement>

<Think>

Z–N is a recipe tuned for plants that look like "delay + lag": the step response is an S-curve. Method 1 reads two numbers off the S-curve (delay $L$ and lag $T$). Method 2 needs the gain $K_u$ at which the loop just oscillates and the period $P_u$ of that oscillation. The delay $e^{-2s}$ is not a polynomial, so Routh cannot find $K_u$: that is why it is given.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) Step response">

$Y=\dfrac{e^{-2s}}{s(3s+1)}=\Big(\dfrac1s-\dfrac1{s+1/3}\Big)e^{-2s}\Rightarrow y(t)=\big[1-e^{-(t-2)/3}\big]H(t-2)$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) Tangent at the steepest point">

The slope is greatest when the rise starts: $y'(2^+)=1/3$. Tangent $y=(t-2)/3$ reaches the final value 1 at $t=5$. So **$L=2$ s, $T=5-2=3$ s**.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) Z–N method 1 rules">

$K_p=\dfrac{1.2T}{L}=1.8$, $T_i=2L=4$ s, $T_d=0.5L=1$ s. (Equivalent: $K_i=K_p/T_i=0.45$, $K_d=K_pT_d=1.8$.)

</Step>
<Step title="(b) The period (exact)">

Oscillation at $K_u$ means the loop phase is $-180^\circ$ at $\omega_u$: $-2\omega-\tan^{-1}(3\omega)=-\pi$, so $\omega_u=0.9535$ rad/s and $P_u=2\pi/\omega_u=6.59$ s. The exact ultimate gain is $|1+3j\omega_u|=3.030$ (the given 3.044 is 0.5% higher: read from a plot).

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Z–N method 2 rules">

$K_p=0.6K_u=0.6\cdot3.044=1.83$, $T_i=0.5P_u=3.29$ s, $T_d=0.125P_u=0.82$ s. (Reading $P_u\approx7$ s off the plot gives $T_i=3.5$, $T_d=0.875$: within 6%, accepted.)

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) $K_p=1.8$, $T_i=4$ s, $T_d=1$ s. (b) $K_p=1.83$, $T_i\approx3.3$–$3.5$ s, $T_d\approx0.82$–$0.875$ s (exact $P_u=6.59$ s).

*Real-world:* basis-weight control on a paper machine: stock changes reach the sheet only after the 2 s transport lag, so gains must be modest ($K_p\approx1.8$ against $K_u=3.04$).

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="Submissions">

Anthony and Jad get (a) right. For (b) the typed classmate wrote "I do not know how to get $P_u$" and stopped at $K_p=1.8264$: the period must be read from the oscillating step response, or computed from the phase condition as in step (b).

</Callout>

<Traps>

- A delay cannot go into Routh directly (not a polynomial).
- $L$ is the *apparent* delay; here it is exactly 2 s.
- $P_u$ is the period of the steady oscillation, not the peak time.

</Traps>

<Source>solution file ✔ ($P_u\approx7$ s); Anthony / Jad ✔; exact $P_u=6.59$ s by the digest.</Source>

<Problem id="HW7 Q5" title="Root locus: choose K for OS < 10 % and 2 % settling time < 4 s" tags="root locus, asymptotes, dominant poles">

<Statement>

Unity feedback; controller $K\dfrac{(s+2)^2}{(s+10)(s+20)}$; process $\dfrac{1}{s(s^2+3s+3.5)}$. Sketch the root locus and select $K$ so that the step response has $OS<10\%$ and $t_s(2\%)<4$ s.

</Statement>

<Think>

Translate the specs to the $s$-plane first: $OS<10\%\Rightarrow\zeta\ge0.59$; $t_s<4$ s $\Rightarrow\sigma\ge4/4=1$. Then find where the locus lives and pick $K$ where the **slowest** poles are inside that region. **There is no official solution for Q5**; everything is the digest author's, numerically verified.

</Think>

<Plan>

Poles and zeros, then real-axis segments, asymptotes, closed-loop polynomial, a table of poles against $K$, and the range of valid $K$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Poles and zeros">

Open-loop poles: $0$, $-1.5\pm j1.118$ (roots of $s^2+3s+3.5$), $-10$, $-20$. Zeros: a **double zero at $-2$**. So $n=5$, $m=2$, $n-m=3$ asymptotes.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Asymptotes">

Angles $\pm60^\circ$ and $180^\circ$; centroid $\sigma_a=\dfrac{\sum p-\sum z}{n-m}=\dfrac{-33-(-4)}{3}=-9.67$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Real-axis segments">

Count real poles and zeros to the right (the double zero counts twice): $(-2,0)$: 1, odd, **on the locus**; $(-10,-2)$: 3, **on**; $(-20,-10)$: 4, not; $(-\infty,-20)$: 5, **on**.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Closed-loop polynomial">

$s(s^2+3s+3.5)(s^2+30s+200)=s^5+33s^4+293.5s^3+705s^2+700s$; adding $K(s^2+4s+4)$:
$$s^5+33s^4+293.5s^3+(705+K)s^2+(700+4K)s+4K=0.$$
Note $(s+2)^2=s^2+4s+4$.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Closed-loop poles as K grows">

| K | closed-loop poles | OS | $t_s$ (2%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | −0.53, −1.44 ± j1.39, −9.1, −20.4 | ≈ 0% | 7.0 s ✘ |
| 200 | −0.88, −1.50 ± j1.74, −8.3, −20.8 | ≈ 0% | 3.91 s ✔ |
| 300 | −1.07, −1.67 ± j2.09, −7.4, −21.2 | ≈ 0% | 3.09 s |
| 460 | −1.24, −2.06 ± j2.71, −5.9, −21.7 | 7.3% | 2.50 s |
| 520 | −1.28, −2.23 ± j2.96, −5.4, −21.9 | 9.9% | 2.34 s |
| 600 | −1.32, −2.42 ± j3.40, −4.7, −22.1 | 13% ✘ | 2.15 s |

</Step>
<Step title="6. Reading the table">

The very slow real pole near the origin (it starts at the open-loop pole $s=0$ and slides left toward the double zero at $-2$) sets the **settling time**; the complex pair sets the **overshoot**. Both specs hold for about **$194\le K\le523$** ($t_s=4$ s at $K=194$; $OS=10\%$ at $K=523$).

</Step>
<Step title="7. A good choice">

$K\approx300$–$400$ ($OS\approx0$–$4.5\%$, $t_s\approx2.7$–$3.1$ s): comfortably inside the range.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$194\lesssim K\lesssim523$; for example $K=300$ to $460$.

*Real-world:* a lead-lag-style compensator on a plant with an integrator and a lightly damped mode ($-1.5\pm j1.1$): the controller zeros at $-2$ tame that sluggish pair while the controller poles at $-10,-20$ limit noise gain.

</Answer>
