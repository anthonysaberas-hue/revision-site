<Problem id="MT3 practice P1" title="Routh, error constants and margins on G = K/[s(s+1)(s+5)] (20 marks)" tags="Routh range, K_v, accuracy vs stability, margins">

<Statement>

Unity negative feedback around $G(s)=\dfrac{K}{s(s+1)(s+5)}$, $K>0$. (a) Find the range of $K$ for stability (Routh). (b) Find the velocity constant and the steady-state error for a unit ramp as a function of $K$; what $K$ is needed for $e_{ss}\le0.2$? (c) For $K=10$ and for the $K$ from (b), find the gain and phase margins and the step overshoot. (d) Comment: can a pure gain meet both accuracy and a decent stability margin?

</Statement>

<Think>

Three questions about the same loop: **how far** can $K$ go (Routh), **how accurate** must it be ($K_v$), and **how comfortable** is the stability (margins). The punchline of the course is that these pull in opposite directions, which is why compensators exist.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Characteristic polynomial">

$s(s+1)(s+5)+K=s^3+6s^2+5s+K=0$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Routh array">

$s^3$: $1,\ 5$. $s^2$: $6,\ K$. $s^1$: $\dfrac{6\cdot5-1\cdot K}{6}=\dfrac{30-K}6$. $s^0$: $K$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Conditions">

First column $1,\ 6,\ \dfrac{30-K}6,\ K$ all positive: $K<30$ and $K>0$. **Stable for $0<K<30$.** At $K=30$ the $s^1$ row vanishes: auxiliary equation $6s^2+30=0\Rightarrow s=\pm j\sqrt5$; the closed-loop roots are $-6,\ \pm j\sqrt5$ ✔.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 1. Velocity constant and ramp error">

Type 1: $K_v=\lim_{s\to0}sG=\dfrac K{1\cdot5}=\dfrac K5$, so for a unit ramp $e_{ss}=\dfrac1{K_v}=\dfrac5K$.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 2. Requirement">

$e_{ss}\le0.2\Rightarrow K\ge25$. Combined with (a): $25\le K<30$.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 1. K = 10 (e_ss = 0.5)">

Gain crossover $\omega_{gc}=1.23$ rad/s, **$PM=25.4^\circ$**; phase crossover $\omega_{pc}=\sqrt5=2.236$ rad/s, **$GM=9.5$ dB** (factor $30/10=3$). Closed-loop poles $-5.42,\ -0.291\pm j1.327$ ($\zeta=0.21$): step overshoot **48.6%**.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 2. K = 25 (e_ss = 0.2)">

$\omega_{gc}=2.04$ rad/s, **$PM=3.9^\circ$**, $GM=20\log(30/25)=1.58$ dB; poles $-5.87,\ -0.063\pm j2.06$ (almost on the axis); overshoot **85.6%**.

</Step>
<Step title="(d) The moral">

To get a ramp error of 0.2 with a pure gain you must run at $K=25$, which is **at the edge of instability** (PM about 4°, GM 1.6 dB): useless. To get decent margins ($K\approx5$: $PM=43^\circ$, $GM=15.6$ dB, overshoot 25%) the ramp error is 1.0. **Accuracy and stability margin cannot both be had with a gain alone**: this is exactly what lag compensation (more $K_v$ at low frequency) and lead compensation (more phase near crossover) fix.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) $0<K<30$; (b) $K_v=K/5$, $e_{ss}=5/K$, $K\ge25$; (c) $K=10$: $PM=25.4^\circ$, $GM=9.5$ dB, OS 48.6%; $K=25$: $PM=3.9^\circ$, $GM=1.6$ dB, OS 85.6%; (d) no: a compensator is needed.

*Real-world:* a position servo whose designer wants to track a moving target tightly: turning up the gain makes it hunt; the controller design problem is to get the accuracy without the hunting.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Using the final-value theorem for the ramp error only after the loop is stable (here $K<30$).
- PM is read at the **gain** crossover, GM at the **phase** crossover.
- Do not forget the integrator when counting the system type.

</Traps>

<Source>Lecture 17–18, 21 examples (Anthony's $K/[s(s+1)(s+5)]$ loop): $K=10$: $\omega_{gc}=1.23$, $PM=25.4^\circ$, $GM=9.5$ dB; $K_{cr}=30$.</Source>

<Problem id="MT3 practice P2" title="Root locus and Ziegler–Nichols on the same plant (25 marks)" tags="asymptotes, break-away, ζ = 0.5, Z–N PID overshoot">

<Statement>

For the loop of P1: (a) sketch the root locus for $K>0$: poles, asymptotes, real-axis segments, break-away point, $j\omega$ crossing. (b) Find $K$ for a dominant pair with $\zeta=0.5$ and check dominance. (c) Use Ziegler–Nichols method 2 to get PID gains from the root-locus information, and find the closed-loop poles and overshoot. (d) Is Z–N a design to a spec?

</Statement>

<Think>

Use the ten-step recipe of <Ch slug="root-locus">Chapter 18</Ch>: poles/zeros, segments, asymptotes, break points from $dK/ds=0$ (reject points with $K<0$), $j\omega$ crossing from Routh, then pick a point on a line of constant $\zeta$ and verify that the other pole is far to the left.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Poles, zeros, segments">

$n=3$ poles ($0,-1,-5$), no finite zeros. Real-axis points with an odd number of poles to the right: $(-1,0)$ and $(-\infty,-5)$ are **on** the locus; $(-5,-1)$ is **not**.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Asymptotes">

$n-m=3$: angles $\pm60^\circ,\ 180^\circ$; centroid $\sigma_a=\dfrac{(0-1-5)-0}{3}=-2$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Break-away">

$K=-s(s+1)(s+5)=-(s^3+6s^2+5s)$; $\dfrac{dK}{ds}=0\Rightarrow3s^2+12s+5=0\Rightarrow s=-0.4725$ or $-3.528$. At $-0.4725$: $K=1.128>0$ ✔ (on the locus). At $-3.528$: $K=-13.1<0$: **reject** (it is on the part of the axis that is not on the locus).

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 4. jω crossing">

From P1: $K=30$, $\omega=\sqrt5=2.236$ rad/s (the roots at $K=30$ are $-6,\pm j2.236$).

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 5. Sketch">

Two branches leave 0 and $-1$, meet at $-0.47$ ($K=1.13$) and leave the axis, curve right, cross the imaginary axis at $\pm j2.24$ ($K=30$) and go off along $\pm60^\circ$; the branch from $-5$ goes left along the negative real axis.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 1. ζ = 0.5 line">

$\theta=\cos^{-1}0.5=60^\circ$ from the negative real axis. Solving numerically for the point on the locus with $\zeta=0.5$: $K=3.59$, with closed-loop poles $-0.417\pm j0.722$ and $-5.17$.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 2. Dominance and check">

The real pole $-5.17$ is about 12 times farther left than the pair's real part $0.417$, so the pair dominates ✔ (sum of roots $=-6$ ✔: $-0.417\cdot2-5.17=-6.0$). Simulated step overshoot: **16.1%** (second-order estimate at $\zeta=0.5$: 16.3%) with $t_p=4.6$ s: slow, because $\sigma=0.417$ is small.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 1. Z–N method 2">

$K_{cr}=30$, $P_{cr}=\dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt5}=2.81$ s. PID: $K_p=0.6K_{cr}=18$, $T_i=0.5P_{cr}=1.40$ s, $T_d=0.125P_{cr}=0.351$ s ($K_i=12.8$, $K_d=6.32$). Equivalent form $G_c=6.32\dfrac{(s+1.42)^2}{s}$ (double zero at $-4/P_{cr}=-1.42$).

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 2. Closed loop">

Closed-loop poles $-4.13,\ -1.12,\ -0.374\pm j1.62$: the slow lightly damped pair ($\zeta=0.23$) gives a step overshoot of **about 62%**.

</Step>
<Step title="(d) Is Z–N a design?">

No: it is a **first cut**. It ignores your specs and typically leaves 25–60% overshoot; you then retune by root locus or Bode to meet the real requirement (the same lesson as <Hw n="9">HW9 Q1</Hw>, where Z–N gave 59%).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) segments $(-1,0)$ and $(-\infty,-5)$; asymptotes $\pm60^\circ,180^\circ$ from $-2$; break-away $-0.4725$ ($K=1.13$); crossing $\pm j\sqrt5$ at $K=30$. (b) $K=3.59$, poles $-0.417\pm j0.722,\ -5.17$, OS 16%. (c) $K_p=18$, $T_i=1.40$, $T_d=0.351$: OS about 62%. (d) No.

*Real-world:* Z–N is the "autotune" button on a temperature controller: it runs exactly the $K_{cr}/P_{cr}$ experiment and gives you a starting point, not a finished design.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Transcription slips in the lecture notes">

Anthony's page writes the double zero as "$(s+1.49)$" and the gain as "6.39"; the correct values are $(s+1.42)^2$ and $K_d=6.32$ (zeros at $-4/P_{cr}$).

</Callout>

<Traps>

- Rejecting break points with $K<0$ (here $-3.528$).
- Always check that the chosen pair is **dominant** (the third pole at $-5.17$ is far left: good; contrast with the second solution of the L20 tachometer example).
- Z–N is not a design to a specification.

</Traps>

<Source>Lecture 19–20 examples (the $K/[s(s+1)(s+5)]$ Z–N exercise and the root-locus rules); HW9 Q1, Q3; numbers recomputed.</Source>

<Problem id="MT3 practice P3" title="Lead compensator by Bode: 4/[s(s+2)], K_v = 20, PM = 50°, GM ≥ 10 dB (20 marks)" tags="lead design, K_c√α, margin check">

<Statement>

Plant $G=\dfrac{4}{s(s+2)}$, unity feedback. Design a lead compensator so that $K_v=20$ s⁻¹, $PM\ge50^\circ$ and $GM\ge10$ dB (Ogata Ex 11-6).

</Statement>

<Think>

**Step 1:** the gain from $K_v$. **Step 2:** read the margin of the gain-adjusted loop. **Step 3:** required lead = target PM − current PM + safety. **Step 4:** $\alpha$ from $\sin\varphi_m$. **Step 5:** put $\omega_m$ at the frequency where the uncompensated loop has gain $-20\log(1/\sqrt\alpha)$, so the new crossover is at $\omega_m$. **Step 6:** corners $1/T=\omega_m\sqrt\alpha$, $1/(\alpha T)=\omega_m/\sqrt\alpha$. Then **verify**.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Gain from K_v">

$K_v=\lim sG_cG=\lim\dfrac{4K}{s+2}=2K=20\Rightarrow K=10$: loop $\dfrac{40}{s(s+2)}$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Margin of the gain-adjusted loop">

$\omega_{gc}=6.17$ rad/s, $PM=18^\circ$, $GM=\infty$ (the phase never reaches $-180^\circ$).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Lead needed">

$50^\circ-18^\circ=32^\circ$, plus a $5^\circ$ safety margin: $\varphi_m=38^\circ$.

</Step>
<Step title="4. α">

$\alpha=\dfrac{1-\sin38^\circ}{1+\sin38^\circ}=\dfrac{1-0.6157}{1.6157}=0.238\approx0.24$.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Where to put ω_m">

The compensator gain at $\omega_m$ is $1/\sqrt\alpha=2.04$ ($+6.2$ dB), so the uncompensated loop must be at $-6.2$ dB there: $|40/(j\omega(j\omega+2))|=0.49$ at $\omega\approx9$ rad/s. So $\omega_m=9$ rad/s.

</Step>
<Step title="6. Corners">

$\dfrac1T=\omega_m\sqrt\alpha=9\sqrt{0.24}=4.4$ (zero), $\dfrac1{\alpha T}=\dfrac{\omega_m}{\sqrt\alpha}=18.4$ (pole). With $K_c=K/\alpha=10/0.24$ (so the DC gain of the compensator stays $K$ and $K_v$ is unchanged):
$$G_c(s)=41.7\,\frac{s+4.4}{s+18.4}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="7. Verify (always)">

$K_v=\dfrac{41.7\cdot4.4}{18.4}\cdot\dfrac42=19.9\approx20$ ✔; $\omega_{gc}=8.88$ rad/s; **$PM=50.6^\circ$** ✔; $GM=\infty$ ✔. Closed-loop poles $-6.96\pm j8.05$, $-6.47$: overshoot about 22%.

</Step>
<Step title="8. Why K_c = K/α">

Choosing $K_c\alpha=K$ keeps the DC gain, hence $K_v$; the price is the high-frequency gain $K_c=K/\alpha$ (here 41.7: noise amplification, the cost of lead).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$G_c(s)=41.7\dfrac{s+4.4}{s+18.4}$: $K_v=20$, $PM=50.6^\circ$, $GM=\infty$.

*Real-world:* a lead is a driver looking ahead: it reacts to how fast the error is changing, stopping the car weaving; the cost is amplified noise.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="Shortcut (K_c√α)">

The compensator gain at $\omega_m$ is $K_c\sqrt\alpha$ (not 1). Choosing $K_c=1/(\sqrt\alpha\,|G(j\omega_m)|)$ puts the crossover at $\omega_m$ in one go (see the closed-form lead of <Hw n="8">HW8 A1</Hw>).

</Callout>

<Traps>

- Not accounting for the shift of the crossover frequency (hence the iterative designs in textbooks).
- Forgetting to scale $K_c$ so that $K_v$ is preserved.
- Never skip the verification step.

</Traps>

<Source>Lecture 22 (Ogata Ex 11-6): $G_c=41.7(s+4.4)/(s+18.4)$, $K_v=19.97$, $PM=50.5^\circ$, $\omega_{gc}=8.88$ rad/s; verified.</Source>

<Problem id="MT3 practice P4" title="Nyquist: critical gain and an unstable open loop (15 marks)" tags="Z = N + P, indentation, K_max">

<Statement>

(a) For $L=\dfrac{K}{s(s^2+s+6)}$ decide closed-loop stability by the Nyquist criterion and find the largest $K$ from the real-axis crossing. (b) For $L=\dfrac{K}{s-1}$ (one open-loop pole in the right half-plane) find the values of $K$ for which the closed loop is stable.

</Statement>

<Think>

$Z=N+P$: $P$ = open-loop RHP poles, $N$ = clockwise encirclements of $-1$, $Z$ = closed-loop RHP poles. Poles on the $j\omega$ axis need indentations. For (b), with $P=1$ you need **one counter-clockwise** encirclement ($N=-1$).

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Counting">

$P=0$ (the origin pole is handled by the indentation). For $K=1$ the curve makes no encirclement of $-1$: $N=0\Rightarrow Z=0$, stable.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Real-axis crossing">

$L(j\omega)=\dfrac{K}{j\omega(6-\omega^2+j\omega)}$; the imaginary part vanishes at $\omega^2=6$, where $u=-\dfrac{6K}{36}=-\dfrac K6$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. K_max">

Stable while $-K/6>-1\Rightarrow K<6$. Check with Routh on $s^3+s^2+6s+K$: stable iff $K<6$ ✔; at $K=6$ the poles are $\pm j\sqrt6$ and $-1$.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 1. Direct check">

Closed loop: $s-1+K=0\Rightarrow s=1-K$: stable iff $K>1$.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 2. Nyquist reading">

$L(j\omega)=\dfrac{K}{-1+j\omega}$: at $\omega=0$, $L=-K$; as $\omega\to\infty$, $L\to0$; the plot is a circle through $-K$ and the origin. For $K>1$ the circle encloses $-1$ **once counter-clockwise** ($N=-1$), so $Z=N+P=-1+1=0$ ✔ stable. For $K<1$ it does not enclose $-1$: $N=0$, $Z=1$, unstable.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) stable for $0<K<6$, $K_{max}=6$; (b) stable for $K>1$ (a *minimum* gain, as for any unstable plant).

*Real-world:* (b) is the inverted pendulum in one line: too little feedback and it falls; enough gain and it is held.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- With $P=1$ you need a **counter-clockwise** encirclement.
- Poles on the $j\omega$ axis need indentations; confirm with Routh when a polynomial is available.

</Traps>

<Source><Hw n="8">HW8 A3(a)</Hw> and Lecture 23 ($K/(s-1)$-type example); verified by Routh and the closed-loop root.</Source>

<Problem id="MT3 practice P5" title="Review: op-amp network recognised as a PID (10 marks)" tags="−Z₂/Z₁, PID gains, units">

<Statement>

Inverting op-amp: input $C_1=1\ \mu$F in parallel with $R_1=10$ kΩ; feedback $R_2=20$ kΩ in series with $C_2=10\ \mu$F. Find the transfer function, show it is a PID and give $K_p,K_i,K_d$.

</Statement>

<Think>

Same as $\text{T4 Q3}$ and $\text{MT2 W2011 Q4}$: $V_o/V_i=-Z_2/Z_1$ with $Z_1=R_1\parallel C_1$ and $Z_2=R_2+1/(C_2s)$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Transfer function">

$\dfrac{V_o}{V_i}=-\dfrac{(R_1C_1s+1)(R_2C_2s+1)}{R_1C_2s}$ with $R_1C_1=0.01$ s, $R_2C_2=0.2$ s, $R_1C_2=0.1$ s: $-\dfrac{(0.01s+1)(0.2s+1)}{0.1s}$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Split into P, I, D">

$-\dfrac{0.002s^2+0.21s+1}{0.1s}=-\Big[0.02\,s+2.1+\dfrac{10}{s}\Big]$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Gains">

$K_p=-\Big(\dfrac{C_1}{C_2}+\dfrac{R_2}{R_1}\Big)=-(0.1+2)=-2.1$; $K_i=-\dfrac1{R_1C_2}=-10$ s⁻¹; $K_d=-R_2C_1=-0.02$ s. All negative (inverting stage): follow it with an inverting stage to make them positive.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$-\Big[0.02s+2.1+\dfrac{10}s\Big]$: $K_p=-2.1$, $K_i=-10$ s⁻¹, $K_d=-0.02$ s ($T_i=K_p/K_i=0.21$ s, $T_d=K_d/K_p=0.0095$ s).

</Answer>

<Traps>

- The input impedance is the **parallel** pair $R_1\parallel C_1$.
- $K_p$ depends on both ratios; $K_i$ only on $R_1C_2$; $K_d$ only on $R_2C_1$.

</Traps>

<Problem id="MT3 practice P6" title="Review: DC motor with tachometer feedback, error and sensitivity (10 marks)" tags="back emf, e_ss, S^T_{R_a}">

<Statement>

Motor with $K_m=10$ N·m/A, $J=1$ kg·m², $R_a=1\ \Omega$, $K_b=0.1$ V·s/rad, tachometer gain $K_t=1$, controller gain $K_p$. (a) Closed-loop $\omega/V$. (b) $K_p$ so that the error to a unit step is at most 0.1. (c) For $K_p=2$: the error, and the sensitivity of the closed loop to $R_a$ at low and high frequency.

</Statement>

<Think>

This is the structure of $\text{MT2 W2012 Q3}$: the back emf makes the motor a first-order lag; the tachometer loop sets the DC gain; sensitivity to $R_a$ is zero at DC only when the loop gain is large.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) Closed loop">

$T=\dfrac{K_mK_p}{R_aJs+K_mK_b+K_mK_pK_t}=\dfrac{10K_p}{s+1+10K_p}$.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Error">

$e_{ss}=1-T(0)=\dfrac1{1+10K_p}\le0.1\Rightarrow K_p\ge0.9$.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) K_p = 2">

$e_{ss}=\dfrac1{21}=0.048$ (4.8%). Sensitivity $S^T_{R_a}=-\dfrac{R_as}{R_as+1+10K_p}=-\dfrac{s}{s+21}$: at low frequency $S\approx-s/21\to0$; at high frequency $S\to-1$ whatever $K_p$.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) $\dfrac{10K_p}{s+1+10K_p}$; (b) $K_p\ge0.9$; (c) $e_{ss}=4.8\%$, $S=-\dfrac s{s+21}$.

</Answer>
