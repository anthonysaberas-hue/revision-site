<Problem id="MT2 W2011 Q1a" title="Three-mass mechanical system: mobility and impedance analogs (≈ 12 marks)" tags="mobility analog, force source first, 5th order">

<Statement>

Three masses $M_{M1},M_{M2},M_{M3}$ on a frictionless floor; a spring $C_{M1}$ and mass $M_{M1}$ on the left with dashpots $R_{M1}$ (to the wall above) and $R_{M2}$ (between $M_{M1}$ and $M_{M2}$); a spring $C_{M2}$ between $M_{M2}$ and $M_{M3}$; a dashpot $R_{M3}$ from $M_{M3}$ to the right wall; the force $F(t)$ pushes $M_{M2}$. Draw equivalent circuits in the mobility and impedance analogs.

</Statement>

<Think>

Mobility: potential = velocity, flux = force; masses are capacitors **to ground** (absolute velocity), springs inductors $C_M=1/k$, dashpots resistors $1/R_M$. Crib rule: **if there is a force input, start with that component.** A damper or spring to a fixed wall goes from that mass's node to ground; one between two masses goes between the nodes.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Nodes">

$\dot x_1,\dot x_2,\dot x_3$ (absolute velocities) and ground (the fixed walls).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Masses">

Capacitors $M_{M1},M_{M2},M_{M3}$ from each node to ground.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Springs">

Inductors of value $C_M=1/k$: $C_{M1}$ from node 1 to ground (wall spring); $C_{M2}$ between nodes 2 and 3.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Dashpots">

Resistors $1/R_M$: $R_{M1}$ (wall) from its node to ground; $R_{M2}$ between nodes 1 and 2; $R_{M3}$ from node 3 to ground.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Source">

$F(t)$ is a **current source** into node 2 (the node of $M_{M2}$).

</Step>
<Step title="6. Impedance analog (official, second sketch)">

Three series loops: $F$ as a voltage source; masses become inductors $M_M$, springs capacitors $C_M$, dashpots resistors $R_M$; the elements shared between adjacent masses ($R_{M2}$, $C_{M2}$) are the elements shared between adjacent loops.

</Step>
<Step title="7. Count check">

3 masses + 2 springs = 5 states: a **5th-order** system.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

Mobility: current source $F$ into node 2; capacitors $M_{M1},M_{M2},M_{M3}$ to ground; inductors $C_{M1}$ (1 to ground), $C_{M2}$ (2 to 3); resistors $1/R_{M1}$ (1 to ground), $1/R_{M2}$ (1 to 2), $1/R_{M3}$ (3 to ground).

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="Figure ambiguity">

The figure is slightly ambiguous about which mass $R_{M1}$ acts on; the rule is the same either way: a damper to a fixed wall is a resistor from that mass's node to ground. The circuit is **non-planar** in the official drawing (the $R_{M1}$ branch crosses): redraw with the ground as the common bottom rail.

</Callout>

<Traps>

- Putting $R_{M1}$ in series with the mass instead of to the wall.
- A spring is an inductor in the mobility analog.

</Traps>

<Problem id="MT2 W2011 Q1b" title="Hydro-pneumatic system: tank, valve, pipe masses, piston-closed air cavity (≈ 13 marks)" tags="flux divider, transformer 1:A_p, volume vs mass flux">

<Statement>

A liquid tank feeds a pipe through a valve; the pipe has fluid inertias; a piston-closed air cavity $C_A$ branches off at a node $b$ (as in the L14–15 representative problem). Draw the equivalent circuit.

</Statement>

<Think>

Work in **volume flux** (mass flux $\div\rho_f$). Liquid compliance is a shunt capacitor; valve and pipe inertias are series elements; the piston branch takes a share of the flow ($A_p\dot x$) and converts it to mechanical motion with a transformer $1:A_p$; the air cavity pushes back on the piston.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Source and tank">

Current source $m_{in}/\rho_f$ into a shunt capacitor $C_f/\rho_f$ (the tank).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Valve and first pipe section">

Series $\rho_fR_f$ and $\rho_fM_{f1}$, arriving at node $b$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Node b: flux divider">

Volume balance: flow arriving $=$ flow leaving through the pipe $+A_p\dot x$. One branch continues through the series $\rho_fM_{f2}$ to the output $m_{out}/\rho_f$; the other leaves through the piston.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Piston branch">

A transformer $1:A_p$ to the mechanical **mobility** circuit (capacitor $M_M$, piston velocity $\dot x$), and a second transformer to the acoustic impedance circuit with $C_A$ (the air cavity pushing back on the piston). The official page labels the blocks "mech. imp. / acoustic imp. / fluid" with the ratios "$1{:}A_p$" and "$A_p{:}1$".

</Step>
<Step title="5. Reasoning check">

The piston moves because the water pressure $P_b$ pushes on $A_p$ and the air cavity $C_A$ pushes back. Units: mass flux $\div\rho_f=$ volume flux.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

Source $\to C_f/\rho_f\to\rho_fR_f\to\rho_fM_{f1}\to$ node $b$ (piston branch via transformers) $\to\rho_fM_{f2}\to$ output.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Forgetting transformers between media, or using mass flux without converting by $\rho_f$.

</Traps>

<Source>official ✔ (same solution as in the lecture-exercise pack).</Source>

<Problem id="MT2 W2011 Q2" title="Tank level regulator: error equation, offset with P and with I, sensitivity to C (25 marks)" tags="final value, integral action, S^T_C">

<Statement>

A tank (capacitance $C$, orifice resistance $R$) with inflow $q_1$ from a controller and a disturbance inflow $q_3$; level $h$. Block diagram: desired variation $H_d(s)=0$, error $E(s)\to$ controller $G(s)\to\dfrac{R}{RCs+1}\to\oplus$ (with the disturbance $Q_3(s)$) $\to H(s)$; negative feedback of $H$ to the error junction. (a) The equation for $E(s)$. (b) The steady-state error for a unit-step disturbance $Q_3=1/s$ with $G=K$. (c) The same for $G=K/s$. (d) The closed-loop sensitivity to variations in $C$ for $G=K$, $Q_3=0$.

</Statement>

<Think>

A float-controlled tank. A step disturbance (an extra inflow) raises the level; a proportional controller leaves an **offset**, an **integral** controller removes it; sensitivity measures how much the closed loop changes if the tank's area changes.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Error">

$E=H_d-H=-H$ (since $H_d=0$).

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Level from the diagram">

$H=Q_3+\dfrac{R}{RCs+1}G\,E$ (the figure adds $Q_3$ after the tank block, as the official solution does).

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Substitute">

$E=-Q_3-\dfrac{R}{RCs+1}GE\Rightarrow E\Big[1+\dfrac{RG}{RCs+1}\Big]=-Q_3$:
$$E(s)=-\frac{(RCs+1)\,Q_3(s)}{RCs+1+RG(s)} .$$

</Step>
<Step title="(b) G = K, Q₃ = 1/s">

Final-value theorem: $e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}sE=-\dfrac{1}{1+RK}$: a **constant offset** (the larger $K$, the smaller, but never zero).

</Step>
<Step title="(c) G = K/s">

$sE(s)=-\dfrac{s(RCs+1)}{RCs^2+s+RK}\Rightarrow e_{ss}=0$ ✔: an integrator in the controller rejects a constant disturbance (the plant has no integrator before the disturbance entry here, so the controller must supply it). The characteristic polynomial $RCs^2+s+RK$ has all positive coefficients, so it is stable and the final-value theorem is valid.

</Step>
<Step title="(d) 1. Closed loop (Q₃ = 0)">

$H=\dfrac{R}{RCs+1}K(H_d-H)\Rightarrow T=\dfrac{H}{H_d}=\dfrac{RK}{RCs+1+RK}$.

</Step>
<Step title="(d) 2. Sensitivity">

$S^T_C=\dfrac CT\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial C}$ with $\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial C}=-\dfrac{RK\cdot Rs}{(RCs+1+RK)^2}$:
$$S^T_C=-\frac{RCs}{RCs+1+RK}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="(d) 3. Reading S">

$S=0$ at DC (the steady value $\tfrac{RK}{1+RK}$ does not depend on $C$); at high frequency $S\to-1$; a larger loop gain $RK$ enlarges the denominator and lowers the sensitivity at low frequencies.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) $E=-\dfrac{(RCs+1)Q_3}{RCs+1+RG}$; (b) $e_{ss}=-\dfrac1{1+RK}$; (c) $e_{ss}=0$; (d) $S^T_C=-\dfrac{RCs}{RCs+1+RK}$.

*Real-world:* a water-tank or boiler level controller: proportional-only leaves a level offset when demand changes; integral action (PI/PID) removes it.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Applying the final-value theorem without checking stability (fine here).
- Dropping the minus sign in $E=-H$.

</Traps>

<Source>official ✔ (all four parts reproduced).</Source>

<Problem id="MT2 W2011 Q3" title="DC servomotor: transfer function, position servo, maximum gain for overshoot < 20 % (25 marks)" tags="back-emf damping, ζ from overshoot, K_max">

<Statement>

$J_m\ddot\theta_m+\Big(b+\dfrac{K_tK_e}{R_a}\Big)\dot\theta_m=\dfrac{K_t}{R_a}v_a$ with $J_m=0.01$ kg·m², $b=0.001$ N·m·s, $K_e=0.02$ V·s, $K_t=0.02$ N·m/A, $R_a=10\ \Omega$. (a) $\Theta_m/V_a$. (b) With feedback $v_a=K(\theta_r-\theta_m)$, $\Theta_m/\Theta_r$. (c) The largest $K$ for overshoot $M_p<20\%$.

</Statement>

<Think>

The back emf adds to the mechanical damping ($K_tK_e/R_a$), and the plant is an integrator times a lag. With position feedback the closed loop is a second-order system whose $\omega_n^2$ is proportional to $K$ and whose damping term does **not** grow with $K$: more gain means less damping.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Laplace">

$J_ms^2\Theta+\Big(b+\dfrac{K_tK_e}{R_a}\Big)s\Theta=\dfrac{K_t}{R_a}V_a$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Numbers">

$\dfrac{K_tK_e}{R_a}=\dfrac{0.02\cdot0.02}{10}=4\times10^{-5}$; total damping $0.001+0.00004=1.04\times10^{-3}$; $\dfrac{K_t}{R_a}=2\times10^{-3}$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Result">

$$\frac{\Theta_m}{V_a}=\frac{0.002}{0.01s^2+0.00104s}=\frac{0.2}{s(s+0.104)}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Close the loop">

$V_a=K(\Theta_r-\Theta_m)$: $(J_ms^2+1.04\times10^{-3}s+0.002K)\Theta_m=0.002K\,\Theta_r$:
$$\frac{\Theta_m}{\Theta_r}=\frac{0.2K}{s^2+0.104s+0.2K}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 1. Match the standard form">

$\omega_n^2=0.2K$, $2\zeta\omega_n=0.104\Rightarrow\zeta=\dfrac{0.104}{2\sqrt{0.2K}}=\dfrac{0.052}{\sqrt{0.2K}}$.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 2. Overshoot to damping">

$M_p=e^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}<0.20$: $\ln0.20=-1.609$, so $\dfrac{\zeta\pi}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}>1.609\Rightarrow\dfrac{\zeta^2\pi^2}{1-\zeta^2}>2.590\Rightarrow\zeta^2(\pi^2+2.590)>2.590\Rightarrow\zeta^2>0.2079\Rightarrow\zeta>0.4559$.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 3. Solve for K">

$\dfrac{0.104}{2\sqrt{0.2K}}>0.4559\Rightarrow\sqrt{0.2K}<0.1140\Rightarrow0.2K<0.0130\Rightarrow K<0.065$. So **$K_{max}=0.065$ V/rad**.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) 4. Check">

$K=0.065$: $\omega_n=\sqrt{0.013}=0.114$ rad/s, $\zeta=0.456$, overshoot $20.0\%$ ✔. The response is **slow**: settling $4/(\zeta\omega_n)=77$ s (the small gain needed comes from the almost frictionless, low-gain motor: damping 0.104 relative to the inertia).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) $\dfrac{0.2}{s(s+0.104)}$; (b) $\dfrac{0.2K}{s^2+0.104s+0.2K}$; (c) $K_{max}=0.065$ V/rad.

*Real-world:* a position servo: small gain gives a gentle, well-damped response; high gain a fast but oscillatory motor.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Forgetting the back-emf contribution $K_tK_e/R_a$ to the damping.
- $\zeta$ must use the **total** damping 0.104, not $b$ alone.

</Traps>

<Source>official ✔ ($0.2/(s^2+0.104s)$, $0.2K/(s^2+0.104s+0.2K)$, $K_{max}=0.065$).</Source>

<Problem id="MT2 W2011 Q4" title="Op-amp circuit as a PID controller (25 marks)" tags="inverting op-amp, PID identification">

<Statement>

Inverting op-amp; input: $C_1$ in parallel with $R_1$ in series with $V_i$; feedback: $R_2$ in series with $C_2$. (a) $V_o/V_i$. (b) Show it can be written $K_p+K_i/s+K_ds$. (c) Identify $K_p,K_i,K_d$ in terms of $R_1,R_2,C_1,C_2$.

</Statement>

<Think>

Ideal inverting amplifier: $V_o/V_i=-Z_2/Z_1$. The input element is a **parallel** pair, the feedback element a **series** pair.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Impedances">

$Z_1=R_1\parallel C_1=\dfrac{R_1}{R_1C_1s+1}$; $Z_2=R_2+\dfrac1{C_2s}=\dfrac{R_2C_2s+1}{C_2s}$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Divide">

$$\frac{V_o}{V_i}=-\frac{Z_2}{Z_1}=-\frac{(R_1C_1s+1)(R_2C_2s+1)}{R_1C_2s}=-\frac{R_1R_2C_1C_2s^2+(R_1C_1+R_2C_2)s+1}{R_1C_2s}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Divide each term by the denominator R₁C₂s">

$$G(s)=-\frac{R_1C_1+R_2C_2}{R_1C_2}-\frac{1}{R_1C_2s}-R_2C_1s .$$

</Step>
<Step title="(c) Read the gains">

$$K_p=-\Big(\frac{C_1}{C_2}+\frac{R_2}{R_1}\Big),\quad K_i=-\frac1{R_1C_2},\quad K_d=-R_2C_1 .$$
All three are negative (the stage inverts): follow it by an inverting stage to get positive gains.

</Step>
<Step title="Checks">

The expansion of the numerator ✔. With $R_2\to0$ the derivative action disappears (a PI remains); with $C_1\to0$ likewise ✔.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$\dfrac{V_o}{V_i}=-\dfrac{(R_1C_1s+1)(R_2C_2s+1)}{R_1C_2s}$; $K_p=-\Big(\dfrac{C_1}{C_2}+\dfrac{R_2}{R_1}\Big)$, $K_i=-\dfrac1{R_1C_2}$, $K_d=-R_2C_1$.

*Real-world:* the classic analog PID amplifier: the proportional gain depends on both ratios, the integral gain only on $R_1C_2$, the derivative gain only on $R_2C_1$.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Using the parallel impedance as $R_1+1/(C_1s)$ (series).
- Dropping the minus sign.

</Traps>

<Source>official ✔ (final page).</Source>
