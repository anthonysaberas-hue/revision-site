<Problem id="MT1 F2011 Q1" title="State-space model with no input: ODE and closed-form response from initial conditions (25 marks)" tags="initial conditions, completing the square">

<Statement>

A Matlab script `A=[0 1;-3 -1]; B=[0;0]; C=[1 0]; D=0; initial(A,B,C,D,[1;2])` produces a plot: the response starts at 1, rises to about 1.4, then decays in an oscillation of period about 3.8 s. (a) Find the differential equation for $y(t)$. (b) With the Laplace transform find a closed-form $y(t)$.

</Statement>

<Think>

No input ($B=0$): only the initial state $[1;2]$ drives the response. Read the ODE from the rows of $A$: row 1 says $\dot x_1=x_2$ (velocity), row 2 says $\dot x_2=-3x_1-x_2$ (a spring of 3 and a damper of 1). So $y=x_1$ solves $\ddot y+\dot y+3y=0$ and the initial state **is** $(y(0),\dot y(0))$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Read the rows">

$\dot x_1=x_2$, $\dot x_2=-3x_1-x_2$, $y=x_1$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Eliminate">

$\ddot x_1=\dot x_2=-3x_1-\dot x_1\Rightarrow\ddot y+\dot y+3y=0$, with $y(0)=x_1(0)=1$ and $\dot y(0)=x_2(0)=2$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Read the dynamics">

$\omega_n^2=3\ (\omega_n=1.732)$, $2\zeta\omega_n=1\Rightarrow\zeta=0.289$ (underdamped), $\omega_d=1.658$ rad/s, period $2\pi/\omega_d=3.79$ s ✔ (matches the plot).

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 1. Laplace with initial conditions">

$[s^2Y-sy(0)-\dot y(0)]+[sY-y(0)]+3Y=0\Rightarrow(s^2+s+3)Y=\dot y(0)+(s+1)y(0)=2+s+1=s+3$:
$$Y(s)=\frac{s+3}{s^2+s+3}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 2. Complete the square">

$s^2+s+3=(s+\tfrac12)^2+\tfrac{11}4$ and $s+3=(s+\tfrac12)+\tfrac52$, so
$$Y=\frac{s+\frac12}{(s+\frac12)^2+\frac{11}4}+\frac{5/2}{(s+\frac12)^2+\frac{11}4}.$$
Write $\tfrac52=\tfrac5{\sqrt{11}}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2$ to match the sine form:
$$y(t)=e^{-t/2}\Big[\cos\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2t+\tfrac5{\sqrt{11}}\sin\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2t\Big].$$

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 3. Checks">

$y(0)=1$ ✔. $\dot y(0)=-\tfrac12+\tfrac{5}{\sqrt{11}}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2=-\tfrac12+\tfrac52=2$ ✔. The first peak is about 1.4, as on the plot.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$\ddot y+\dot y+3y=0$, $y(0)=1$, $\dot y(0)=2$; $y(t)=e^{-t/2}\big[\cos\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2t+\tfrac5{\sqrt{11}}\sin\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2t\big]$.

*Real-world:* a lightly damped spring–mass ($m=1$, $k=3$, $c=1$) released with displacement 1 and velocity 2: it overshoots to 1.4, swings through $-0.5$ and settles.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Using zero initial velocity (the initial state is $[1;2]$).
- Forgetting that the numerator is $\dot y(0)+(s+1)y(0)$.

</Traps>

<Source>official ✔ (identical; sympy confirms).</Source>

</Problem>

<Problem id="MT1 F2011 Q2" title="Block diagram with three integrators: transfer function and state-space model (25 marks)" tags="reading a diagram, Method II">

<Statement>

Three integrators $\tfrac1s$ in series with outputs $X_1,X_2,X_3$ ($X_2=X_1/s$, $X_3=X_1/s^2$). The first integrator is driven by $U-a_1X_1-a_2X_2-a_3X_3$; the output is $Y=b_1X_1+b_2X_2+b_3X_3$. (a) Find $T(s)=Y/U$. (b) Find the state-space model $A,B,C,D$ in terms of the $a_i,b_j$.

</Statement>

<Think>

An integrator's input is the derivative of its output, so the first summing junction equals $sX_1$. Substitute the integrator relations and everything becomes a polynomial ratio.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. First junction">

$U-a_1X_1-a_2X_2-a_3X_3=sX_1$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Substitute X₂ and X₃">

$U=\Big(s+a_1+\dfrac{a_2}s+\dfrac{a_3}{s^2}\Big)X_1=\dfrac{s^3+a_1s^2+a_2s+a_3}{s^2}X_1$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Output">

$Y=\Big(b_1+\dfrac{b_2}s+\dfrac{b_3}{s^2}\Big)X_1=\dfrac{b_1s^2+b_2s+b_3}{s^2}X_1$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 4. Divide">

$$T(s)=\frac{b_1s^2+b_2s+b_3}{s^3+a_1s^2+a_2s+a_3}.$$

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 1. State choice">

The numerator has $s^2$ and $s$ terms (input derivatives), but this diagram is already a **Method II** realisation: the states are the integrator outputs and the $b$'s form the output equation, so no $\beta$ coefficients are needed. Choose the states in reverse order of the chain: $x_1=X_3$ (last integrator), $x_2=X_2$, $x_3=X_1$ (first).

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 2. Equations">

$\dot x_1=x_2$, $\dot x_2=x_3$, $\dot x_3=u-a_3x_1-a_2x_2-a_1x_3$, $y=b_3x_1+b_2x_2+b_1x_3$:
$$A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\-a_3&-a_2&-a_1\end{bmatrix},\ B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix},\ C=[b_3\ \ b_2\ \ b_1],\ D=0.$$

</Step>
<Step title="(b) 3. Method I alternative and check">

Method I gives the same $A$ with $B'=\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2-a_1b_1\\b_3-a_1(b_2-a_1b_1)-a_2b_1\end{bmatrix}$, $C=[1\ 0\ 0]$, $D=0$. Check: $C(sI-A)^{-1}B$ for Method II returns exactly $\dfrac{b_3+b_2s+b_1s^2}{s^3+a_1s^2+a_2s+a_3}$ ✔ (the order of $C$ follows $[b_3\,b_2\,b_1]$).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$T(s)=\dfrac{b_1s^2+b_2s+b_3}{s^3+a_1s^2+a_2s+a_3}$; $A,B,C,D$ as above.

*Real-world:* any third-order system can be built from three integrators and gain blocks (an analog computer or a Simulink model).

</Answer>

<Traps>

- $C=[b_3\ b_2\ b_1]$: $b_1$ multiplies the **highest** power $s^2$.
- The diagram's numbering ($X_1$ = first integrator) is the reverse of the companion-form numbering ($x_1$ = last integrator).

</Traps>

<Source>official ✔ (it gives both the Method II and the Method I $B$ vectors).</Source>

</Problem>

<Problem id="MT1 F2011 Q3" title="Parallel spring–dashpot plus an end dashpot (massless junction): TF, state space, frequency response (25 marks)" tags="massless node, lead element, frequency response">

<Statement>

$k=6$ N/m, $R_{M1}=R_{M2}=2$ N·s/m. A **massless** junction is connected to the input end (displacement $u$) through the parallel pair spring $k\parallel$ dashpot $R_{M1}$, and to the wall by dashpot $R_{M2}$; $y$ is the junction's displacement. (a) $Y/U$. (b) One valid state-space model. (c) Amplitude and phase of the frequency response at $\omega=5$ rad/s.

</Statement>

<Think>

A massless point cannot store energy, so the forces on it balance. That gives a first-order system with a zero (a "lead" element): the dashpot $R_{M1}$ passes high-frequency motion straight through.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Force balance on the junction">

$k(u-y)+R_{M1}(\dot u-\dot y)-R_{M2}\dot y=0$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Collect">

$(R_{M1}+R_{M2})\dot y+ky=ku+R_{M1}\dot u$. Divide by $R_{M1}+R_{M2}=4$: $\dot y+a\,y=a\,u+b\,\dot u$ with $a=\dfrac{k}{R_{M1}+R_{M2}}=\dfrac32$, $b=\dfrac{R_{M1}}{R_{M1}+R_{M2}}=\dfrac12$.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Laplace">

$$\frac YU=\frac{a+bs}{s+a}=\frac{s/2+3/2}{s+3/2}=\frac{s+3}{2s+3}.$$
**Checks:** DC gain $3/3=1$ (a static pull of $u$ moves the junction by the same amount) ✔. High-frequency gain $\tfrac12$: the dashpots form a divider $R_{M1}/(R_{M1}+R_{M2})$ ✔.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) State space (Method II with one state)">

$\dot z+az=u$, $y=az+b\dot z=az+b(-az+u)=a(1-b)z+bu$:
$$\dot x=-\tfrac32x+u,\qquad y=\tfrac34x+\tfrac12u .$$
Method I gives the equivalent $\dot x=-1.5x+0.75u$, $y=x+0.5u$ (same transfer function: $\tfrac{0.75}{s+1.5}+0.5=\tfrac{0.5s+1.5}{s+1.5}$ ✔).

</Step>
<Step title="(c) Frequency response at ω = 5">

$G(j5)=\dfrac{3+5j}{3+10j}$. Magnitude $\dfrac{\sqrt{34}}{\sqrt{109}}=0.559$. Phase $\tan^{-1}\tfrac53-\tan^{-1}\tfrac{10}3=59.04^\circ-73.30^\circ=-14.3^\circ$ ($=-0.25$ rad).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) $\dfrac{s+3}{2s+3}$; (b) $\dot x=-1.5x+u$, $y=0.75x+0.5u$; (c) $|G|=0.56$, phase $-14.3^\circ$.

*Real-world:* a mount with a viscoelastic coupling: slow motions pass 1:1, fast motions are attenuated to $\tfrac12$ (phase lag about $14^\circ$ at 5 rad/s).

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Giving the junction a mass (it has none; an inertia would change the order).
- Using $u$ instead of $u-y$ for the elongations of the spring and dashpot.

</Traps>

<Source>official ✔ (textbook problem A-5-11).</Source>

</Problem>

<Problem id="MT1 F2011 Q4" title="Time-domain design with a zero: choose τ, ω_n, ζ for 16.3 % overshoot and 2.4 s rise time (25 marks)" tags="second-order with a zero, rise time, α = 1/(τζω_n)">

<Statement>

An aircraft control system $G(s)=\dfrac{\tau\omega_n^2(s+1/\tau)}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$ (second order with a zero at $-1/\tau$). The step response must have **overshoot 16.3 %** and **rise time 2.4 s**. Given charts of the step response for $\alpha=1,2,4,100$ ($\alpha=\dfrac1{\tau\zeta\omega_n}$) and of the overshoot versus $\alpha$ for $\zeta=0.3,0.5,0.7$: (a) find one set $\tau,\omega_n,\zeta$; (b) show the poles and zero; (c) how does the overshoot change if the zero moves closer to the poles?

</Statement>

<Think>

A zero near the poles adds a "derivative kick": more overshoot, faster rise. $\alpha$ is the zero's distance divided by the real part of the poles: when $\alpha$ is large the zero is far away and the system behaves as a plain second-order system (overshoot $16.3\%$ for $\zeta=0.5$). So choose $\zeta=0.5$ for the 16.3 %, a large $\alpha$, and $\omega_n$ from the rise time.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) 1. Damping ratio">

$100e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ with $\zeta=0.5$: $16.30\%$ ✔ (the target). So $\zeta=0.5$ and a zero far enough away that it hardly changes this.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 2. Rise time">

Course formula $t_r=\dfrac{\pi-\beta}{\omega_d}$ with $\beta=\cos^{-1}\zeta=60^\circ=\pi/3$: $t_r=\dfrac{2\pi/3}{\omega_d}=\dfrac{2.094}{\omega_d}=2.4\Rightarrow\omega_d=0.87$ rad/s.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 3. Natural frequency">

$\omega_n=\dfrac{\omega_d}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}=\dfrac{0.87}{0.866}\approx1.0$ rad/s.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 4. Zero position">

Official: read $\alpha=10$ from the chart: $\tau=\dfrac1{\alpha\zeta\omega_n}=\dfrac1{10\cdot0.5\cdot1}=0.2$ s, zero at $-1/\tau=-5$. Official answer: $\omega_n=1$, $\zeta=0.5$, $\tau=0.2$ s.

</Step>
<Step title="(a) 5. Check by simulation">

With $\alpha=10$ the overshoot is **16.7%** (first reaching 1 at 2.2 s): slightly **above** the 16.3% target (the zero adds about 0.4%). Overshoot against $\alpha$ for $\zeta=0.5$: $\alpha=2$: 29.8%; $5$: 18.0%; $10$: 16.7%; $20$: 16.39%; $100$: 16.31% with rise time 2.40 s. So for both specs "to the digit" use $\alpha=100$ ($\tau=0.02$ s, zero at $-50$).

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Pole-zero map">

Poles $-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=-0.5\pm j0.866$ (angle $60^\circ$ from the negative real axis); zero at $-1/\tau=-5$ on the real axis, left of the poles.

</Step>
<Step title="(c) Zero closer to the poles">

Overshoot **increases** as $\alpha$ decreases (the zero's derivative action makes the response faster and more oscillatory: $\alpha=5$ gives 18.0%, $\alpha=2$ about 30%). Moving the zero away ($\alpha\to\infty$) recovers the plain second-order 16.3%.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

(a) $\zeta=0.5$, $\omega_n=1$ rad/s, $\tau=0.2$ s ($\alpha=10$) or, to meet 16.3% exactly, $\tau=0.02$ s ($\alpha=100$). (b) poles $-0.5\pm j0.866$, zero $-5$. (c) overshoot increases.

*Real-world:* aircraft pitch control: the zero from the lead term gives a quicker response but a bigger overshoot than the second-order approximation suggests.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Official choice is slightly out of spec">

$\alpha=10$ gives **16.7%**, not "below 16%" as the official reading of the chart suggests. Use $\alpha\ge20$ (16.39%) or $\alpha=100$ (16.31%, rise time 2.40 s).

</Callout>

<Traps>

- Choosing $\zeta$ from a chart without checking the zero's effect.
- Using the rule of thumb $t_r=1.8/\omega_n$ when the course formula $t_r=(\pi-\beta)/\omega_d$ is expected.

</Traps>

<Source>official: structure ✔; the final choice $\alpha=10$ ✘ marginal; (b), (c) ✔.</Source>
