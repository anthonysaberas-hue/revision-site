# Impedance and mobility analogies, transformers and gyrators

<div className="lede">

The most-examined skill in MECH 412: given a mechanical (or hydraulic, or acoustic) system, **draw an equivalent electrical circuit**. Every MT2 has it as Question 1. The reason it is worth the points: once the circuit is drawn, you already know how to analyse it (Chapter 8), and the *same* circuit language works for mass–spring systems, tanks, pipes and motors alike.

</div>

<Idea title="The 30-second version">

Pick which mechanical quantity plays the role of **voltage** (the "potential") and which plays **current** (the "flux"). Two choices:

- **Mobility analog:** potential = **velocity**, flux = **force**. The circuit has the same *shape* as the mechanical sketch (parallel stays parallel). Masses are **capacitors to ground**, springs are **inductors**, dashpots are **resistors**.
- **Impedance analog:** potential = **force**, flux = **velocity**. It is the *dual* of the other: series ↔ parallel, mass → **inductor**, spring → **capacitor**, dashpot → **resistor**.

Both give the same equations; use whichever is easier. Then translate between domains with **transformers** (levers, gears, piston areas) and **gyrators**.

</Idea>

## Why does this work at all?

Compare a mass–spring–damper and a series RLC loop:

$$
M\ddot x+R_M\dot x+\frac1{C_M}x=F(t)\qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad L\ddot q+R\dot q+\frac1Cq=e(t).
$$

Same equation, different symbols. Systems described by the same differential equation are *analogous*, and the solution of one is the solution of the other.

## The element maps

| Mechanical element | Relation | **Mobility** circuit element | **Impedance** circuit element |
|---|---|---|---|
| **mass** $M_M$ | $F=M_M\dot u$ | capacitor $C=M_M$ (one end **grounded**) | inductor $L=M_M$ |
| **spring** $k=1/C_M$ | $F=k\!\int\!u\,dt$ | inductor $L=C_M=1/k$ | capacitor $C=C_M=1/k$ |
| **dashpot** $R_M$ | $F=R_M(u_1-u_2)$ | resistor $r_M=1/R_M$ | resistor $R_M$ |
| given **velocity** source | | voltage source | current source |
| given **force** source | | current source | voltage source |

<Remember title="Why a mass is always grounded">

Newton's law holds in an *inertial* frame: the mass accelerates relative to the fixed ground, not relative to another mass. So a mass is a capacitor with **one terminal to ground** in the mobility analog. A spring or dashpot, by contrast, sees the **relative** velocity $u_1-u_2$ of its two ends, so it connects *between* two nodes (or from a node to ground if the other end is fixed to a wall).

</Remember>

<Callout kind="note" title="Compliance, not stiffness">

The handout uses the **compliance** $C_M=1/k$. In the impedance analog the spring is a capacitor whose *value* is $1/k$, not $k$ (the official solution to $\text{MT2 F2022 Q1a}$ labels one capacitor with the stiffness instead of the compliance).

</Callout>

## The recipe for the mobility analog (this is what you do on the exam)

1. **Nodes = absolute velocities** of the masses, plus **ground** (every fixed wall).
2. **Start with the force input** (the crib-sheet rule: "if there is a force input, start with that component"): a current source into the node of the mass it pushes.
3. **Each mass** → capacitor $M_M$ from its node to ground.
4. **Each spring/dashpot between two masses** → inductor $C_M$ / resistor $1/R_M$ between the two nodes (a spring and dashpot in parallel mechanically are in parallel electrically).
5. **Each spring/dashpot to a wall** → from the mass's node to ground.
6. Label every element and say *potential = velocity, flux = force* in your answer (marks are given for labels).
7. **Check the order:** number of storage elements (masses + springs) = number of states.

### Example 1: a single mass (the building block)

A mass $M$ held by a spring $k$ and dashpot $R_M$ to the wall, force $F$ on it.

**Mobility:** all three elements share the same velocity, so they are in *parallel* across the node, with the force as a current source.

### Example 2: two carts ($\text{MT2 W2012 Q1a}$)

Cart 1 (mass $M_1$, velocity $\dot p$) is pushed by force $u$ and joined to cart 2 (mass $M_2$, velocity $\dot q$) by a spring $k_1$ and dashpot $b_1$ in parallel; cart 2 is joined to the wall by $k_2$ and $b_2$ in parallel. Wheels frictionless.

1. Nodes $\dot p$, $\dot q$.
2. Force source $u$ into node $\dot p$.
3. $M_1$ from $\dot p$ to ground; $M_2$ from $\dot q$ to ground.
4. $k_1$ and $b_1$ **between** the nodes (parallel pair).
5. $k_2$, $b_2$ from $\dot q$ to ground.

The impedance analog is the dual: the shared elements ($b_1,k_1$) are shared between two loops, the force is a voltage source in the loop of cart 1.

## Levers, gears, pistons: transformers

A **lever** (arms $l_1,l_2$): moments $f_1l_1=f_2l_2$ and small displacements $u_1l_2=u_2l_1$. A transformer changes the *level* of the potential and flux but conserves power $f\!\cdot\!u$.

- **Mobility analog** ratio $(l_1/l_2):1$; **impedance analog** ratio $(l_2/l_1):1$ (reciprocal, because mobility = 1/impedance).
- Quantities seen through a transformer scale by the ratio **squared**: $\xi_{M1}=(l_1/l_2)^2\xi_{M2}$ and $Z_{M1}=(l_2/l_1)^2Z_{M2}$.
- **Gear trains** ($n$ = number of teeth/radius): mobility $(n_2/n_1):1$, impedance $(n_1/n_2):1$ (Anthony's crib sheet; the slide prints the same label on both, which is the inconsistency to be aware of). A load inertia $J_2$ seen from shaft 1 is $J_1^{eq}=J_1+(n_1/n_2)^2J_2$.
- **Piston (hydraulic/acoustic):** force $F=A\,p$, velocity-to-volume-flow $U=A\,u$: a transformer of ratio $A$ (Chapter 12).

### Example 3: a pivoted bar ($\text{MT2 F2022 Q1a}$ = HW5 Q5)

A uniform bar (mass $m$, length $L$) pivots about a point $L/4$ from its left end. Spring $k$ at the left end, damper $c$ and force $F(t)$ at the right end.

1. $I_O=I_G+m(L/4)^2=\tfrac{mL^2}{12}+\tfrac{mL^2}{16}=\dfrac{7mL^2}{48}$ (parallel-axis theorem; the centre of mass is $L/4$ from the pivot).
2. Spring at arm $L/4$, damper and force at arm $3L/4$: moments about the pivot

 $$\frac{7mL^2}{48}\ddot\theta+\frac{9cL^2}{16}\dot\theta+\frac{kL^2}{16}\theta=\frac{3L}4F .$$
3. **Mobility (potential $\dot\theta$, flux torque):** a torque source $3FL/4$ into one node; in parallel: capacitor $7mL^2/48$ (inertia), resistor $16/(9cL^2)$ (damper), inductor $16/(kL^2)$ (spring).
4. **Impedance:** one series loop: voltage source $3FL/4$, inductor $7mL^2/48$, resistor $9cL^2/16$, capacitor $16/(kL^2)$ (**compliance**, the reciprocal of the stiffness).
5. *Or keep the translations* $x=\tfrac L4\theta$ and $y=\tfrac{3L}4\theta$ and join the blocks by transformers with the arm ratios.

<Trap title="Lever mistakes">

Forgetting the parallel-axis term; squaring the arms in the elements but not in the force source (the source carries the *first* power of its arm); using the spring *stiffness* instead of *compliance* in the impedance analog.

</Trap>

## Transformer or gyrator? The one rule

When you couple two subsystems:

- both sides drawn in the **same** analog (both mobility, or both impedance) ⇒ **transformer** ($a/c=g$, $cb=d$, potential → potential);
- the two sides use **different** analogs (e.g. fluid in impedance form, a piston in mobility form) ⇒ **gyrator** (potential → flux: $a=cd$, $g=cb$).

*The motor and the loudspeaker* are the textbook examples: the armature circuit (impedance form: voltage, current) coupled to the shaft (mobility form: velocity, torque) through a **gyrator** with constant $K_t$, or through a **transformer** if you draw both sides in mobility form (Chapter 11).

## The dot method (converting between the two analogs)

For **planar** circuits only: (1) put a dot inside every loop and one outside, (2) connect the dots with lines that each cross exactly one element, (3) replace each crossed element by its dual (L ↔ C, R ↔ 1/R, series ↔ parallel, voltage source ↔ current source), (4) redraw. Useful when you drew one analog and the problem asks for the other.
