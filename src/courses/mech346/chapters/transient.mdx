# Transient conduction: lumped systems, the one-term solution and semi-infinite solids

<div className="lede">

Steady conduction asks "what is the temperature after a long time?". Transient conduction asks "how long does it take, and what is the temperature **now**?". Every Midterm 2 starts here: Question 1 is a lumped system, Question 2 is a one-term solution on a wall, cylinder, sphere or a combination of them. There are only three ideas: decide **if the body has a temperature gradient** (the Biot number), then either use a one-line exponential (lumped) or the one-term cosine / Bessel / sine solution, and use the **maximum possible heat** $Q_{max}=mc_p(T_i-T_\infty)$ as the yardstick for how much has been transferred.

</div>

<Idea title="The 30-second version">

1. $Bi=\dfrac{hL_c}{k}$ with $L_c=V/A_s$. If $Bi\le0.1$ the body is **isothermal** and
$$\frac{T(t)-T_\infty}{T_i-T_\infty}=e^{-bt},\quad b=\frac{hA_s}{\rho Vc_p}=\frac1{\tau_c}.$$
2. If $Bi>0.1$ the temperature depends on position as well as time. For $Fo=\alpha t/L^2>0.2$ **one term** of the exact series is enough: $\theta=A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2Fo}\times\{\cos(\lambda_1x/L),\ J_0(\lambda_1r/r_0),\ \sin(\lambda_1r/r_0)/(\lambda_1r/r_0)\}$, with $\lambda_1,A_1$ from Table 4-2 using $Bi=hL/k$ (here $L$ is the half-thickness or the **radius**).
3. Heat transferred: $Q/Q_{max}=1-\theta_0\dfrac{\sin\lambda_1}{\lambda_1}$ (wall), etc.
4. Short cylinders, bricks, hemispheres on a table: **multiply** the dimensionless temperatures of the one-dimensional solutions.

</Idea>

## Part 1: the lumped system

**When does a body behave as a single temperature?** Think of heat getting in through the surface and spreading through the interior. If conduction inside is fast compared with convection at the surface, the inside keeps up and stays nearly uniform. The ratio of the two resistances is the **Biot number**:

$$Bi=\frac{hL_c}{k}=\frac{L_c/k}{1/h}=\frac{\text{conduction resistance inside}}{\text{convection resistance at the surface}},\qquad L_c=\frac VA_s.$$

*Picture it as passengers boarding an island:* if the bridge from the car park to the ferry (the interior) is wide and the ferry (the surface film) is slow, everyone queues at the ferry and the car park stays full. A small copper ball in cool air ($Bi\ll1$) is uniform; a roast beef ($Bi\sim1$) is not. When $Bi\le0.1$ the temperature differences inside are within about 5 % of the overall difference, and the lumped model is accepted.

$L_c=V/A_s$ for common shapes: sphere $D/6=r_0/3$; long cylinder $D/4=r_0/2$; cube $a/6$; plane wall (both sides) half the thickness. For a finite cylinder compute $V$ and the **whole** surface area. **Always compute $L_c$ for the lumped model; do not use the radius** (that is for the exact solution).

### The lumped solution

Energy balance on the body, $hA_s(T_\infty-T)\,dt=\rho Vc_p\,dT$, gives

$$\frac{d(T-T_\infty)}{T-T_\infty}=-\frac{hA_s}{\rho Vc_p}dt\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{\frac{T(t)-T_\infty}{T_i-T_\infty}=e^{-bt}},\qquad b=\frac{hA_s}{\rho Vc_p}\ [\text{s}^{-1}],\ \ \tau_c=\frac1b=\frac{\rho Vc_p}{hA_s}.$$

- $\tau_c$ is the **time constant**: after one $\tau_c$ the temperature difference has fallen to 36.8 % of its starting value ($e^{-1}$); after $2\tau_c$ to 13.5 %; after $5\tau_c$ below 1 % ($e^{-5}=0.0067$): the body has "arrived" at $T_\infty$.
- $\tau_c$ is the product of a **thermal capacitance** $\rho Vc_p$ and a **convection resistance** $1/(hA_s)$: a big mass or a small $h$ means slow.
- Time to reach a given $T$: $t=-\dfrac1b\ln\dfrac{T-T_\infty}{T_i-T_\infty}$.
- Heat rate: $\dot Q(t)=hA_s[T(t)-T_\infty]$ (largest at the start); total heat $Q=mc_p[T(t)-T_i]$ (J); maximum $Q_{max}=mc_p(T_\infty-T_i)$, reached as $t\to\infty$. The **average** rate over a period is $Q/\Delta t$.

<Trap title="Use the right $L_c$, and say which Biot number">

The slides use two different characteristic lengths: $L_c=V/A_s$ for lumped analysis, and the half-thickness $L$ or the radius $r_0$ for the one-term solution. A sphere of diameter 30 cm has $L_c=r_0/3=\mathbf{0.05}$ m, **not** 0.025 m (the Nov 2023 student copy used 0.025 and lost the whole question).

</Trap>

### Worked example 1: annealing steel cylinders (Midterm 2, Nov 2022, Q1)

A carbon-steel cylinder ($\rho=7833$, $k=54$, $c_p=0.465$ kJ/kg·K), 8 mm in diameter and 10 mm high, is heated to 900 °C and allowed to cool to 100 °C in air at 35 °C with $h=75$ W/m²K. (a) Verify lumped analysis; (b) how long does it take; (c) the heat transferred (J) and the average rate.

**(a)** $V=\pi r^2H=\pi(0.004)^2(0.01)=5.027\times10^{-7}$ m³; $A_s=2\pi r^2+2\pi rH=2\pi(0.004)^2+2\pi(0.004)(0.01)=3.52\times10^{-4}$ m²; $L_c=V/A_s=0.00143$ m. 
$$Bi=\frac{hL_c}k=\frac{75(0.00143)}{54}=0.002\ll0.1\ \checkmark\ \text{(valid)}.$$

**(b)** Time constant $\tau_c=\dfrac{\rho c_pL_c}{h}=\dfrac{7833(465)(0.00143)}{75}=69.4$ s. 
$$\frac{100-35}{900-35}=0.0751=e^{-t/69.4}\;\Rightarrow\;t=69.4\ln\frac1{0.0751}=\mathbf{179.6\ s}.$$
(About $2.6\,\tau_c$: the temperature difference is 7.5 % of its start.)

**(c)** $Q=mc_p\Delta T=\rho Vc_p\Delta T=7833(5.027\times10^{-7})(465)(800)=\mathbf{1465\ J}$; the average rate is $1465/179.6=8.16$ W. (The instructor's note on the exam: the instantaneous rate is larger at the start, $\dot Q(0)=hA_s(900-35)=22.8$ W, and falls exponentially.)

## Part 2: the one-term solution

When $Bi>0.1$, solve the conduction equation $\partial^2T/\partial x^2=\alpha^{-1}\partial T/\partial t$ with the surface condition $-k\,\partial T/\partial x=h(T-T_\infty)$ and a symmetric initial profile. **Non-dimensionalise** (this is what makes the solution fit on one page):

$$\theta=\frac{T(x,t)-T_\infty}{T_i-T_\infty},\qquad X=\frac xL,\qquad Bi=\frac{hL}k,\qquad Fo=\tau=\frac{\alpha t}{L^2}.$$

Three dimensionless numbers replace eight dimensional parameters. The method of separation of variables gives an infinite series; the first eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ solves a characteristic equation depending only on $Bi$:

| Geometry | $\lambda_1$ solves | Characteristic length |
|---|---|---|
| plane wall, thickness $2L$ | $\lambda\tan\lambda=Bi$ | half-thickness $L$ |
| long cylinder | $\lambda J_1(\lambda)/J_0(\lambda)=Bi$ | radius $r_0$ |
| sphere | $1-\lambda\cot\lambda=Bi$ | radius $r_0$ |

The terms of the series die out like $e^{-\lambda_n^2Fo}$ with $\lambda_n$ growing, so for $Fo>0.2$ the **first term alone is within 2 %**. Here is the entire solution on a card (these are on the formula sheet):

| | Wall | Cylinder | Sphere |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\theta(x\ \text{or}\ r,t)$ | $A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}\cos(\lambda_1x/L)$ | $A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}J_0(\lambda_1r/r_0)$ | $A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}\dfrac{\sin(\lambda_1r/r_0)}{\lambda_1r/r_0}$ |
| centre $\theta_0$ | $A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}$ | same | same |
| $Q/Q_{max}$ | $1-\theta_0\dfrac{\sin\lambda_1}{\lambda_1}$ | $1-2\theta_0\dfrac{J_1(\lambda_1)}{\lambda_1}$ | $1-3\theta_0\dfrac{\sin\lambda_1-\lambda_1\cos\lambda_1}{\lambda_1^3}$ |

with $\lambda_1$ and $A_1$ from **Table 4-2** (given on the exam, along with the Bessel Table 4-3) for the relevant $Bi$. The positions relative to the centre ratios are
$\theta/\theta_0=\cos(\lambda_1x/L)$, $J_0(\lambda_1r/r_0)$, $\sin(\lambda_1r/r_0)/(\lambda_1r/r_0)$.

A few rows of Table 4-2 to build intuition ($\lambda_1$ for wall / cylinder / sphere): $Bi=0.1$: 0.311 / 0.442 / 0.542; $Bi=1$: 0.860 / 1.256 / 1.571; $Bi=10$: 1.429 / 2.179 / 2.855; $Bi\to\infty$: $\pi/2$ / 2.405 / $\pi$. For small $Bi$, $\lambda_1^2\approx Bi$ (wall), $2Bi$ (cylinder), $3Bi$ (sphere): then $\theta_0\approx e^{-Bi\,Fo\cdot\{1,2,3\}}=e^{-bt}$: **the lumped result is the small-$Bi$ limit** of the exact solution (check: $Bi\,Fo=hL/k\cdot\alpha t/L^2=ht/(\rho c_pL)$ and $b=h/(\rho c_pL_c)$ with $L_c=L,\ r_0/2,\ r_0/3$ ✔).

### Physical reading of $Bi$ and $Fo$

- **$Bi\to0$:** uniform temperature, decays at the rate set by the surface (lumped).
- **$Bi\to\infty$:** the surface instantly takes the fluid's temperature; the interior follows at the pace of conduction alone, controlled by $\alpha$.
- **$Fo$** is dimensionless time: the ratio of the heat conducted through the body to the heat stored in it. $Fo>0.2$ means "time enough for the heat to have reached the centre"; before that the one-term solution is not valid and you need the charts or the semi-infinite model.

### The backward approach (the instructor's tip)

Ask "**what do I need to find, and which equation gives it?**" and work back: *to find $T$ at a place and time*, I need $\theta=A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}\times(\text{shape factor})$; this needs $A_1,\lambda_1$ (from $Bi$ and Table 4-2) and $\tau=\alpha t/L^2$; $Bi$ needs the **right** $L$; check $\tau>0.2$ at the end. *To find the total heat:* $Q/Q_{max}$, so I need $\theta_0$, then $Q_{max}=\rho Vc_p(T_i-T_\infty)$ with the **volume of the actual body** (not of the idealised symmetric one).

### Worked example 2: a short brass cylinder (Midterm 2, Nov 2022, Q2)

A brass cylinder $D=6$ cm, $H=6$ cm, $T_i=120$ °C, stands on a table (heat exchange with the table ignored, so the base is insulated) in air at 25 °C with $h=60$ W/m²K. $k=90$, $\alpha=33.9\times10^{-6}$, $\rho=8530$, $c_p=0.380$ kJ/kg·K. Find (a) the temperature at the **centre of the top surface** after 15 min, (b) the total heat transfer.

**Think.** Two effects: heat leaves through the **curved side** (radial, a long cylinder of $r_0=3$ cm) and through the **top** (axial, a wall). With the base insulated, the base is a plane of symmetry, so the body is the half of a plane wall of thickness $2L$ with **$L=H=6$ cm**, and the centre of the top is the *surface* of that wall ($x=L$) on the axis of the cylinder ($r=0$). The short cylinder is the **intersection** of the two one-dimensional bodies, so
$$\theta_{short\ cyl}(x,r,t)=\theta_{wall}(x,t)\times\theta_{cyl}(r,t).$$

**(a)** Wall: $Bi_w=hL/k=60(0.06)/90=0.040$; Table 4-2: $\lambda_1=0.1987$, $A_1=1.0066$; $\tau_w=\alpha t/L^2=33.9\times10^{-6}(900)/0.06^2=8.475>0.2$ ✓. 
$\theta_{wall}(x=L)=A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}\cos(\lambda_1)=1.0066\,e^{-0.3346}\cos(0.1987)=0.7062$. 
Cylinder: $Bi_c=hr_0/k=60(0.03)/90=0.020$; $\lambda_1=0.1995$, $A_1=1.0050$; $\tau_c=33.9\times10^{-6}(900)/0.03^2=33.9$ ✓. $\theta_{cyl}(r=0)=A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}=1.0050\,e^{-1.349}=0.2607$. 
$$\theta=0.7062(0.2607)=0.1841,\quad T=T_\infty+\theta(T_i-T_\infty)=25+0.1841(95)=\mathbf{42.5\ ^\circ C}.$$
(The instructor's answer is 42.48 °C, using the table-rounded roots.)

**(b)** Maximum heat: $Q_{max}=\rho Vc_p(T_i-T_\infty)$ with $V=\pi r_0^2H=\pi(0.03)^2(0.06)=1.696\times10^{-4}$ m³ (the **actual** half-body): $8530(1.696\times10^{-4})(380)(95)=52\,240$ J.

Fractions of the maximum for each 1-D solution, with the centre values $\theta_{0,w}=1.0066\,e^{-0.3346}=0.7204$ and $\theta_{0,c}=0.2607$:
- wall: $q_w=1-\theta_{0,w}\dfrac{\sin\lambda_1}{\lambda_1}=1-0.7204(0.9934)=0.2844$;
- cylinder: $q_c=1-2\theta_{0,c}\dfrac{J_1(\lambda_1)}{\lambda_1}=1-2(0.2607)(0.4975)=0.7406$.

Combining the two one-dimensional solutions:
$$\frac Q{Q_{max}}=q_w+q_c(1-q_w)=0.2844+0.7406(0.7156)=0.8143,\qquad Q=0.8143(52\,240)=\mathbf{42.5\ kJ}.$$

<Callout kind="note" title="Why the fractions multiply like that">

If a fraction $q_1$ of the maximum has already been transferred by the wall effect, only the remainder $1-q_1$ is left for the radial effect to remove, and the radial effect removes its own fraction $q_2$ of *that*. Same logic as two filters in series.

</Callout>

### Worked example 3: the sphere that must be lumped (Midterm 2, Nov 2023, Q1)

A metal sphere ($\rho=7833$, $c_p=0.465$ kJ/kg·K) of diameter 30 cm is annealed: heated to 900 °C, then cooled to 100 °C in air at 35 °C with $h=75$ W/m²K. (a) The minimum $k$ for the sphere to be treated as lumped; (b) the cooling time; (c) the average heat rate.

**(a)** $L_c=V/A_s=r_0/3=0.15/3=0.05$ m. $Bi\le0.1\Rightarrow k\ge\dfrac{hL_c}{0.1}=\dfrac{75(0.05)}{0.1}=\mathbf{37.5\ W/m\cdot K}$.

**(b)** $b=\dfrac{hA_s}{\rho Vc_p}=\dfrac{h}{\rho c_pL_c}=\dfrac{75}{7833(465)(0.05)}=4.118\times10^{-4}$ s⁻¹, $\tau_c=2428$ s. $t=\tau_c\ln\dfrac{900-35}{100-35}=2428(2.588)=\mathbf{6285\ s}$ (104.8 min).

**(c)** $Q=\rho Vc_p(900-100)=7833(0.014137)(465)(800)=41.19\times10^6$ J; average rate $=Q/t=\mathbf{6.55\ kW}$.

<Callout kind="warn" title="⚠ Graded student copy: an $L_c$ slip that propagates">

The scan shows $L_c=0.025$ m (half the correct value), which gives $k\ge18.75$, $t=3143$ s (52 min), $Q=25.7\times10^6$ J and $\dot Q=8176$ W; the grader marked 3.0/4.5. The method is right; $V/A_s=\dfrac{\frac43\pi r_0^3}{4\pi r_0^2}=\dfrac{r_0}3=0.05$ m. Also note that **$Q$ (J) does not depend on $L_c$**; the student's 25.7 MJ shows a mass slip too.

### Worked example 4: a brass hemisphere on a table (Midterm 2, Nov 2023, Q2)

A brass hemisphere, $D=60$ cm, $T_i=120$ °C, on a table (base insulated) in air at 25 °C with $h=6$ W/m²K; $k=2$ W/m·K, $\alpha=33.9\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $\rho=8530$, $c_p=0.380$ kJ/kg·K. After 15 min find (a) the temperature at the top, (b) at the centre of the flat bottom surface, (c) the total heat transferred.

**Think.** An insulated flat base is a plane of symmetry: the hemisphere behaves **exactly** as one half of a full sphere of the same radius $r_0=0.3$ m. So use the sphere solution, with the centre of the sphere = centre of the bottom face, and the apex of the dome at $r=r_0$.

$Bi=hr_0/k=6(0.3)/2=0.9$; Table 4-2 (sphere): $\lambda_1=1.5044$, $A_1=1.2488$. $\tau=\alpha t/r_0^2=33.9\times10^{-6}(900)/0.09=0.339>0.2$ ✓. 
$$\theta_0=A_1e^{-\lambda_1^2\tau}=1.2488\,e^{-0.767}=0.5798.$$
**(a)** Top (apex, $r=r_0$): $\theta=\theta_0\dfrac{\sin\lambda_1}{\lambda_1}=0.5798(0.6632)=0.3845$, $T=25+0.3845(95)=\mathbf{61.5\ ^\circ C}$. 
**(b)** Centre of the bottom: $T=25+0.5798(95)=\mathbf{80.1\ ^\circ C}$. 
**(c)** $\dfrac Q{Q_{max}}=1-3\theta_0\dfrac{\sin\lambda_1-\lambda_1\cos\lambda_1}{\lambda_1^3}=1-3(0.5798)(0.2637)=0.541$, with $Q_{max}=\rho Vc_p\Delta T$ using the **hemisphere's** volume $V=\tfrac23\pi r_0^3=0.05655$ m³: $Q_{max}=8530(0.05655)(380)(95)=17.4$ MJ. 
$$Q=0.541(17.4\ \text{MJ})=\mathbf{9.4\ MJ}=9400\ \text{kJ}.$$
*(The graded student copy has the right temperatures, 61.58 and 80.08 °C, but a total of 31 401 kJ: more than the entire heat content of the body, a sign that $V$ was wrong. Always sanity-check $Q<Q_{max}$.)*

## Multidimensional bodies: the product solution

Bodies that are intersections of simple shapes: a **short cylinder** (wall × long cylinder), a **rectangular bar** (wall × wall), a **brick** (wall × wall × wall), a **semi-infinite cylinder** (semi-infinite × cylinder). The dimensionless temperature is the **product**,
$$\theta_{2D}=\theta_1\theta_2,\qquad\theta_{3D}=\theta_1\theta_2\theta_3,$$
and the heat fractions combine as in the box above: $q_{2D}=q_1+q_2(1-q_1)$, $q_{3D}=q_1+q_2(1-q_1)+q_3(1-q_1)(1-q_2)$. Use each 1-D solution with **its own** $Bi$, $Fo$ and characteristic length (half-thickness for a wall, radius for a cylinder).

## Part 3: the semi-infinite solid

If a thick body is heated or cooled from one surface for a **short** time, the disturbance never reaches the far side and the body acts as if it extended to infinity. The thermal **penetration depth** is about $\delta\approx2\sqrt{\alpha t}$. For $x\gg\delta$ the temperature is still the initial one. Examples: the ground under a pipe in winter, a thick wall exposed to fire, skin touching a hot pan.

| Surface condition | $\dfrac{T(x,t)-T_i}{\ldots}$ |
|---|---|
| constant surface temperature $T_s$ | $\dfrac{T-T_s}{T_i-T_s}=\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right)$ |
| constant surface flux $\dot q_0$ | $T-T_i=\dfrac{2\dot q_0\sqrt{\alpha t/\pi}}{k}\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{x^2}{4\alpha t}\right)-\dfrac{\dot q_0x}{k}\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\dfrac x{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right)$ |
| convection $(h,T_\infty)$ | $\dfrac{T-T_i}{T_\infty-T_i}=\operatorname{erfc}(\eta)-\exp\!\left(\dfrac{hx}k+\dfrac{h^2\alpha t}{k^2}\right)\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\eta+\dfrac{h\sqrt{\alpha t}}k\right)$, $\eta=\dfrac x{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}$ |

*Penetration examples.* Soil ($\alpha=0.15\times10^{-6}$ m²/s): after one day the cold wave of a cold night has gone $2\sqrt{\alpha t}=0.23$ m; after 90 days of winter, **2.2 m**: that is why water mains are buried 2 m deep.

**Contact of two semi-infinite solids.** When two bodies at $T_A$ and $T_B$ touch, the interface instantly sits at

$$T_s=\frac{e_AT_A+e_BT_B}{e_A+e_B},\qquad e=\sqrt{k\rho c_p}\ \text{(thermal effusivity)}.$$

A high-effusivity material (copper, 37 100) dominates: touching a 100 °C copper block with skin at 35 °C gives a contact temperature of **98 °C** (it burns); touching 100 °C wood ($e=357$) gives **50 °C** (it does not). That is why metal feels colder or hotter than wood at the same temperature.
