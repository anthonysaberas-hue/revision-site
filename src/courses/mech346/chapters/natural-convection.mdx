# Natural (free) convection

<div className="lede">

No fan, no pump, yet air still moves over a hot radiator, a coffee cup, a steam pipe and a laptop. The fluid next to a hot surface warms, becomes lighter, and **rises**; cooler fluid slides in to replace it. Gravity does the pumping, and the "pump" is the density difference. This chapter is the same four-step routine as before, with one change: the dimensionless group that decides the regime is the **Rayleigh number** $Ra$, which replaces $Re$.

</div>

<Idea title="The 30-second version">

1. Film temperature $T_f=(T_s+T_\infty)/2$ (in kelvin for gases, since $\beta=1/T_f$ for an ideal gas).
2. $Ra_L=\dfrac{g\beta\,\vert T_s-T_\infty\vert L_c^3}{\nu\alpha}=Gr_L\,Pr$ with $\beta=\dfrac1{T_f}$ (ideal gas), $L_c$ from the geometry.
3. Find $Nu=f(Ra,Pr)$ from the table of correlations: vertical plate, horizontal plate (up or down), horizontal cylinder, sphere, enclosure.
4. $h=Nu\,k/L_c$, then $\dot Q=hA_s(T_s-T_\infty)$, **plus radiation** (comparable in size for gases!).
5. $Ra<10^9$: laminar boundary layer; $Ra>10^9$ (vertical plate): turbulent.

</Idea>

## The physical mechanism: buoyancy

A fluid element that is warmer than its surroundings is less dense (for most fluids), so a net upward **buoyancy force** acts on it: $\rho g$ weighs the cold fluid, the warm one weighs less. The difference between the two drives a flow. The strength of the effect is the **volume expansion coefficient**
$$\beta=-\frac1\rho\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}\right)_P\approx-\frac1\rho\frac{\rho_\infty-\rho}{T_\infty-T},$$
which for an **ideal gas** is simply $\beta=1/T$ (kelvin). For water $\beta\approx2\times10^{-4}$ K⁻¹ at 20 °C (much smaller than air's $3.4\times10^{-3}$), so water at the same $\Delta T$ is driven less per unit volume, but its much lower $\nu$ and $\alpha$ raise $Ra$ anyway.

On a **vertical hot plate** the layer begins as laminar at the bottom (thin, $h$ large), thickens as it rises ($h\propto x^{-1/4}$, falling), and becomes turbulent above $Ra_x\approx10^9$ (where $h$ becomes roughly constant, $h\propto x^0$). Velocity is zero at the wall (no-slip) **and** zero far away; it peaks within the layer. That is a difference from forced convection, where the free-stream velocity is imposed.

## The dimensionless numbers

- **Grashof number** $Gr_L=\dfrac{g\beta(T_s-T_\infty)L_c^3}{\nu^2}$: buoyancy over viscous force (the natural-convection analogue of $Re^2$).
- **Rayleigh number** $Ra_L=Gr_L\,Pr=\dfrac{g\beta(T_s-T_\infty)L_c^3}{\nu\alpha}$: the product that appears in all the correlations.
- The **Gr/Re² test** for mixed convection: $Gr/Re^2\ll1$ (below about 0.1) means forced convection only; $\gg1$ (above about 10) means natural only; in between, both matter.

$Ra\sim L_c^3\,\Delta T$: a thirty-fold change in size changes $Ra$ by $27\,000$, so small objects (wires, electronics) have small $Ra$ and thick laminar layers, and big walls have huge $Ra$ and turbulence.

## Correlations (all with properties at $T_f$)

| Geometry | $L_c$ | Correlation | Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertical plate/cylinder (all $Ra$) | $L$ (height) | $Nu=\left\{0.825+\dfrac{0.387Ra^{1/6}}{[1+(0.492/Pr)^{9/16}]^{8/27}}\right\}^2$ | any $Ra$ |
| Vertical plate, laminar | $L$ | $Nu=0.68+\dfrac{0.67Ra^{1/4}}{[1+(0.492/Pr)^{9/16}]^{4/9}}$ | $Ra<10^9$ |
| Horizontal plate, hot face **up** (or cold face down) | $A_s/p$ | $Nu=0.54Ra^{1/4}$; $Nu=0.15Ra^{1/3}$ | $10^4$–$10^7$; $10^7$–$10^{11}$ |
| Horizontal plate, hot face **down** (or cold face up) | $A_s/p$ | $Nu=0.27Ra^{1/4}$ | $10^5$–$10^{11}$ |
| Horizontal cylinder | $D$ | $Nu=\left\{0.6+\dfrac{0.387Ra^{1/6}}{[1+(0.559/Pr)^{9/16}]^{8/27}}\right\}^2$ | $Ra\le10^{12}$ |
| Sphere | $D$ | $Nu=2+\dfrac{0.589Ra^{1/4}}{[1+(0.469/Pr)^{9/16}]^{4/9}}$ | $Ra\le10^{11}$, $Pr\ge0.7$ |

**Why "hot face up" is better:** the hot air rising from a plate facing up leaves freely; under a plate facing down it is trapped against the surface and has to slide sideways. So $h$ is higher (0.54 vs 0.27): in the worked example below, **up is twice as good**.

**Inclined plates** (angle $\theta$ from the vertical, $0<\theta<60^\circ$): use the vertical plate equation with $g\to g\cos\theta$ in $Ra$.

Older textbooks give simpler single-power forms $Nu=C\,Ra^n$ with a table of $C$ and $n$; the Churchill–Chu forms above are the more accurate versions. Use whichever the formula sheet provides.

### Enclosures (the air gap in a window or a wall)

In a gap of thickness $L$ between a hot and a cold surface, use $Ra_L$ with the **gap** as length and $\Delta T=T_1-T_2$. The result is an **effective conductivity** $k_{eff}=Nu\,k$, so $\dot Q=k_{eff}A\dfrac{T_1-T_2}L=Nu\cdot(\text{conduction value})$.

- $Ra<1708$: no motion, pure conduction, $Nu=1$. (Stable layering: warm above cold.)
- Horizontal gap heated from below (unstable): cells form; $Nu=0.195Ra^{1/4}$ ($10^4<Ra<4\times10^5$), $Nu=0.068Ra^{1/3}$ (above). Heated from above: stable, $Nu=1$.
- **Vertical gap** (window): the air rises on the hot side and falls on the cold side. $Nu=0.42\,Ra^{1/4}Pr^{0.012}(H/L)^{-0.3}$ for $10<H/L<40$ and $10^4<Ra<10^7$; $Nu=0.046Ra^{1/3}$ for $10^6<Ra<10^9$ (aspect ratio $1$–$40$).
- **Design insight:** widening the gap increases $Ra\propto L^3$, so the air starts to circulate, and beyond a point does not insulate more. A double-pane window gap of 1–2 cm is optimal for air. *(Argon has a lower conductivity than air, so an argon-filled gap insulates a little better.)*

### Heat sinks and fin arrays (Elenbaas)

For vertical parallel plates (a finned heat sink with natural convection), the boundary layers on neighbouring fins merge if the spacing is too small. The **optimum fin spacing** that maximizes the heat from the whole array is $S_{opt}\approx2.7\,L/Ra_L^{1/4}$ (Cengel's formula with $Ra$ based on $L$). Too wide: few fins. Too narrow: the layers choke the flow.

## Natural convection plus radiation

At room temperature, radiation and natural convection from the same surface are **comparable** ($h_{conv}\approx3$–$8$ W/m²K and $h_{rad}\approx5$–$7$ W/m²K for a body at $\varepsilon\approx0.9$). Whenever the surface has high emissivity and the surroundings are cool, the total heat loss is the sum of both:
$$\dot Q=hA_s(T_s-T_\infty)+\varepsilon\sigma A_s(T_s^4-T_{surr}^4).$$
Ignoring radiation underestimates a steam pipe's loss by a factor of two (see Example 2). A **low-emissivity** coating (bright foil, $\varepsilon\approx0.05$) kills the second term, which is how thermos flasks and double-glazed low-E windows work.

<Trap title="Natural convection: the three most common errors">

1. Using $\beta=1/T$ with $T$ in °C. Always kelvin.
2. Wrong characteristic length: **vertical plate: height**; **cylinder: diameter**; **horizontal plate: $A_s/p$** (for a square of side $a$ that is $a/4$).
3. Forgetting that the horizontal plate's **orientation** (hot face up vs down) changes the correlation.

</Trap>

## Worked example 1: a vertical wall of a hot oven

A 2 m tall wall is at 40 °C in still 20 °C air. Find the convection coefficient and the heat flux.

1. $T_f=30$ °C $=303.15$ K. Air: $k=0.0266$ W/mK, $\nu=1.605\times10^{-5}$ m²/s, $\alpha=2.27\times10^{-5}$, $Pr=0.707$, $\beta=1/303.15=3.30\times10^{-3}$ K⁻¹.
2. $Ra=\dfrac{g\beta\Delta TL^3}{\nu\alpha}=\dfrac{9.81(3.30\times10^{-3})(20)(8)}{(1.605\times10^{-5})(2.27\times10^{-5})}=1.42\times10^{10}$. Greater than $10^9$: **turbulent**; use the all-$Ra$ equation.
3. $Nu=\{0.825+0.387(1.42\times10^{10})^{1/6}/[1+(0.492/0.707)^{9/16}]^{8/27}\}^2=\mathbf{281.8}$.
4. $h=Nu\,k/L=281.8(0.0266)/2=\mathbf{3.75\ W/m^2K}$, and $q''=3.75(20)=\mathbf{75\ W/m^2}$ (a 2 m wall of width 3 m loses 450 W by convection).

*Physical check: still air would give $Nu=1$ ($h=0.013$). Free convection makes it 280 times better, but $h=3.75$ is still tiny compared with forced convection (10–100).*

## Worked example 2: a bare steam pipe (convection plus radiation)

A horizontal pipe of $D=10$ cm and surface temperature 100 °C is in room air at 20 °C; $\varepsilon=0.8$, walls at 20 °C. Heat loss per metre?

1. $T_f=60$ °C: $k=0.0288$, $\nu=1.897\times10^{-5}$, $Pr=0.703$, $\beta=1/333.15$.
2. $Ra_D=\dfrac{9.81(1/333.15)(80)(0.1)^3}{(1.897\times10^{-5})(2.697\times10^{-5})}=4.61\times10^6$.
3. Churchill–Chu (cylinder): $Nu=22.5$; $h=22.5(0.0288)/0.1=\mathbf{6.47\ W/m^2K}$.
4. Convection: $\dot Q'=hA'(\Delta T)=6.47(\pi\cdot0.1)(80)=163$ W/m.
5. Radiation: $\dot Q'=0.8(5.67\times10^{-8})(\pi\cdot0.1)(373.15^4-293.15^4)=171$ W/m, $h_{rad}=6.8$ W/m²K.
6. **Total $=334$ W/m.** Radiation is **51 %** of the loss: ignoring it halves the answer.

*This is why steam pipes are insulated and wrapped in shiny foil: the foil cuts the radiation term.*

## Worked example 3: hot plate, face up or face down

A square plate $0.5\times0.5$ m at 80 °C sits in 20 °C air. Compare heat loss by natural convection from the **upper face** (hot face up) with the **lower face** (hot face down).

1. $L_c=A_s/p=a^2/(4a)=0.125$ m. $T_f=50$ °C: $k=0.0281$, $\nu=1.797\times10^{-5}$, $\alpha=2.552\times10^{-5}$, $\beta=1/323.15$; $\Delta T=60$.
2. $Ra=\dfrac{9.81(3.095\times10^{-3})(60)(0.125)^3}{(1.797\times10^{-5})(2.552\times10^{-5})}=7.76\times10^6$ (just below $10^7$).
3. Face up: $Nu=0.54Ra^{1/4}=28.5$, $h=28.5(0.0281)/0.125=\mathbf{6.40}$ W/m²K, $\dot Q=6.40(0.25)(60)=\mathbf{96}$ W.
4. Face down: $Nu=0.27Ra^{1/4}=14.2$, $h=\mathbf{3.20}$, $\dot Q=\mathbf{48}$ W. 

*Face up loses twice as much heat: this is why a CPU board is cooled better lying flat with the heat sink on top than with it hanging underneath.*

## Worked example 4: the air gap in a double-pane window

Two panes $H=0.6$ m tall are $L=2$ cm apart, at 18 °C and 6 °C. $T_f=12$ °C: $k=0.0253$, $\nu=1.438\times10^{-5}$, $Pr=0.709$.

1. $Ra_L=\dfrac{9.81(1/285.15)(12)(0.02)^3}{(1.438\times10^{-5})(2.029\times10^{-5})}=1.13\times10^4$ (above 1708: air does move). $H/L=30$.
2. $Nu=0.42Ra^{1/4}Pr^{0.012}(H/L)^{-0.3}=0.42(10.31)(1.004)(0.360)=1.56$ (the circulation raises the heat flow 56 % above pure conduction).
3. $q''=Nu\,\dfrac{k\Delta T}L=1.56\dfrac{0.0253(12)}{0.02}=\mathbf{23.6\ W/m^2}$ (conduction alone: 15.2 W/m²). Plus radiation across the gap (see Chapter 13): for glass with $\varepsilon=0.84$ that is far larger, about 46 W/m², hence low-E coatings.
