<Problem id="P3b-1" title="Rectangular aluminum fin: three tip models compared" tags="adiabatic tip, convective tip, corrected length, efficiency, effectiveness">

<Statement>

A rectangular aluminum fin ($k=200$ W/mK) has thickness $t=3$ mm, width $w=10$ cm, length $L=3$ cm. The base is at 120 °C, the air at 25 °C, $h=40$ W/m²K. Find $m$, the heat rate with (a) an adiabatic tip, (b) a convective tip, (c) the corrected length with an adiabatic tip, (d) the infinitely-long-fin formula, and the efficiency and effectiveness.

</Statement>

<Think>

$p=2(w+t)$, $A_c=wt$, $m=\sqrt{hp/kA_c}$, $\dot Q_{ad}=\sqrt{hpkA_c}\,\theta_b\tanh(mL)$. The corrected-length rule $L_c=L+A_c/p$ should reproduce the convective-tip result.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Geometry and fin parameter">

$A_c=0.1(0.003)=3\times10^{-4}$ m²; $p=2(0.1+0.003)=0.206$ m. $m=\sqrt{\dfrac{40(0.206)}{200(3\times10^{-4})}}=\sqrt{137.3}=11.72$ m⁻¹; $mL=0.352$ (a **short** fin).

</Step>
<Step title="2. The prefactor">

$\sqrt{hpkA_c}\,\theta_b=\sqrt{40(0.206)(200)(3\times10^{-4})}\,(95)=\sqrt{0.4944}(95)=0.7031(95)=66.80$ W.

</Step>
<Step title="3. (a) Adiabatic tip">

$\dot Q=66.80\tanh(0.352)=66.80(0.3377)=22.56$ W.

</Step>
<Step title="4. (b) Convective tip">

With $\dfrac h{mk}=\dfrac{40}{11.72(200)}=0.01706$: $\dot Q=66.80\dfrac{\sinh mL+0.01706\cosh mL}{\cosh mL+0.01706\sinh mL}=23.57$ W.

</Step>
<Step title="5. (c) Corrected length">

$L_c=L+\dfrac{A_c}p=0.03+0.001456=0.03146$ m: $\dot Q=66.80\tanh(11.72(0.03146))=23.57$ W: **identical** to the convective tip to four digits.

</Step>
<Step title="6. (d) Infinite fin">

$\dot Q=66.80$ W: an **183 % over-estimate** of the correct value (23.6 W), because $mL<1$: this fin is much too short to be "infinite".

</Step>
<Step title="7. Efficiency and effectiveness (adiabatic-tip model)">

$\eta=\dfrac{\tanh mL}{mL}=\dfrac{0.3377}{0.3516}=0.960$ (equivalently $\dot Q/(hpL\theta_b)$ with $pL=0.00618$ m²); $\varepsilon=\dfrac{\dot Q}{hA_c\theta_b}=\dfrac{22.56}{40(3\times10^{-4})(95)}=19.8$.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$m=11.72$ m⁻¹; $\dot Q=22.6$ W (adiabatic), 23.6 W (convective tip = corrected length); infinite fin 66.8 W (wrong); $\varepsilon\approx20$.**

</Answer>

<Traps>

- The infinite-fin formula needs $mL\gtrsim3$; always compute $mL$ first.
- The corrected length reproduces the convective-tip result and avoids the long hyperbolic formula: in exams use it.

</Traps>

</Problem>

<Problem id="P3b-2" title="Thermometer well: error from fin conduction" tags="fin as a measurement error, 1/cosh(mL)">

<Statement>

A steel rod (diameter 6 mm, $k=15$ W/mK, protruding 80 mm into a gas) is used as a thermometer well. The pipe wall is at 60 °C; the gas is at 200 °C with $h=100$ W/m²K. The tip is adiabatic. What temperature does a sensor at the tip read?

</Statement>

<Think>

The well is a fin: heat flows down the rod to the cold wall, so the tip is a little cooler than the gas. For an adiabatic tip $\dfrac{T(L)-T_\infty}{T_b-T_\infty}=\dfrac1{\cosh mL}$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. $m$ and $mL$">

$m=\sqrt{\dfrac{4h}{kD}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{4(100)}{15(0.006)}}=66.67$ m⁻¹; $mL=66.67(0.08)=5.33$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Tip temperature">

$\cosh5.33=103.6$: $T_{tip}=200-\dfrac{200-60}{103.6}=198.65$ °C.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**Reads 198.6 °C: an error of 1.4 K.** To lower it: lengthen the well, use a poor conductor (smaller $k$) and thin walls, or insulate the pipe near the well (raise $T_b$).

</Answer>

</Problem>

<Problem id="P3b-3" title="A finned heat sink: overall heat rate and effectiveness" tags="fin array, base area, efficiency">

<Statement>

A heat sink with a 10 cm × 10 cm base carries 11 thin rectangular fins ($k=180$ W/mK) of thickness 1.5 mm and height 2 cm, 10 cm long. Base 70 °C, air 25 °C, $h=60$ W/m²K. Find the total heat rate, and the ratio to the bare base.

</Statement>

<Think>

Thin fin: $m=\sqrt{2h/kt}$. Use the corrected length $L_c=L+t/2$ for the tip. Total $=$ fins $+$ unfinned base.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Fin parameter and efficiency">

$m=\sqrt{\dfrac{2(60)}{180(0.0015)}}=21.08$ m⁻¹; $L_c=0.02+0.00075=0.02075$; $mL_c=0.4374$; $\eta=\dfrac{\tanh0.4374}{0.4374}=0.941$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Areas">

Fin area (both sides, corrected length): $A_{fin}=11(2)(0.1)(0.02075)=0.04565$ m². Unfinned base: $0.1(0.1)-11(0.0015)(0.1)=0.01-0.00165=0.00835$ m².

</Step>
<Step title="3. Heat">

$\dot Q=h\Delta T(\eta A_{fin}+A_{unf})=60(45)(0.941\cdot0.04565+0.00835)=2700(0.05131)=138.5$ W. Bare base: $60(45)(0.01)=27$ W.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$\dot Q=138$ W, 5.1× the bare base.**

</Answer>

<Traps>

If the fins are crowded (spacing under about the boundary-layer thickness) $h$ drops: the extra area would not give a proportional gain.

</Traps>

</Problem>

<Problem id="P3b-4" title="How long must a fin be?" tags="design, tanh(mL)">

<Statement>

For the fin of P3b-1 (m = 11.72 m⁻¹) what length makes it deliver 99 % of the heat of an infinitely long fin? Is it worth building it that long?

</Statement>

<Think>

$\dot Q/\dot Q_\infty=\tanh(mL)=0.99\Rightarrow mL=\tanh^{-1}(0.99)=2.65$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Length">

$L=\dfrac{2.65}{11.72}=0.226$ m.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Judgement">

At $mL=1$ the fin already delivers 76 %, at $mL=2$ 96 %; but the material grows linearly with $L$ while the heat flattens. The **efficiency** falls: $\eta=\tanh(mL)/mL=0.37$ at $mL=2.65$. A short fin with high efficiency, or more fins, is a better use of metal.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$L=22.6$ cm for 99 %, but a design near $mL\approx1$–$1.5$ is the practical optimum.**

</Answer>

</Problem>
