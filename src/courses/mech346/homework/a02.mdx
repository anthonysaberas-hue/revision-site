<Problem id="2-19" title="Solar pond: total heat generated by an exponentially decaying source" tags="integrate the generation over the volume">

<Statement>

In a solar pond the absorbed solar energy is modelled as heat generation $\dot e_{gen}=\dot e_0e^{-bx}$, where $x$ is the depth measured down from the top surface, $\dot e_0$ is the rate of absorption per unit volume at the top and $b$ is a constant. Obtain the total rate of heat generation in a water layer of surface area $A$ and thickness $L$ at the top of the pond.

</Statement>

<Think>

Nothing to solve: the generation depends on depth only, so slice the layer into sheets of thickness $dx$ (volume $A\,dx$) and add them up: $\dot E=\int\dot e\,dV$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="Your steps">

$\dot E=\displaystyle\int\dot e\,dV=\int_0^L\dot e_0e^{-bx}A\,dx=\dot e_0A\left[\frac{e^{-bx}}{-b}\right]_0^L=\frac{\dot e_0A}{-b}\left(e^{-bL}-1\right)$.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$$\dot E_{gen}=\frac{\dot e_0A}{b}\left(1-e^{-bL}\right)\quad\text{(your form }\tfrac{\dot e_0A}{-b}(e^{-bL}-1)\text{ is identical).}$$

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="✔ Matches your notes. What it teaches">

For **non-uniform generation** the total is an integral, never $\dot e\,V$. Limits to check: $b\to0$ gives $\dot e_0AL$ (uniform); $bL\gg1$ gives $\dot e_0A/b$ (all the energy is absorbed in the first $1/b$ of depth).

</Callout>

</Problem>

<Problem id="2-43" title="Steel pan on an electric range: formulate, do not solve" tags="differential equation, flux boundary condition, convection boundary condition">

<Statement>

The bottom of a steel pan is $L=0.5$ cm thick and $D=20$ cm in diameter. The electric unit consumes 1250 W and 85 % of this heat is transferred uniformly to the pan. Heat leaves the top of the bottom section to the water by convection with coefficient $h$. Assuming constant $k$ and one-dimensional steady conduction, write the differential equation and the boundary conditions. Do not solve. ($x=0$ at the heated underside, $x=L$ at the water side.)

</Statement>

<Think>

Steady, 1-D, constant $k$, no generation inside the pan: $\dfrac{d^2T}{dx^2}=0$. The heater side has a **prescribed heat flux**, the water side a **convection** condition. The flux is the heat rate over the pan-bottom area.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="Your steps">

$\dot Q=0.85(1250)$ W; $q''_0=\dfrac{\dot Q}{A}=\dfrac{0.85(1250)}{\pi(0.20/2)^2}=33\,820$ W/m². Boundary conditions written in your notes: $-kA\dfrac{dT(0)}{dx}=\dot Q$ at $x=0$ (flux in), and $-kA\dfrac{dT(L)}{dx}=hA\,[T(L)-T_\infty]$ at $x=L$.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$$\frac{d^2T}{dx^2}=0,\qquad -k\frac{dT}{dx}\Big\vert_{x=0}=\frac{0.85(1250)}{\pi D^2/4}=33\,820\ \text{W/m}^2,\qquad -k\frac{dT}{dx}\Big\vert_{x=L}=h\left[T(L)-T_\infty\right].$$

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="✔ Matches your notes. What it teaches">

A boundary condition is just an **energy balance on the surface**. Note the sign convention for $x$ pointing from the heater toward the water: heat flows in $+x$, so $-k\,dT/dx>0$ at both ends. The "85 %" is the part of the electric power that actually enters the pan, so it goes in the flux, not in $h$.

</Callout>

</Problem>

<Problem id="2-48" title="Water pipe wrapped with an electric heater: formulate, do not solve" tags="cylindrical equation, flux on the outer surface, convection on the inner surface">

<Statement>

Water at $T_\infty=70$ °C flows in a pipe with inner radius $r_1=6$ cm and outer radius $r_2=6.5$ cm. A thin electric heater around the outside consumes 300 W per metre of pipe, all of it transferred to the pipe (the outside is heavily insulated). Heat passes from the inner surface to the water by convection with $h=85$ W/m²K. Assume constant $k$, 1-D steady conduction. Write the differential equation and boundary conditions. Do not solve.

</Statement>

<Think>

Cylinder without generation: $\dfrac1r\dfrac d{dr}\!\left(r\dfrac{dT}{dr}\right)=0$. Outer surface: given heat flux (the heater). Inner surface: convection to the water.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="Your steps">

$\dfrac d{dr}\!\left(r\dfrac{dT}{dr}\right)=0\Rightarrow\dfrac{dT}{dr}=\dfrac{C_1}r\Rightarrow T=C_1\ln r+C_2$. Inner: $k\dfrac{dT(r_1)}{dr}=h\,[T(r_1)-T_\infty]$. Outer: $kA\dfrac{dT(r_2)}{dr}=\dot q$, i.e. $k\dfrac{dT(r_2)}{dr}=\dfrac{300}{2\pi r_2(1\ \text{m})}=734.6$ W/m².

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$$\frac d{dr}\!\left(r\frac{dT}{dr}\right)=0;\qquad k\frac{dT}{dr}\Big\vert_{r_1}=h\,[T(r_1)-T_\infty];\qquad k\frac{dT}{dr}\Big\vert_{r_2}=\frac{300}{2\pi r_2(1\ \text{m})}=734.6\ \text{W/m}^2.$$

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="✔ Matches your notes (flux 734.6 W/m²). What it teaches">

For a cylinder, the heat **rate** per length is constant but the heat **flux** is not ($q''=\dot Q'/2\pi r$): when a condition gives a total power, divide by the area *at that radius*. Signs: heat flows toward the water, i.e. in the $-r$ direction, so $-k\,dT/dr<0$ there. Your inner condition $k\,dT/dr=h(T-T_\infty)$ is the same statement written with the heat flowing inward.

</Callout>

</Problem>

</Statement>

$T''=0\Rightarrow T=C_1x+C_2$. Left BC: $T(0)=90$ gives $C_2=90$. Right BC is a balance at $x=L$: conduction arriving $=$ convection leaving, and $T(L)=C_1L+90$ is unknown (it contains $C_1$).

</Think>

<Steps>

$\dot Q=-kA\dfrac{dT}{dx}=-2.3(30)(-131.09)=9045$ W.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$T(x)=90-131.09\,x$ °C (so $T(L)=37.6$ °C); $\dot Q=\mathbf{9045}$ W.

</Answer>

<Problem id="2-77" title="Spherical container with a heat flux on the inside: derive the given profile" tags="sphere without generation, flux boundary condition">

<Statement>

A spherical container: $r_1=1$ m, $r_2=1.05$ m, $k=1.5$ W/mK. The inner surface has a uniform flux $\dot q_1=7$ kW/m², the outer surface is at $T_2=25$ °C. Show that $T(r)=\dfrac{\dot q_1r_1^2}{k}\left(\dfrac1r-\dfrac1{r_2}\right)+T_2$ and find the inner-surface temperature.

</Statement>

<Think>

Sphere without generation: $\dfrac d{dr}\!\left(r^2\dfrac{dT}{dr}\right)=0\Rightarrow T=-\dfrac{C_1}r+C_2$. Flux BC at $r_1$ gives $C_1$; $T(r_2)=T_2$ gives $C_2$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="Your steps">

$r^2\dfrac{dT}{dr}=C_1$, $T=-\dfrac{C_1}r+C_2$. At $r_1$: $-k\dfrac{dT}{dr}=\dot q_1\Rightarrow\dfrac{dT}{dr}=-\dfrac{\dot q_1}k$, and $\dfrac{dT}{dr}=\dfrac{C_1}{r_1^2}$, so $C_1=-\dfrac{\dot q_1r_1^2}k$. Then $T=\dfrac{\dot q_1r_1^2}{k}\dfrac1r+C_2$; at $r_2$: $C_2=T_2-\dfrac{\dot q_1r_1^2}{k}\dfrac1{r_2}$. Result: $T(r)=\dfrac{\dot q_1r_1^2}{k}\left(\dfrac1r-\dfrac1{r_2}\right)+T_2$. Numbers: $\dfrac{7000(1)^2}{1.5}=4666.7$; $T(r_1)=4666.7(1-0.95238)+25=247.2$ °C.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

The given profile is verified; **$T(r_1)=247.2$ °C.**

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="✔ Matches your notes (4666.67, 247.2). What it teaches">

In a sphere the heat **rate** is constant ($\dot q_1\,4\pi r_1^2$), so the flux scales as $1/r^2$: $\dot q(r)=\dot q_1(r_1/r)^2$, and the temperature varies as $1/r$, not linearly. Quick check by resistance: $\dot Q=7000(4\pi)=87.96$ kW; $\Delta T=\dot Q\,\dfrac{r_2-r_1}{4\pi kr_1r_2}=222.2$ K ✓.

</Callout>

<Problem id="2-97" title="Check a given temperature profile and find the surface flux" tags="reading a solution: steady or transient, dimensionality, flux">

<Statement>

A long solid cylinder, $r_0=4$ cm, $k=25$ W/mK, generates heat at 35 W/cm³ uniformly, with the side surface at $T_s=80$ °C. <Problem id="Review-cyl-sph" title="Hollow cylinder and hollow sphere between two fixed temperatures: general results (your review notebook, 2-75 and 2-76)" tags="derivations of the cylindrical and spherical conduction formulas">

<Statement>

(a) Cylinder shell, inner radius $r_1$ at $T_1$, outer radius $r_2$ at $T_2$, no generation. (b) Spherical shell with the same conditions. Derive $T(r)$ and the heat rate.

</Statement>

<Steps>
<Step title="(a) Cylinder (your steps)">

$\dfrac d{dr}\!\left(r\dfrac{dT}{dr}\right)=0\Rightarrow\dfrac{dT}{dr}=\dfrac{C_1}r\Rightarrow T=C_1\ln r+C_2$. Two conditions: $T_1=C_1\ln r_1+C_2$, $T_2=C_1\ln r_2+C_2$ give $C_1=\dfrac{T_2-T_1}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}$. Then $T(r)=\dfrac{T_2-T_1}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}\ln\dfrac r{r_1}+T_1$ and $\dot Q=-kA\dfrac{dT}{dr}=-k(2\pi rL)\dfrac{C_1}r=2\pi Lk\dfrac{T_1-T_2}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}$.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Sphere (your steps)">

$r^2\dfrac{dT}{dr}=C_1\Rightarrow T=-\dfrac{C_1}r+C_2$; $T_2-T_1=C_1\left(\dfrac1{r_1}-\dfrac1{r_2}\right)$, so $C_1=\dfrac{(T_2-T_1)r_1r_2}{r_2-r_1}$. $\dot Q=-k(4\pi r^2)\dfrac{C_1}{r^2}=4\pi kr_1r_2\dfrac{T_1-T_2}{r_2-r_1}$.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

Cylinder: $T=T_1+(T_2-T_1)\dfrac{\ln(r/r_1)}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}$, $\dot Q=\dfrac{2\pi kL(T_1-T_2)}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}$. Sphere: $T=T_1+(T_2-T_1)\dfrac{r_2\,(r-r_1)}{r\,(r_2-r_1)}$ (check: $r=r_1$ gives $T_1$, $r=r_2$ gives $T_2$), and $\dot Q=\dfrac{4\pi kr_1r_2(T_1-T_2)}{r_2-r_1}$.

</Answer>

<Callout kind="note" title="✔ Matches the resistance formulas of Chapter 3. What it teaches">

These two results are where the **cylindrical and spherical resistances** come from: $R_{cyl}=\dfrac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{2\pi kL}$ and $R_{sph}=\dfrac{r_2-r_1}{4\pi kr_1r_2}$. The areas to remember (your blue note): cylinder $2\pi rL$, sphere $4\pi r^2$. Your notebook also records the three generation shortcuts: $\dot eV=hA_s(T_s-T_\infty)$ for the surface temperature, and $T_{max}-T_s=\dfrac{\dot eL^2}{2k},\ \dfrac{\dot er_0^2}{4k},\ \dfrac{\dot er_0^2}{6k}$ for wall, cylinder, sphere.

</Callout>

<Problem id="P2-1" title="Electric heating wire in air" tags="cylinder with generation, convection boundary">

<Statement>

A nichrome wire of diameter 2 mm ($k=15$ W/mK) generates heat uniformly at $\dot e=5\times10^8$ W/m³ when carrying current. It sits in 30 °C air with $h=800$ W/m²K. Find the surface temperature and the centreline temperature in steady state.

</Statement>

<Think>

Long wire, steady, uniform generation, 1-D radial: $\dfrac1r\dfrac d{dr}\left(r\dfrac{dT}{dr}\right)+\dfrac{\dot e}k=0$. Two boundary conditions: symmetry at the centre and convection at the surface. The surface temperature does not need the conduction solution at all: **energy balance on the whole wire** (generation = convection) fixes it.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Energy balance on the wire (per unit length)">

Generated $=\dot e\pi r_0^2$; lost $=h(2\pi r_0)(T_s-T_\infty)$. So $T_s=T_\infty+\dfrac{\dot er_0}{2h}=30+\dfrac{5\times10^8(0.001)}{2(800)}=30+312.5=342.5$ °C.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Temperature rise inside the wire">

Integrating the equation with $dT/dr=0$ at $r=0$ and $T=T_s$ at $r_0$ gives $T(r)=T_s+\dfrac{\dot e}{4k}(r_0^2-r^2)$, so $T_0-T_s=\dfrac{\dot er_0^2}{4k}=\dfrac{5\times10^8(10^{-6})}{4(15)}=8.33$ K.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Centreline">

$T_0=342.5+8.33=350.8$ °C.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$T_s=342.5$ °C, $T_0=350.8$ °C.** The outside film (312 K drop) dominates over the internal conduction drop (8 K): the wire is nearly isothermal, so the lumped idea is valid ($Bi=hr_0/2k=0.027$).

</Answer>

<Traps>

Do not use the wire's *surface-to-centre* formula $\dot er_0^2/4k$ for the temperature difference to the air: it only covers the interior.

</Traps>

</Problem>

<Problem id="P2-2" title="A slab with generation and unequal face temperatures" tags="plane wall, asymmetric boundary conditions, location of the maximum">

<Statement>

A slab 10 cm thick ($k=2$ W/mK) generates heat uniformly at $\dot e=2\times10^5$ W/m³. Its faces are held at $T(0)=100$ °C and $T(L)=60$ °C. Find $T(x)$, the position and value of the maximum temperature, and the heat flux leaving each face.

</Statement>

<Think>

$k\dfrac{d^2T}{dx^2}+\dot e=0$ is integrated twice; two Dirichlet conditions fix the constants. Because the faces are at different temperatures, the maximum is **not** at the centre: it is where $dT/dx=0$.

</Think>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. General solution">

$\dfrac{dT}{dx}=-\dfrac{\dot e}kx+C_1$; $T=-\dfrac{\dot e}{2k}x^2+C_1x+C_2$, with $\dfrac{\dot e}{2k}=\dfrac{2\times10^5}{4}=5\times10^4$ K/m².

</Step>
<Step title="2. Boundary conditions">

$T(0)=100\Rightarrow C_2=100$. $T(L)=60$: $-5\times10^4(0.01)+C_1(0.1)+100=60\Rightarrow C_1(0.1)=460\Rightarrow C_1=4600$ K/m. So $T(x)=-5\times10^4x^2+4600x+100$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Maximum">

$dT/dx=0$: $x_m=\dfrac{C_1k}{\dot e}=\dfrac{4600(2)}{2\times10^5}=0.046$ m (4.6 cm: shifted toward the cold face). $T_{max}=100+4600(0.046)-5\times10^4(0.046)^2=100+211.6-105.8=205.8$ °C.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Heat fluxes at the faces">

$q''=-k\,dT/dx$: at $x=0$: $-2(4600)=-9200$ W/m² (9200 W/m² flows out to the left); at $x=L$: $dT/dx=4600-2\times10^5(0.1)/2=-5400$, so $q''=+10\,800$ W/m² (out to the right).

</Step>
<Step title="5. Check">

$9200+10\,800=20\,000=\dot eL$ ✓ (all the generated energy leaves through the two faces).

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

**$T(x)=100+4600x-5\times10^4x^2$ (°C, $x$ in m); maximum 205.8 °C at $x=4.6$ cm; fluxes 9200 W/m² (left face) and 10 800 W/m² (right face).**

</Answer>

<Traps>

If the temperatures on the two faces are equal the maximum is at $L/2$ and the fluxes are equal; here the hot face (100 °C) loses less because its gradient is smaller.

</Traps>

</Problem>
