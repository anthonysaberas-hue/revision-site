<Problem id="PS3 P1" title="When does Bernoulli break down?" tags="compressibility, Bernoulli, M < 0.3">

<Statement>

Air (MW 28.9, γ 1.4) accelerates isentropically from rest at 1 bar and 300 K to 50 m/s. Compute $p_0 - p$ (a) with incompressible Bernoulli, (b) with the isentropic relations. (c) Repeat for 250 m/s, (d) for 500 m/s. When does Bernoulli break down?

</Statement>

<Think>

Compare $\frac12\rho_0V^2$ with the exact $p_0[1 - (T/T_0)^{3.5}]$. The ratio is $1 + M^2/4 + \dots$, so the answer is "when $M$ is no longer small" (rule of thumb M = 0.3). [Chapter 5 §compressibility](#/mech430/ch/isentropic)

</Think>

<Plan>

$\rho_0 = p_0/RT_0$. For each V: Bernoulli; then $T = T_0 - V^2/2c_p$ → $M$ → $p$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Bernoulli">

$\rho_0 = 100\,000/(288.7 \times 300) = 1.155$ kg/m³ (official R from MW 28.8). $\frac12\rho_0V^2$ = 1444 Pa (50 m/s), 36.1 kPa (250 m/s), 144.3 kPa (500 m/s).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Isentropic, 50 m/s">

$T = T_0 - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}\frac{V^2}{\gamma R} = 300 - 0.2 \times 2500/404.2 = 298.76$ K, $M = 0.144$; $p = 10^5/(300/298.76)^{3.5}$, so $p_0 - p = 1436$ Pa (official 1439 with rounded T).

</Step>
<Step title="3. Isentropic, 250 m/s">

$T = 269.1$ K, $M = 0.758$, $p_0/p = (300/269.1)^{3.5} = 1.463$, so $p_0 - p = 31.7$ kPa.

</Step>
<Step title="4. Isentropic, 500 m/s">

$T = 176.3$ K, $M = 1.87$, $p_0/p = 6.43$, so $p_0 - p = 84.4$ kPa. (Not in the official scan.)

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

| V | M | Bernoulli | Isentropic | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 m/s | 0.14 | 1444 Pa | 1436 Pa | 0.995 |
| 250 m/s | 0.76 | 36.1 kPa | 31.7 kPa | 0.88 |
| 500 m/s | 1.87 | 144 kPa (impossible: > p₀) | 84.4 kPa | 0.59 |

Bernoulli is good to about 2% for $M < 0.3$; the error grows like $M^2/4$.

</Answer>

<CompressibilitySim />

<Traps>

- Anthony concluded that the methods differ significantly only "for M > 1". Too generous: the error is already 12% at M = 0.76.
- At 500 m/s Bernoulli gives $p_0 - p > p_0$, a negative absolute pressure.
- Why Bernoulli overestimates: it keeps $\rho = \rho_0$, but the gas expands as it accelerates, and $dp = -\rho V\,dV$ needs less pressure drop with a lower ρ.

</Traps>

<Problem id="PS3 P2" title="N₂ nozzle: optimum exit Mach, exhaust velocity, throat and exit areas" tags="rocket nozzle, Fliegner, Isp">

<Statement>

N₂ (MW 28, γ 1.4) at $p_0 = 2.5$ MPa and $T_0 = 1400$ K expands in a nozzle to an ambient pressure of 40 kPa. (a) Exit Mach for optimum thrust ($p_e = p_\text{amb}$)? (b) Exhaust velocity? (c) For 5 kg/s, throat and exit areas?

</Statement>

<Think>

Optimum thrust means matched exit, so $p_0/p_e$ fixes $M_e$. The throat is choked (Fliegner), and the exit is $A^*$ times $A/A^*$ at $M_e$. See [Chapter 6](#/mech430/ch/nozzles).

</Think>

<Plan>

$p_0/p_e = 62.5$ → $M_e$ → $T_e$, $V_e$ → Fliegner → $A_t$ → $A_e$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Exit Mach (a)">

$p_0/p_e = 2500/40 = 62.5$ ⇒ $M_e = \sqrt{5(62.5^{0.2857} - 1)} = 3.36$. <T>A2 @ M = 3.36: p/p₀ = 0.0160</T>

</Step>
<Step title="2. Exhaust velocity (b)">

$T_e = 1400/(1 + 0.2 \times 3.36^2) = 429.6$ K; $c_e = \sqrt{1.4 \times 296.9 \times 429.6} = 422.6$ m/s; $V_e = 3.36 \times 422.6 = 1420$ m/s. Same from the rocket equation: $\sqrt{7 \times 296.9 \times 1400(1 - 0.016^{0.2857})} = 1420$ m/s. Matched, so $I_{sp} = V_e = 1420$ m/s = 144.7 s.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Throat (c)">

Fliegner for N₂: $\dot m = 0.0397\,A_tp_0/\sqrt{T_0}$ ⇒

$$
A_t = \frac{5\sqrt{1400}}{0.0397 \times 2.5\times10^6} = 0.00188\ \text{m}^2 = 18.8\ \text{cm}^2
$$

</Step>
<Step title="4. Exit (c)">

<T>A2 @ M = 3.36: A/A* = 5.956</T> ⇒ $A_e = 0.00188 \times 5.96 = 0.0112$ m² = 112 cm². (Check by continuity: $\rho_e = 40\,000/(296.9 \times 429.6) = 0.313$ kg/m³, $A_e = 5/(0.313 \times 1420) = 0.0112$ m².)

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- (a) $M_e = 3.36$
- (b) $V_e = 1420$ m/s ($I_{sp} = 144.7$ s)
- (c) $A_t = 18.8$ cm², $A_e = 112$ cm² ($A_e/A_t = 5.96$)

</Answer>

<IsentropicDuct3D M1={0.15} M2={3.36} wall="engine" plume="ideal" label1="N₂ · 2.5 MPa · 1400 K" label2="exit · M = 3.36 · p = 40 kPa" />

<Traps>

- Fliegner's constant depends on the gas: 0.0404 for air, 0.0397 for N₂ ($\sqrt{\gamma/R}(2/(\gamma+1))^3$).
- At the matched condition all the thrust is momentum; $I_{sp} = V_e$.

</Traps>

<Problem id="PS3 P3" title="Balloon, airliner and sample bottle" tags="reference frames, stagnation pressure, choking">

<Statement>

You float in a balloon at 10 km (T = −50 °C, p = 0.26 atm) and fill an evacuated sample bottle (p = 0) with ambient air. An airliner passes at M = 0.86. (a) From the balloon, what are the static and stagnation pressures of the air? (b) From the airliner? (c) At the choke point in the bottle's valve (isentropic)?

</Statement>

<Think>

**Static properties are frame-independent; stagnation properties are not.** For (c), the still air is the reservoir and the vacuum chokes the valve.

</Think>

<Plan>

(a) V = 0 ⇒ $p_0 = p$. (b) $p_0 = p(1 + 0.2M^2)^{3.5}$. (c) $p_0$ = ambient, $p^* = 0.5283\,p_0$.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Balloon frame (a)">

The air is at rest relative to you: $p = p_0 = 0.26$ atm.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Airliner frame (b)">

<T>A2 @ M = 0.86: p/p₀ = 0.6170</T> ⇒ $p_0 = 0.26/0.617 = 0.421$ atm. Static $p$ is still 0.26 atm.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Valve (c)">

$p_b/p_0 = 0 < 0.528$: choked, M = 1 at the constriction. $p_0 = 0.26$ atm and $p^* = 0.5283 \times 0.26 = 0.137$ atm.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- (a) $p = 0.26$ atm, $p_0 = 0.26$ atm
- (b) $p = 0.26$ atm, $p_0 = 0.421$ atm
- (c) $p_0 = 0.26$ atm, $p = p^* = 0.137$ atm (choked)

</Answer>

<Traps>

- The handout figure says M 0.85 and the text says 0.86; the official solution uses 0.86.
- Anthony rounded 0.26 atm to 26 kPa (it is 26.3 kPa), giving 13.73 kPa instead of 13.9 kPa at the valve.
- The valve stays choked until the bottle pressure rises above 0.137 atm.

<Problem id="PS3 P4" title="C-D nozzle 1 → 5 m²: lowest p₀ to choke vs p₀ for supersonic flow" tags="two roots of A/A*, first critical, design">

<Statement>

A C-D nozzle (throat 1 m², exit 5 m²) is fed air at $p_0$ and $T_0 = 300$ K and exhausts to 1 atm. (a) Lowest $p_0$ that chokes the flow, with exit Mach and $\dot m$. (b) $p_0$ for isentropic supersonic flow through the whole diverging part, with exit Mach and $\dot m$. (c) Both are choked: are the mass flows the same?

</Statement>

<Think>

Choked ⇒ $A^* = A_t$, so $A_e/A^* = 5$, which has **two roots**. Subsonic root = first critical (just choked); supersonic root = design. Here $p_b$ is fixed and $p_0$ is the unknown.

</Think>

<Plan>

A/A* = 5 → $M_\text{sub}$, $M_\text{sup}$ → $p_0 = p_b(p_0/p)$ each → $\dot m$ from Fliegner at the throat.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. The two roots">

<T>A2 @ A/A* = 5.0: M = 0.117 (sub) or 3.17 (sup)</T> — exact 0.1167 and 3.175. <CalcLink calc="isentropic" q="in=A&v=5&b=sup">check</CalcLink>

</Step>
<Step title="2. (a) Just choked">

$p_0 = 1 \times (1 + 0.2 \times 0.1167^2)^{3.5} = 1.0096$ atm = 102.3 kPa. Then

$$
\dot m = 0.0404\frac{A_tp_0}{\sqrt{T_0}} \approx \frac{1 \times 102\,300}{\sqrt{300}}\sqrt{\frac{1.4}{288.7}}(0.5787) = 238\ \text{kg/s}
$$

</Step>
<Step title="3. (b) Fully supersonic">

$p_0 = (1 + 0.2 \times 3.175^2)^{3.5} = 47.6$ atm = 4.83 MPa; $\dot m = 238 \times 47.6/1.0096 = 11\,230$ kg/s.

</Step>
<Step title="4. (c) Same mass flow?">

No. Choking fixes **M = 1 at the throat**, not $\dot m$. With the same $A_t$ and $T_0$, $\dot m \propto p_0$: $11\,230/238 = 47.2 = 47.6/1.0096$.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- (a) $p_0 = 1.0096$ atm, $M_e = 0.117$, $\dot m = 238$ kg/s
- (b) $p_0 = 47.6$ atm (4.83 MPa), $M_e = 3.17$, $\dot m = 11\,230$ kg/s
- (c) No: $\dot m \propto p_0$; choking only makes it independent of the back pressure.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Between 1.0096 and 47.6 atm the nozzle is choked but there is a shock inside or an over-expanded exit (Ch. 8).
- Anthony interpolated M = 0.116 (A/A* = 5.03), giving 237 kg/s; the official answer used 0.1167.
- The official solution (and statement) use R = 288.7; with 287 the mass flows go up 0.3%.

<Problem id="PS3 P5" title="Maximum velocity of an isentropic expansion" tags="V_max, c₀, light gases">

<Statement>

For fixed stagnation conditions, start from $h_0 = h + V^2/2$ and derive the maximum velocity when the flow is expanded to $M \to \infty$, in terms of $c_0$ and γ. With a 3000 K pressure vessel and the best gas, what is the fastest isentropic flow? Which gas? Could a lab make faster flow?

</Statement>

<Think>

$M \to \infty$ means $T \to 0$ (c → 0, not V → ∞): all enthalpy becomes kinetic energy. Then pick the gas with the largest $c_p$, i.e. the lowest MW.

</Think>

<Plan>

Energy with $T = 0$ → $V_\text{max} = \sqrt{2c_pT_0}$ → in terms of $c_0$ → numbers.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Energy at T → 0">

$c_pT_0 = c_pT + V^2/2 \to V_\text{max}^2/2$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Rewrite">

$c_p = \gamma R/(\gamma-1)$ and $c_0^2 = \gamma RT_0$:

$$
V_\text{max} = \sqrt{2c_pT_0} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma - 1}}\,c_0 \quad (= 2.236\,c_0 \text{ for } \gamma = 1.4)
$$

</Step>
<Step title="3. Best gas at 3000 K">

$V_\text{max}^2 = \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{\Re}{MW}T_0$: MW matters more than γ. H₂: $\sqrt{7 \times 4157 \times 3000} = 9343$ m/s. He: $\sqrt{(2 \times 1.67/0.67)(2078.5)(3000)} = 5575$ m/s. Air: 2455 m/s.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- $V_\text{max} = \sqrt{2/(\gamma-1)}\,c_0 = \sqrt{2c_pT_0}$
- Hydrogen: about 9.3 km/s at 3000 K (helium only 5.6 km/s)
- Yes, faster flow is possible with **unsteady** devices (shock and expansion tubes, light-gas guns) that heat the gas only for microseconds (notes' footnote: see Ch. 16).

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Anthony first wrote "choose helium" (lowest MW?) and then correctly computed with H₂ (MW 2 < 4).
- At 3000 K hydrogen partly dissociates and $c_p$ is not constant, so 9.3 km/s is idealised.
- The official solution stops after the derivation; the numbers above are Anthony's (H₂) plus a check.

</Traps>
