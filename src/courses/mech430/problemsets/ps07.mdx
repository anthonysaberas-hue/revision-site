<Remember title="The two Fanno moves you will use everywhere">

- **Subtract L\*** (only when $L \le L^*$): $(4fL^*/D)_2 = (4fL^*/D)_1 - 4fL/D$.
- **p\* is the same on both sides of a normal shock in the duct** (same $\rho V$, same $T_0$). L\* is not: it jumps to the long subsonic value.

</Remember>

<Problem id="PS7 P1" title="Derive 4fL*/D_H(M)" tags="derivation, partial fractions">

<Statement>

Starting with the momentum equation for constant-area frictional flow,

$$
\frac{dM}{M} = \frac{\gamma M^2\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)}{2(1 - M^2)}\,\frac{4f}{D_H}\,dx,
$$

integrate to find the channel length $L^*$ needed to bring a flow entering at Mach $M$ to sonic conditions at the exit:

$$
\frac{4fL^*}{D_H} = \frac{1 - M^2}{\gamma M^2} + \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\frac{(\gamma+1)M^2}{2\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)}.
$$

</Statement>

<Think>

A pure calculus problem: separate the variables, then integrate a rational function of $M$ by partial fractions. The physics is in the limits: start at the inlet Mach $M$ (where $x = 0$) and end at $M = 1$ (where $x = L^*$). This formula is Table A4's first column. It plays the role for friction that $A/A^*$ plays for area change.

</Think>

<Plan>

Separate → partial fractions → integrate from $M$ to 1 (left side) and from 0 to $L^*$ (right side) → tidy the logs.

<FannoFLPlot mark={[0.5, 3]} />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Separate the variables">

Put all the $M$ dependence on the left (assume $f$ is constant along the pipe):

$$
\frac{2(1 - M^2)}{\gamma M^3\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)}\,dM = \frac{4f}{D_H}\,dx
$$

</Step>
<Step title="2. Partial fractions">

Write the left side as $\dfrac{A}{\gamma M^3} + \dfrac{B}{\gamma M^2} + \dfrac{C}{\gamma M} + \dfrac{DM + E}{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2}$. Matching coefficients of the powers of $M$ gives

$$
A = 2,\quad B = 0,\quad C = -(\gamma+1),\quad D = \frac{\gamma^2 - 1}{2\gamma},\quad E = 0
$$

so the integrand is $\dfrac{2}{\gamma M^3} - \dfrac{\gamma+1}{\gamma M} + \dfrac{(\gamma^2-1)M}{2\gamma\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)}$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Integrate between the right limits">

Integrate $M \to 1$ on the left and $0 \to L^*$ on the right. The last term integrates with $u = 1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2$:

$$
\left[-\frac{1}{\gamma M^2}\right]_M^1 + \left[-\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}\ln M\right]_M^1 + \left[\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\left(1 + \tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]_M^1 = \frac{4fL^*}{D_H}
$$

</Step>
<Step title="4. Evaluate and combine the logs">

$$
-\frac{1}{\gamma} + \frac{1}{\gamma M^2} + \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}\ln M + \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\frac{(\gamma+1)/2}{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2} = \frac{4fL^*}{D_H}
$$

Combine $-\frac1\gamma + \frac{1}{\gamma M^2} = \frac{1 - M^2}{\gamma M^2}$, and write $\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}\ln M = \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln M^2$ so it merges into the second log:

$$
\boxed{\frac{4fL^*}{D_H} = \frac{1 - M^2}{\gamma M^2} + \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\frac{(\gamma+1)M^2}{2\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)}}
$$

Check: at $M = 0.5$ this gives 1.0691, matching <T>A4 @ M = 0.5: 4fL*/D = 1.0691</T> — <CalcLink calc="fanno" q="in=M&v=0.5">check</CalcLink>.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- $\dfrac{4fL^*}{D_H} = \dfrac{1 - M^2}{\gamma M^2} + \dfrac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\dfrac{(\gamma+1)M^2}{2\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)}$. It is positive on both branches and zero at $M = 1$.
- As $M \to \infty$ it tends to $-\frac1\gamma + \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1} = 0.8215$.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Get the limits the right way round: the inlet $M$ is the lower limit and 1 the upper. Flip them and you get a negative length.
- Anthony decomposed an equivalent fraction, $A/M + B/M^2 + C/M^3 + (DM+E)/(2 + (\gamma-1)M^2)$, and got $A = (\gamma+1)/4$, $C = -1/2$, $D = -(\gamma^2-1)/4$. His middle lines have small sign slips, but the boxed result is correct.
- Friction drives $M$ toward 1 on both branches, which is why the expression is positive for $M$ below and above 1.

</Traps>

<Problem id="PS7 P2" title="Friction factor for choked flow in a 20 mm pipe" tags="Fanno, choked, L = L*">

<Statement>

Air flows down a 20 mm diameter pipe 0.8 m long. The inlet velocity is 200 m/s and the inlet temperature is 30 °C. Find the average friction factor $f$ if the flow is choked at the pipe exit.

</Statement>

<Think>

"Choked at the exit" means $M_\text{exit} = 1$, so **the given length is $L^*$** for the inlet Mach number. Find $M_1$ from the speed of sound, read $4fL^*/D$, and solve for $f$. It's subsonic, so take the subsonic row.

</Think>

<Plan>

$T_1$ → $c_1$ → $M_1$ → A4: $4fL^*/D$ → $f = (4fL^*/D)\cdot D/(4L)$.

<FannoDuctSetupFigure friction Min={0.571} Mout={1} inlet="V₁ = 200 m/s | T₁ = 303 K" exit="choked: M = 1 | L = L* = 0.8 m" duct="D = 20 mm, f = ?" caption="A subsonic inlet; friction accelerates the flow to exactly M = 1 at the exit." />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Inlet Mach number">

$T_1 = 30 + 273.15 = 303.15$ K. With the official $R = 8314/28.8$:

$$
c_1 = \sqrt{\gamma R T_1} = \sqrt{1.4 \times 288.7 \times 303.15} = 350.0\ \text{m/s}, \qquad M_1 = \frac{200}{350.0} = 0.571
$$

</Step>
<Step title="2. Read L* for this inlet">

<T>A4 @ M = 0.57: 4fL*/D ≈ 0.62</T> (exact at $M = 0.5714$: 0.6161) — <CalcLink calc="fanno" q="in=M&v=0.5714&b=sub">check</CalcLink>.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Solve for f">

$L^* = L = 0.8$ m:

$$
f = \frac{4fL^*}{D}\cdot\frac{D}{4L^*} = 0.6161 \times \frac{0.02}{4 \times 0.8} = 0.00385
$$

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- $f \approx 0.0038$–$0.00385$ (official 0.00385; Anthony 0.00383 using $T = 303$ K).

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Convert °C to K before computing $c$.
- "Choked at the exit" is the whole trick: $L = L^*$. Don't look for an extension.
- Anthony and the official solution both used MW = 28.8. With $R = 287$ the answer barely changes ($M_1 = 0.573$).

</Traps>

<Problem id="PS7 P5" title="M = 3 duct (L/D = 60) with p_exit = 50 kPa: exit Mach and shock location" tags="Fanno, shock in duct, iteration, p* invariance">

<Statement>

Air with stagnation pressure 300 kPa and stagnation temperature 30 °C enters a constant-area duct at Mach 3. The duct length-to-diameter ratio is 60. The flow is adiabatic and the average friction factor is 0.0025. If the pressure at the duct exit is 50 kPa, find the exit Mach number and the location of the shock down the duct (in diameters).

</Statement>

<Think>

<Problem id="PS7 P7" title="With friction, do dp and dV still have opposite signs?" tags="sign analysis, momentum, closure relations">

<Statement>

For steady inviscid flow, $dp + \rho V\,dV = 0$ ensures that changes in velocity and pressure are always inversely related. For steady frictional flow, the momentum equation becomes $dp + \rho V\,dV + \frac{4f}{D_H}\left(\tfrac12\rho V^2\right)dx = 0$, with three terms that might have different signs. Can we still say that changes in velocity and pressure are always inversely related? With friction, can both pressure and velocity increase? Can both decrease? Under what conditions?

</Statement>

<Think>

A sign argument. Momentum alone has three terms, so it is not enough. The trap is to stop there. Bring in the **closure relations** for constant-area, adiabatic, perfect-gas flow (continuity, energy, state). They add a constraint the momentum equation doesn't show.

</Think>

<Plan>

Take flow left to right, so $dx$, $V$, $\rho$, $p$ are positive and the friction term is **always positive**. Go case by case, then close with state + continuity + energy.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Both increase? (dp > 0, dV > 0)">

Then all three terms are positive and can't sum to zero. **Impossible.** Friction can only oppose the motion. (An external push could do it, but friction can't.)

</Step>
<Step title="2. dp > 0">

Then $\rho V\,dV = -dp - (\text{friction})$ is negative, so $dV$ is negative. This is the inverse relation, the same as inviscid flow.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Both decrease? Momentum alone says maybe">

If $dp$ is negative, then $\rho V\,dV = -dp - (\text{friction})$. This could be negative if $0 > dp > -\frac{4f}{D_H}\frac{\rho V^2}{2}dx$. **Momentum alone does not rule it out.**

</Step>
<Step title="4. Close the system">

State: $\dfrac{dp}{p} = \dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} + \dfrac{dT}{T}$. Continuity (constant area): $\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} = -\dfrac{dV}{V}$. Energy (adiabatic): $c_p\,dT = -V\,dV$. Combining:

$$
\frac{dp}{p} = -\left[\frac{1}{V} + \frac{V}{c_p T}\right] dV, \qquad \text{with the bracket always positive}
$$

So **$dp$ and $dV$ always have opposite signs**, and case 3 is impossible.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- **Yes.** For steady, adiabatic, constant-area flow of a perfect gas with friction, $p$ and $V$ always change in opposite directions.
- Both increasing is impossible (from momentum alone). Both decreasing looks allowed by momentum, but continuity, energy and state exclude it. It could only happen with heat transfer or area change.

</Answer>

<FannoSignsFigure />
