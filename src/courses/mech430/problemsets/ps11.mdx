# Moving shocks and shock tubes

<p className="lede">Six problems that build the shock tube from its parts. A frame change explains when the flow behind a moving shock is supersonic (P1) and what a water hammer is (P2). Riemann invariants give the rarefaction (P3–P4). Contact matching gives the shock-tube equation (P5). The reflected shock turns the shock tube into a lab instrument (P6).</p>

PS11 was not collected, so **there is no solution by Anthony**. The official solution has two errors in P6, which are corrected below. Air: γ = 1.4, R = 8314/28.9 J/kg·K. Theory in [Ch. 16](#/mech430/ch/unsteady).

<Problem id="PS11 P1" title="When is the lab-frame flow behind a moving shock supersonic? (M_s = 2.068 for air)" tags="moving shock, frame change, derivation">

<Statement>

Flow leaving a shock wave is always subsonic relative to the shock. As seen from the fixed laboratory frame, however, the flow behind a moving shock can be subsonic or supersonic. For a shock propagating into stationary gas (arbitrary γ), find the shock Mach number at which the flow behind the shock becomes supersonic in the lab frame. For air (γ = 1.4), show that this happens at a shock Mach number of 2.068.

</Statement>

<Think>

A frame-change problem. In the lab frame the gas behind the shock moves at the piston speed $V_p'$, which the unsteady shock formula gives. Whether that is supersonic depends on the sound speed **behind** the shock, $c_y$. Sound speed is a temperature, and temperature does not depend on the frame, so $c_y/c_x$ comes from the steady normal-shock relation with $M_x = M_s$.

</Think>

<Plan>

$\dfrac{V_p'}{c_y} = \dfrac{V_p'}{c_x}\cdot\dfrac{c_x}{c_y}$ = (unsteady shock formula) × $\sqrt{T_x/T_y}$ (A3) → set it equal to 1 → solve for $M_s$.

<MovingShockFramesFigure Ms={3} c1={348} T1={300} />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Write the lab-frame Mach number behind the shock">

$$
\frac{V_p'}{c_y} = \underbrace{\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\frac{M_s^2-1}{M_s}}_{V_p'/c_x}\;\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma+1)^2M_s^2}{\left[2\gamma M_s^2-(\gamma-1)\right]\left[2+(\gamma-1)M_s^2\right]}}}_{c_x/c_y = \sqrt{T_x/T_y}}
$$

</Step>
<Step title="2. Set it equal to 1 and square">

Why: the $(\gamma+1)^2$ cancels, leaving a quadratic in $M_s^2$:

$$
4(M_s^2-1)^2 = \left[2\gamma M_s^2-(\gamma-1)\right]\left[2+(\gamma-1)M_s^2\right]
$$

Expand and collect, then divide by $(1+\gamma)$:

$$
2(2-\gamma)M_s^4 - (7-\gamma)M_s^2 + 2 = 0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
M_s^2 = \frac{(7-\gamma)+\sqrt{\gamma^2+2\gamma+17}}{4(2-\gamma)}
$$

(The other root gives $M_s$ below 1.)

</Step>
<Step title="3. Air">

$M_s^2 = \dfrac{5.6+\sqrt{21.76}}{2.4} = \dfrac{5.6+4.6648}{2.4} = 4.2770$, so $M_s = 2.068$ ✓.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

The lab-frame flow is supersonic when $M_s^2 > \dfrac{(7-\gamma)+\sqrt{\gamma^2+2\gamma+17}}{4(2-\gamma)}$. For air, that is **M_s above 2.068**. The official solution states the inequality and the number but does not solve the general-γ equation; the closed form above does.

</Answer>

<PistonXTSim up0={460} />

<Traps>

- Test against $c_y$, the sound speed *behind* the shock. Using $c_x$ is the classic mistake.
- Even an infinitely strong shock gives a bounded lab Mach number: $V_p'/c_y \to \sqrt{2/(\gamma(\gamma-1))} = 1.89$.
- The notes' example ($M_s = 3$) gives 773.3/569.6 = 1.357, which is supersonic. In the sim, push at about 460 m/s (M_s ≈ 2.07) and read the lab Mach number.

</Traps>

<Problem id="PS11 P2" title="Water hammer: a valve slammed shut on 50 m/s air" tags="moving shock, frame change, piston analogy">

<Statement>

Air (γ = 1.4) at 25 °C and 100 kPa flows at 50 m/s in a constant-area pipe. A valve at the end of the pipe is suddenly closed. This creates a weak shock that propagates upstream and brings the air to rest. Calculate the pressure caused by this shock wave. (In water, the same effect is the "bang" of a water hammer in old Montreal plumbing.)

</Statement>

<Think>

Choose the frame in which the gas ahead of the shock is **at rest**: the frame moving with the incoming 50 m/s gas. In that frame the closed valve and the stopped gas both move at 50 m/s into still gas. It looks exactly like a piston, so the unsteady-shock formula applies with $V_p' = 50$ m/s.

</Think>

<Plan>

$c_x = \sqrt{\gamma RT}$ → $V_p'/c_x$ → $M_s$ (quadratic) → A3 → $p_y$, $T_y$.

<WaterHammerFigure V={50} Ms={1.09} c={346} />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Sound speed ahead">

$c_x = \sqrt{1.4 \times (8314/28.9) \times 298} = 346.4$ m/s. The official solution writes 349.3 m/s, an arithmetic slip that changes the answer by only 0.2 kPa.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Shock Mach number">

With $k = \tfrac{\gamma+1}{2}\tfrac{V_p'}{c_x} = 1.2 \times 50/346.4 = 0.1732$:

$M_s = \tfrac12\left(k + \sqrt{k^2+4}\right) = 1.0903$ (official 1.0895, from c = 349.3) — <CalcLink calc="moving">check</CalcLink>

</Step>
<Step title="3. Normal-shock ratios">

<T>A3 @ Mₓ = 1.09: p_y/pₓ = 1.218, T_y/Tₓ = 1.058</T> (exact at 1.0903: 1.2203 and 1.0588)

$p_y = 122.0$ kPa (official 121.8 kPa); $T_y = 315.5$ K

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- $p \approx 122$ kPa: a rise of about 22 kPa ("more than enough to cause hearing damage").
- $T \approx 315$ K, $M_s \approx 1.09$.
- In the lab the shock moves upstream at $M_s c_x - 50 \approx 328$ m/s.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Acoustic check: $\Delta p \approx \rho c\,\Delta u = 1.169 \times 346 \times 50 = 20$ kPa, which agrees with the 22 kPa above.
- In water ($\rho c \approx 1.5\times10^6$ kg/m²s) the same 50 m/s, or even 1 m/s, gives MPa. That is why a water hammer bangs.
- Use absolute temperature (298 K).

</Traps>

<Problem id="PS11 P3" title="Show that F = ∫dp/(ρc) = 2c/(γ−1)" tags="derivation, Riemann invariants">

<Statement>

As shown in lecture, the conservation laws for 1-D unsteady flow can be written as

$$
\frac{\partial(u+F)}{\partial t} + (u+c)\frac{\partial(u+F)}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad
\frac{\partial(u-F)}{\partial t} + (u-c)\frac{\partial(u-F)}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad F = \int_{p_o}^{p}\frac{dp}{\rho c}
$$

Show that for a perfect gas $F = 2c/(\gamma-1)$. Thus $C^\pm = u \pm 2c/(\gamma-1)$ are constant along $u \pm c$ waves. This is the basis of the method of characteristics.

</Statement>

<Think>

A short derivation. Write $\rho c$ in terms of p and c, use the isentropic relation between p and c, and the integrand collapses to a constant times dc.

</Think>

<Plan>

$\rho c = \gamma p/c$ → isentropic $p \propto c^{2\gamma/(\gamma-1)}$ → $dp$ in terms of $dc$ → integrate.

<RiemannXTFigure />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Express ρc with p and c">

$c^2 = \gamma p/\rho$, so $\rho c = \gamma p/c$ and $\dfrac{dp}{\rho c} = \dfrac{c}{\gamma}\dfrac{dp}{p}$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Isentropic link between p and c">

$\dfrac{p}{p_o} = \left(\dfrac{T}{T_o}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} = \left(\dfrac{c}{c_o}\right)^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}}$, so taking the log derivative gives $\dfrac{dp}{p} = \dfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\dfrac{dc}{c}$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Integrate">

$$
\frac{dp}{\rho c} = \frac{c}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{dc}{c} = \frac{2}{\gamma-1}\,dc
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
F = \frac{2c}{\gamma-1} \;(+\ \text{const})
$$

The constant from the lower limit cancels whenever invariants are compared, so it is dropped.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$F = \dfrac{2c}{\gamma-1}$, so $C^+ = u + \dfrac{2c}{\gamma-1}$ is constant along $dx/dt = u + c$ and $C^- = u - \dfrac{2c}{\gamma-1}$ along $dx/dt = u - c$.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- The derivation needs **homentropic** flow. Across a shock the entropy jumps and the invariants are not carried through.
- The official scan writes "u + 2/(γ−1) = const" and drops the c. That is a slip.
- The step from the PDEs: their left-hand sides are derivatives *along* the paths $dx/dt = u \pm c$ (Higgins's $D^\pm/Dt$), which are the trajectories of sound waves.

</Traps>

<Source>official 2025 solution p. 3 · Lecture 23 notes (Anthony) for the PDE derivation</Source>

</Problem>

<Problem id="PS11 P4" title="Rarefaction relation u₃(p₃/p₄) in a shock tube" tags="centred expansion, Riemann invariant">

<Statement>

Using the method of characteristics, show that in a shock tube, where high-pressure gas in state 4 (with γ₄) expands through an unsteady rarefaction wave to become state 3, the velocity $u_3$ and pressure $p_3$ are related by

$$
u_3 = \frac{2c_4}{\gamma_4-1}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_3}{p_4}\right)^{\frac{\gamma_4-1}{2\gamma_4}}\right]
$$

Hint: this is similar to the projectile-launcher relation derived in class.

</Statement>

<Think>

The driver fan is made of $u - c$ waves running left into still gas 4. The $u + c$ characteristics cross the fan from left to right, and **all of them start in region 4**, where u = 0 and c = c₄. So $C^+$ has the same value everywhere in the fan and in region 3. Combine that with the isentropic p–c relation.

</Think>

<Plan>

$C^+ = 2c_4/(\gamma_4-1)$ → $c_3/c_4$ in terms of $u_3$ → isentropic $p_3/p_4 = (c_3/c_4)^{2\gamma_4/(\gamma_4-1)}$ → invert.

<ShockTubeXTFigure p41={10} />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. The invariant from region 4">

$u_3 + \dfrac{2c_3}{\gamma_4-1} = 0 + \dfrac{2c_4}{\gamma_4-1}$, so $\dfrac{c_3}{c_4} = 1 - \dfrac{\gamma_4-1}{2}\dfrac{u_3}{c_4}$.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Isentropic expansion">

$\dfrac{p_3}{p_4} = \left(\dfrac{c_3}{c_4}\right)^{\frac{2\gamma_4}{\gamma_4-1}} = \left[1 - \dfrac{\gamma_4-1}{2}\dfrac{u_3}{c_4}\right]^{\frac{2\gamma_4}{\gamma_4-1}}$

</Step>
<Step title="3. Invert">

Raise both sides to $\frac{\gamma_4-1}{2\gamma_4}$ and solve for $u_3$, which gives the result to be shown.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

$u_3 = \dfrac{2c_4}{\gamma_4-1}\left[1 - (p_3/p_4)^{(\gamma_4-1)/(2\gamma_4)}\right]$. Its maximum, for expansion to $p_3 \to 0$, is the escape velocity $2c_4/(\gamma_4-1)$: the same $u_\text{max}$ as the gas gun, with the contact surface playing the role of the projectile.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- Use the invariant of the family that **crosses** the fan ($C^+$), not the family the fan is made of.
- Only driver properties (γ₄, c₄) appear. The driven gas does not enter.

</Traps>

<Source>official 2025 solution p. 4 · Lecture 23 gas-gun derivation</Source>

<Problem id="PS11 P5" title="Derive the shock-tube equation" tags="contact surface, moving shock, algebra">

<Statement>

Using the result of Problem 4 and the fact that pressure and velocity must match at the contact interface ($p_3 = p_2$, $u_3 = u_2$), derive the shock-tube equation

$$
\frac{p_4}{p_1} = \frac{p_2}{p_1}\left[1 - \frac{(\gamma_4-1)(c_1/c_4)(p_2/p_1-1)}{\sqrt{2\gamma_1\left(2\gamma_1+(\gamma_1+1)(p_2/p_1-1)\right)}}\right]^{-\frac{2\gamma_4}{\gamma_4-1}}
$$

</Statement>

<Think>

Four pieces (lecture's list): (1) the expansion relation of P4; (2) the moving-shock relation for the driven gas, **with the contact surface as the piston**; (3) $u_3 = u_2$; (4) $p_3 = p_2$. The rest is eliminating $M_s$.

</Think>

<Plan>

$\dfrac{p_4}{p_1} = \dfrac{p_4}{p_3}\dfrac{p_2}{p_1}$ → P4 with $u_3 = u_2$ → $u_2/c_1$ from the unsteady shock formula → $M_s^2$ from $p_2/p_1$ → simplify.

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Split the ratio">

$\dfrac{p_4}{p_1} = \dfrac{p_2}{p_1}\left[1 - \dfrac{\gamma_4-1}{2}\dfrac{u_2}{c_4}\right]^{-\frac{2\gamma_4}{\gamma_4-1}}$ (P4 inverted, with $p_3 = p_2$ and $u_3 = u_2$).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Shock side">

$\dfrac{u_2}{c_1} = \dfrac{2}{\gamma_1+1}\dfrac{M_s^2-1}{M_s}$ and $M_s^2 = \dfrac{(\gamma_1+1)(p_2/p_1)+(\gamma_1-1)}{2\gamma_1} \equiv \dfrac{X}{2\gamma_1}$.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Eliminate M_s">

$\dfrac{M_s^2-1}{M_s} = \dfrac{X-2\gamma_1}{\sqrt{2\gamma_1}\sqrt X}$, where $X - 2\gamma_1 = (\gamma_1+1)(p_2/p_1-1)$ and $X = 2\gamma_1+(\gamma_1+1)(p_2/p_1-1)$.

So $\dfrac{\gamma_4-1}{2}\dfrac{u_2}{c_4} = \dfrac{\gamma_4-1}{2}\dfrac{c_1}{c_4}\dfrac{2}{\gamma_1+1}\dfrac{(\gamma_1+1)(p_2/p_1-1)}{\sqrt{2\gamma_1 X}}$. The $(\gamma_1+1)$ cancels, which leaves the bracket of the shock-tube equation.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

The shock-tube equation as stated. It links the diaphragm ratio $p_4/p_1$ to the shock strength $p_2/p_1$ (or $M_s$) through $c_1/c_4$, γ₁ and γ₄. A larger $c_4/c_1$ (a light or hot driver) gives a stronger shock for the same $p_4/p_1$.

</Answer>

<ShockTubeSim p41={10} />

<Traps>

- Across the contact only p and u match. T and ρ jump.
- The bracket must stay positive. As $p_4/p_1 \to \infty$ the shock strength saturates: there is a finite maximum $M_s$.
- `shockTube()` in the site's lib solves this equation for $p_2/p_1$ by bisection. Every readout in the sim comes from it.

</Traps>

<Source>official 2025 solution pp. 4–6 · lecture "(1)+(2)+(3)+(4) ⇒ shock tube equation"</Source>

</Problem>

<Problem id="PS11 P6" title="Shock-tube design for T₅ = 1200 K, p₅ = 5 atm; nitrogen vs helium driver" tags="reflected shock, iteration, shock-tube equation, official errors corrected">

<Statement>

Shock tubes are used to bring a test gas almost instantaneously to a chosen state. The state of interest is state 5, behind the shock reflected from the end wall. We want to study methyl-butanoate combustion in air at **5 atm and 1200 K** (state 5). Find the required initial conditions of state 1 (T₁, p₁; the handout says "T₄, p₁", a typo) and the driver pressure p₄, with T₄ = 300 K. Treat the test gas as air. Do this for (a) a nitrogen driver (MW 28, γ = 1.4) and (b) a helium driver (MW 4, γ = 1.666). Which needs the greater p₄?

</Statement>

<Think>

Two moving shocks in series. Seen from gas 2, the **end wall is a piston** moving at $u_2$, so the reflected shock obeys the same formula with $c_x = c_2$. T₅/T₁ depends only on $M_s$, so iterate on $M_s$ until T₅ = 1200 K (with T₁ = 300 K). Then p₁ follows from the pressure ratios, and p₄ from the shock-tube equation (P5).

</Think>

<Plan>

Guess $M_s$ → (A3) $T_2/T_1$, $p_2/p_1$, $c_2 = c_1\sqrt{T_2/T_1}$ → $u_2$ → $M_R$ from $u_2/c_2$ → (A3) $T_5/T_2$, $p_5/p_2$ → $T_5$. Iterate. Then $p_1 = 5\ \text{atm}/[(p_5/p_2)(p_2/p_1)]$ → P5 for $p_4/p_1$.

<ReflectedShockFigure Ms={2.705} MR={2.019} />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Iterate on M_s (c₁ = 347.6 m/s)">

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1.6875 | 4.5 | 451.5 | 434.5 | 1.732 | 1.4815 | 3.33 | 750 |

</Step>

p_1 = \frac{p_5}{(p_5/p_2)(p_2/p_1)} = \frac{5}{4.590 \times 8.370} = 0.130\ \text{atm} \;(13.2\ \text{kPa})

</Step>

$c_4(\text{N}_2) = \sqrt{1.4 \times 296.9 \times 300} = 353.1$ m/s and $c_4(\text{He}) = \sqrt{1.666 \times 2078.5 \times 300} = 1019$ m/s.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- Incident shock $M_s = 2.705$ ($p_2/p_1 = 8.37$, $u_2 = 676.5$ m/s), reflected $M_R = 2.019$.
- T₁ = 300 K, **p₁ = 0.130 atm** (official 0.351 atm, corrected).
- **p₄(N₂) ≈ 32 atm** ($p_4/p_1 \approx 246$) and **p₄(He) ≈ 3.8 atm** ($p_4/p_1 \approx 29.2$). The official values (2.16 and 1.21 atm) are corrected here.
- **Nitrogen needs the greater driver pressure**, about 8.4 times more. The conclusion of the official solution stands, and is even stronger. A heavy driver spends its internal energy accelerating itself; light helium ($c_4/c_1 \approx 2.9$) spends it on the test gas.

</Answer>
