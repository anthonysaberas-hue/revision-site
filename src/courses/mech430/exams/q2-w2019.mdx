<Problem id="W2019 P1" title="Supersonic diffuser: swallowing the shock (25 pts)" tags="inlet starting, normal shock, A2, A3">

<Statement>

A converging-diverging diffuser is designed to decelerate air isentropically from a Mach number of 1.5 to a subsonic state after the throat.

(a) If the throat area is fixed, to what Mach number must the flow be accelerated in order to swallow the shock?

(b) If the throat area can be increased, what percent increase in throat area is required for the shock to be swallowed with Mach 1.5 flow entering the diffuser?

</Statement>

<Think>

The words "diffuser", "designed for M = 1.5" and "swallow the shock" mean this is an **inlet-starting** problem ([Ch. 9](#/mech430/ch/inlets), recipe §1.6). There is one picture to keep in mind. On the edge of starting, the normal shock sits **exactly on the inlet lip**, and the subsonic flow behind it **just chokes at the throat**: $A^*_y = A_t$. Everything follows from that one identity.

- (a) The geometry is fixed, so the area ratio is fixed. Find the free-stream Mach number that makes the post-shock flow fit through the throat.
- (b) The Mach number is fixed. The stagnation-pressure loss across the shock grows $A^*$, so the throat must grow by $A^*_y/A^*_x = p_{0x}/p_{0y}$.

</Think>

<Plan>

Design: $M = 1.5 \to A_{in}/A_t$ (A2). Then:
- (a) $A_{in}/A^*_y = A_{in}/A_t$ → A2 **subsonic** → $M_y$ → A3 inverse → $M_x$.
- (b) $M_x = 1.5$ → A3 → $p_{0y}/p_{0x}$ → $A_{t,new}/A_t = p_{0x}/p_{0y}$.

Click through the four states of the diffuser below: design, overspeed, opened throat, and unstarted.

<InletSection3D M={1.5} />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. The design area ratio">

Isentropic and shock-free, sonic at the throat, so the throat **is** $A^*$:

$$
\frac{A_{in}}{A_t} = \frac{A}{A^*}\bigg|_{M=1.5}
$$

<T>A2 @ M = 1.50: A/A* = 1.1762</T> (exact 1.17617) — <CalcLink calc="isentropic" q="in=M&v=1.5">check</CalcLink>

As a fraction of the inlet, the throat is $1/1.1762 = 0.850\,A_{in}$ (Anthony's framing).

</Step>
<Step title="2. (a) Shock on the lip: the post-shock flow must just fit the throat">

With the shock at the inlet, station $y$ is just behind it, and the subsonic flow chokes at the fixed throat, so $A^*_y = A_t$:

$$
\frac{A_{in}}{A^*_y} = \frac{A_{in}}{A_t} = 1.1762
$$

The flow behind a normal shock is subsonic, so read the **subsonic** branch:

<T>A2 @ A/A* = 1.1766 (sub): M_y = 0.61</T> (exact 0.6104) — <CalcLink calc="isentropic" q="in=A&v=1.1762&b=sub">check</CalcLink>

</Step>
<Step title="3. (a) Which upstream Mach number gives that M_y?">

Inverse lookup in the normal-shock table:

<T>A3 @ M_y = 0.6099: Mₓ = 1.83</T> (exact 1.828) — <CalcLink calc="normal" q="in=My&v=0.61">check</CalcLink>

So the flow must be **overspeeded to M = 1.83**. Once the shock has been swallowed, you can slow back down to M = 1.5 with the shock sitting at the throat, where it is weakest.

</Step>
<Step title="4. (b) Keep M = 1.5 and open the throat">

The shock is still on the lip at $M_x = 1.5$. The new throat must pass the post-shock flow: $A_{t2} = A^*_y$. Because $A^*$ is inversely proportional to $p_0$ at fixed ṁ and $T_0$:

$$
\frac{A^*_x}{A^*_y} = \frac{p_{0y}}{p_{0x}}
$$

<T>A3 @ Mₓ = 1.50: p₀ᵧ/p₀ₓ = 0.9298, M_y = 0.7011</T> — <CalcLink calc="normal" q="in=Mx&v=1.5">check</CalcLink>

</Step>
<Step title="5. (b) The throat increase">

Official route: $A_{in}/A_{t2} = (A_{in}/A^*_x)(A^*_x/A^*_y) = 1.1762 \times 0.9298 = 1.0936$, so

$$
\frac{A_{t2}}{A_t} = \frac{1.1762}{1.0936} = 1.0755
$$

Shortcut, with one lookup: $A_{t2}/A_t = A^*_y/A^*_x = p_{0x}/p_{0y} = 1/0.9298 = 1.0755$. The throat grows from 85.0 % to 91.4 % of the inlet area.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- (a) Overspeed to $M_x = 1.83$.
- (b) Increase the throat area by **7.55 %** ($A_{t2}/A_t = 1.0755$).

</Answer>

<InletStartMap M={1.5} />

<Traps>

- In (a) the identity is $A^*_y = A_t$, **not** $A^*_x$. The flow behind the shock is subsonic, so read A2 on the **subsonic** branch.
- In (b) the free stream stays at 1.5. Only the loss of $p_0$ across the shock changes $A^*$.
- Nearest table values are fine. The quiz says so.
- **Exam-speed version (Anthony's minimum writing):** "(a) fixed area: M = 1.5 → A/A* = 1.1762 → $M_y$ = 0.61 → $M_x$ = 1.83. (b) M = 1.5 shock → $p_{0y}/p_{0x}$ = 0.9298 → $p_{0x}/p_{0y}$ = 1.0755, so the throat must increase by 7.55 %."

</Traps>

<Problem id="W2019 P2" title="Rocket thrust: sea level vs space (25 pts)" tags="rocket, thrust, isentropic">

<Statement>

The supersonic nozzle of a rocket motor is designed to operate at optimal conditions at sea level ($p_{amb}$ = 101.3 kPa). The chamber pressure and temperature are 1000 kPa and 2000 K.

(a) Determine the **ratio of thrust at sea level to the thrust in outer space** ($p_{amb} = 0$) if the area ratio of the nozzle is **fixed**. Assume the rocket exhaust gases behave as a perfect gas with γ = 1.4 and R = 300 J/kg·K.

(b) Determine the ratio of **thrust at sea level** (with the same nozzle as part (a)) to **the thrust in outer space** ($p_{amb}$ = 0) if the **area ratio of the nozzle in space is infinite**.

You may use the "Thrust from Rocket Nozzle" formula on the equation sheet.

</Statement>

<Think>

A thrust **ratio** with no area given means: work **per unit mass flow** and let ṁ cancel (recipe R1). "Optimal at sea level" means **matched expansion**, $p_e = p_{amb}$, so the pressure term is zero at sea level.

- With the same nozzle in space, the supersonic nozzle flow does not know about the outside, so $M_e$, $V_e$ and $p_e$ are unchanged. Only the pressure term $p_e A_e$ appears.
- With an infinite area ratio, $p_e \to 0$, and $V_e \to V_{max} = \sqrt{2\gamma R T_0/(\gamma-1)}$. ṁ is unchanged: same chamber, same throat.

</Think>

<Plan>

$$
F = \dot m V_e + (p_e - p_{amb})A_e
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\frac{F}{\dot m} = V_e + \frac{p_e - p_{amb}}{\rho_e V_e}
$$

$p_e = p_{amb}$ → $M_e$ → $T_e$ → $V_e$ → $p_e/(\rho_e V_e) = RT_e/V_e$ → two ratios.

<RocketSection3D />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Exit Mach number for matched expansion">

$p_0/p_e = 1000/101.3 = 9.872$:

$$
M_e = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma-1}\left[\left(\frac{p_0}{p_e}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - 1\right]} = 2.149
$$

<T>A2 @ p/p₀ = 0.1013: M ≈ 2.15, A/A* ≈ 1.92</T> — <CalcLink calc="isentropic" q="in=p&v=0.1013">check</CalcLink>

</Step>
<Step title="2. Exit temperature and velocity">

$$
T_e = \frac{T_0}{1 + 0.2 M_e^2} = \frac{2000}{1.9236} = 1039.7\ \text{K}
$$

$$
V_e = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1} R T_0 \left[1 - \left(\frac{p_e}{p_0}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\right]} = M_e\sqrt{\gamma R T_e} = 1420.1\ \text{m/s}
$$

</Step>
<Step title="3. (a) Thrust per unit mass flow, at sea level and in space">

At sea level the pressure term is zero: $F_{sea}/\dot m = V_e = 1420.1$ m/s.

In space, with the same nozzle, the pressure term is added. Anthony's trick avoids ever needing $A_e$:

$$
\frac{p_e}{\rho_e V_e} = \frac{R T_e}{V_e} = \frac{300 \times 1039.7}{1420.1} = 219.65\ \text{m/s}
$$

$$
\frac{F_{sea}}{F_{space}} = \frac{1420.06}{1420.06 + 219.65} = 0.866
$$

The official solution works per unit $A_e$ instead: $\rho_e = 0.3248$ kg/m³, $\dot m/A_e = 461.2$ kg/(m²·s), $F_{sea}/A_e = 6.549\times10^5$ Pa, and $F_{space}/A_e = 6.549\times10^5 + 1.013\times10^5 = 7.562\times10^5$ Pa. The ratio is the same.

</Step>
<Step title="4. (b) Infinite area ratio">

$p_e \to 0$, so the bracket in $V_e$ becomes 1:

$$
V_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}RT_0} = \sqrt{7 \times 300 \times 2000} = 2049.4\ \text{m/s}
$$

$$
\frac{F_{sea}}{F_{space,\infty}} = \frac{1420.06}{2049.39} = 0.693
$$

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- (a) $F_{sea}/F_{space} = 0.866$ (86.6 %).
- (b) $F_{sea}/F_{space,\infty} = 0.693$ (69.3 %).

</Answer>

<RocketThrustSim />

<Traps>

- For the fixed nozzle, $p_e$ does **not** change in space. Only the $(p_e - p_{amb})A_e$ term changes.
- For the infinite area ratio, ṁ is unchanged. Only $V_e \to V_{max}$.
- $A_e$ is never given. Divide it out, or work per unit ṁ.
- In the sim above, set $A_e/A_t$ to about 1.92 and read the "sea-level / vacuum thrust" line: 0.866.
- **Exam-speed version:** "$V_e$ = 1420.06 → M = 2.15 → $T_e$ = 1039.72 → $RT/V$ = 219.65 → (a) 1420.06/(1420.06 + 219.65) = 0.866. (b) 1420.06/2049.39 = 0.693."

</Traps>

<Problem id="W2019 P3" title="Nozzle + Fanno pipe: exit state and shock location (50 pts)" tags="Fanno, normal shock, choked exit, iteration">

<Statement>

A reservoir at 10 bar feeds air into a converging-diverging nozzle connected to a pipe 1.5 m long and 0.1 m in diameter. The pipe cross-sectional area is 5 times the nozzle throat area. The pipe exhausts into a tank maintained at 1 bar. The pipe has a friction coefficient f = 0.02. Neglect friction in the nozzle.

(a) Find the Mach number and pressure at the exit of the pipe.

(b) If a shock wave is present, determine its location.

</Statement>

<Think>

A reservoir, a C-D nozzle, a pipe with friction and a back pressure: this is **recipe R2**, the most-examined problem in the course. Recognise it in 30 seconds and set up the hypothesis ladder:

1. Subsonic after the throat? (It justifies choking.)
2. Supersonic all the way? Compare $4fL/D$ with $4fL^*/D$.
3. Shock at the pipe entrance? Compare $p_{exit}$ with $p_b$.
4. Shock inside the pipe: iterate.

The twist in this problem is at step 4. The back pressure (1 bar) is **below** the $p^*$ of the Fanno line (about 1.02 bar), so the exit **must be choked** and $p_{exit} \neq p_b$. You stop matching $p_b$ and instead look for the shock position where the pipe behind the shock is exactly $L^*$ long.

</Think>

<Plan>

$4fL/D = 4(0.02)(1.5)/0.1 = 1.2$. Every guess is checked against this number.

0 reservoir → t (M = 1) → ① pipe inlet ($A/A^* = 5$) → x | y (shock) → ② pipe exit.

<NozzlePipeSim AR={5} fL={1.2} pb={0.1} p0={10} />

</Plan>

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Friction length and the subsonic branch (does it choke?)">

$4fL/D = 1.2$. Try subsonic after the throat:

<T>A2 @ A/A* = 5 (sub): M = 0.12</T> (exact 0.117) and <T>A4 @ M = 0.12: 4fL*/D = 45.408</T> — <CalcLink calc="isentropic" q="in=A&v=5&b=sub">check</CalcLink>

$45.408 - 1.2 = 44.2$, which is almost the same, so the Mach number barely changes along the pipe and $p_{exit} \approx p_{in} \approx 9.9$ bar ($p/p_0 = 0.99$). That is far above 1 bar. "So the flow must have shocked and gone supersonic." The throat is choked.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Supersonic all the way?">

<T>A2 @ A/A* = 5 (sup): M = 3.2, p/p₀ = 0.02023 → p = 0.2023 bar</T> (exact M = 3.175) — <CalcLink calc="isentropic" q="in=A&v=5&b=sup">check</CalcLink>

<T>A4 @ M = 3.0 (row the official used): 4fL*/D = 0.522</T> (exact at 3.175: 0.547) — <CalcLink calc="fanno" q="in=M&v=3.2">check</CalcLink>

$0.522 < 1.2$: the pipe is longer than $L^*$, so supersonic flow cannot reach the exit. There must be a shock.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Try a shock at the pipe entrance">

The nozzle-exit state is the $x$ state: $M_x = 3.2$, $p_x = 0.2023$ bar.

<T>A3 @ Mₓ = 3.2: M_y = 0.4643, p_y/pₓ = 11.78</T> → $p_y = 2.383$ bar — <CalcLink calc="normal" q="in=Mx&v=3.2">check</CalcLink>

<T>A4 @ M = 0.46: 4fL*/D = 1.4509, p/p* = 2.3326</T> → $p^* = 2.383/2.3326 = 1.02$ bar — <CalcLink calc="fanno" q="in=M&v=0.46">check</CalcLink>

$1.4509 > 1.2$, so the flow fits. At the exit, $4fL^*/D = 1.4509 - 1.2 = 0.2509$:

<T>A4 @ 4fL*/D = 0.2509 (sub): M = 0.68, p/p* = 1.5413</T> → $p_{exit} = 1.02 \times 1.5413 = 1.57$ bar — <CalcLink calc="fanno" q="in=fL&v=0.2509&b=sub">check</CalcLink>

The exit is subsonic, so it would have to equal $p_b = 1$ bar. It doesn't. 1.57 bar is too high, so we are "sucking harder" and the shock moves downstream into the pipe.

</Step>
<Step title="4. The key observation: p_b is below p*">

$p_b = 1$ bar is less than $p^* = 1.02$ bar. Any subsonic Fanno exit has $p \geq p^*$, so it can never equal 1 bar. **The exit must be choked**: $M_{exit} = 1$ and $p_{exit} = p^*$.

The target is no longer $p_{exit} = p_b$. It is the shock position where **the pipe behind the shock is exactly $L^*$ long**:

$$
\frac{4fL^*}{D}\bigg|_{M_y} = 1.2 - \frac{4fx_s}{D}
$$

If the $L^*$ behind the shock is shorter than the pipe that remains, the shock is too far downstream. If it is longer, the shock is too far upstream.

</Step>
<Step title="5. Iterate the shock position (the official table)">

At each guess, $4fL^*_x/D = 0.522 - \text{guess}$ → A4 supersonic → $M_x$ → A3 → $M_y$ → A4 → compare with the remaining $1.2 - \text{guess}$.

| Guess $4fx_s/D$ | $0.522 -$ guess | $M_x$ (A4) | $M_y$ (A3) | $4fL^*/D$ at $M_y$ | Remaining | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 0.322 | 2.06 | 0.5675 | 0.635 | 1.0 | $L^*$ too short: move upstream |
| 0.1 | 0.422 | 2.45 | 0.5179 | 0.917 | 1.1 | still too short |
| 0.05 | 0.472 | 2.70 | 0.4956 | 1.06 | 1.15 | still too short |
| 0.02 | 0.502 | 2.87 | 0.4833 | 1.245 (exact 1.214) | 1.18 | longer: the shock is upstream of this |

So $0.02 < 4fx_s/D < 0.05$, which is $0.025\text{ m} < x_s < 0.063$ m. Higgins's red note: "we are finding $p^*$ doesn't change! AND it is very close to $p_{back}$."

<ShockIterationPlot AR={5} fL={1.2} mode="fit" tableRef={0.522} xMax={0.25} guesses={[{ x: 0.2, label: '0.2' }, { x: 0.1, label: '0.1' }, { x: 0.05, label: '0.05' }, { x: 0.02, label: '0.02' }]} />

</Step>
<Step title="6. Why p* never changes: the exact answer is 1.057 bar">

A normal shock in a Fanno duct joins two points on the **same Fanno line**: the same $\dot m/A$ and the same $T_0$. And $p^*$ depends only on those two:

$$
p^* = \rho^* V^* \frac{R T^*}{V^*} = \frac{\dot m}{A}\sqrt{\frac{R T^*}{\gamma}}, \qquad T^* = \frac{2T_0}{\gamma+1}
$$

So $p^*$ is the **same on both sides of the shock, wherever the shock sits**. With the choked throat, $\dot m/A_{pipe} = (\dot m/A_t)(A_t/A_{pipe})$, which gives a one-line result:

$$
p^* = 0.5283\,p_0\,\frac{A_t}{A_{pipe}} = 0.5283 \times 10 \times \frac{1}{5} = 1.057\ \text{bar}
$$

A choked exit therefore has $p_e = p^*_{sup} = 1.057$ bar regardless of where the shock is. The official 1.02 bar comes from table rounding: the M = 3.2 row is used for A/A* = 5 (exact 3.175), then the M = 0.46 row for $M_y$ = 0.4643.

With exact values, the shock sits at $4fx_s/D = 0.068$, or $x_s = 0.085$ m, slightly downstream of the official bracket, because the official working used the M = 3.0 Fanno row (0.522) instead of 0.547. The plot below shows $p_{exit}$ falling from 1.554 bar (shock at the entrance) to $p^*$, where the exit chokes. The 1-bar target is never reached.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Answer>

- (a) **$M_{exit} = 1$ (choked)** and **$p_{exit} \approx 1.02$ bar** (official). The exact value is $p^* = 1.057$ bar. The exit does **not** match $p_b = 1$ bar, and it doesn't have to.
- (b) A normal shock just inside the pipe: $0.02 < 4fx_s/D < 0.05$, so **0.025 m < $x_s$ < 0.063 m** from the pipe entrance (official, table rows). Exact: $x_s \approx 0.085$ m with $M_x \approx 2.74$.

</Answer>

<Traps>

- **Higgins's "WRONG" cartoon:** once you find $L^* < L$ on the supersonic branch, "there is nothing meaningful you can do with $L_{1-2} - L^*$". Computing $1.2 - 0.522 = 0.678$ and reading A4 is wrong. **Only** when the channel is shorter than $L^*$ can you use $L_2^* = L_1^* - L_{1-2}$.
- Full marks required **both** $M_{exit} = 1$ and $p_{exit} = 1.02$ bar. The marking note: since $p_{back} = 1$ bar is less than $p^*$, the flow must be choked at the exit and does not match the back pressure.
- Marking scheme: supersonic nozzle exit +10, $L^*$ and $p^*$ +10, "L > L*, so a shock" +10, shock at the entrance +10, one more trial (or the pure subsonic case) +5, conclusion +5.
- **Official slips, corrected:**
  - Step 3 writes "$M_{exit} = 0.2497$". That is the friction value, not the Mach number: $4fL^*/D = 0.2509$ is **M = 0.68**, and the 1.5413 they used is the M = 0.68 value of $p/p^*$. The 1.57 bar is right.
  - Guess 0.2: the "$4fL^*/D = 0.32253$" written after $M_y = 0.5675$ is the **x-side** value (A4 at $M$ = 2.06). The correct value at $M_y = 0.5675$ is about **0.635**. The verdict is the same.
  - Guess 0.02: the remaining pipe is $1.2 - 0.02 = 1.18$, not "1.198".
- Never mix rows: one Mach number per state (Anthony's first attempt took $p/p^*$ from M = 1.84 and $p_y/p_x$ from M = 1.86).
