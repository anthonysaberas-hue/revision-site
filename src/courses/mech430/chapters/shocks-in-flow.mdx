# Flow with shocks

<p className="lede">A normal shock is a patch between two isentropic flows. Solve isentropically up to it, jump across with Table A3, then solve isentropically again with the new reference states. The same idea, after a change of frame, handles shocks that move.</p>

## The method

<Idea title="Three reference states, one rule each">

- $T_0$: **constant everywhere** (adiabatic, including across the shock).
- $p_0$: constant **until the shock**, then $p_{0y} = (p_{0y}/p_{0x})\,p_{0x}$.
- $A^*$: constant until the shock, then $A^*_y = A^*_x/(p_{0y}/p_{0x})$ (it **grows**).

Downstream of the shock the flow is subsonic, and in a diverging duct a subsonic flow can only decelerate: take the **subsonic** branch of A2 with $A/A^*_y$.

</Idea>

Name the stations before touching a table: reservoir → throat → x (just before) | y (just after) → exit.

## Worked example: shock at a known position (notes §8.1.1)

A C-D nozzle is fed by a reservoir at $p_0 = 1$ atm, $T_0 = 300$ K. The throat A is 1 m², the shock stands at B where the area is 2 m², and the exit C is 3 m². Find M, p and T at A, B and C.

<ThreeAreaNozzleFigure />

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Reference states">

A shock exists in the diverging part, so the flow there is supersonic, so it went sonic at the throat: $A^* = A_A = 1$ m². At A: $p_A = 0.5283$ atm, $T_A = 250$ K.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Isentropic up to the shock">

$A_{B}/A^* = 2$, supersonic branch: <T>A2 @ A/A\* = 2.0 (sup): M = 2.20, p/p₀ = 0.0935, T/T₀ = 0.5081</T>. So $p_{Bx} = 0.0935$ atm, $T_{Bx} = 152$ K. <CalcLink calc="isentropic" q="in=A&v=2&b=sup">check</CalcLink>

</Step>
<Step title="3. Jump across the shock">

<T>A3 @ Mₓ = 2.20: M_y = 0.5471, p_y/pₓ = 5.480, T_y/Tₓ = 1.857, p₀ᵧ/p₀ₓ = 0.6281</T>

$p_y = 5.48 \times 0.0935 = 0.512$ atm, $T_y = 1.857 \times 152 = 282$ K, $p_{0y} = 0.628$ atm, and

$$
A^*_y = \frac{A^*_x}{p_{0y}/p_{0x}} = \frac{1}{0.628} = 1.59\ \text{m}^2
$$

Check: $T_{0y} = 282(1 + 0.2 \times 0.547^2) = 299$ K ≈ 300 K. ✓ <CalcLink calc="normal" q="in=Mx&v=2.2">check</CalcLink>

</Step>
<Step title="4. Isentropic again, with the NEW references">

$A_C/A^*_y = 3/1.59 = 1.88$, subsonic: <T>A2 @ A/A\* = 1.88 (sub): M = 0.33, p/p₀ = 0.927, T/T₀ = 0.979</T>

$p_C = 0.927 \times 0.628 = 0.58$ atm, $T_C = 0.979 \times 300 = 294$ K.

</Step>
</Steps>

| | reservoir | A | Bx | By | C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| M | 0 | 1 | 2.2 | 0.547 | 0.33 |
| p (atm) | 1 | 0.53 | 0.094 | 0.51 | 0.58 |
| T (K) | 300 | 250 | 152 | 282 | 294 |
| p₀ (atm) | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0.63 | 0.63 |
| A\* (m²) | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1.59 | 1.59 |

The exit is subsonic, so the chamber must provide exactly $p_b = p_C = 0.58$ atm. Pressure **keeps rising after the shock** (0.51 → 0.58 atm): the subsonic flow is still diffusing.

## Back-pressure regimes

Fix the reservoir and the nozzle, then lower $p_b$. Three critical back pressures, all from A2 and A3 at the exit area ratio:

| case | how to compute it | AR = 3 |
|---|---|---|
| $p_3$: choked, subsonic exit | A2 subsonic at $A_e/A_t$ → $p/p_0$ | $M_e = 0.20$, $p_3 = 0.97\,p_0$ |
| $p_d$: design, supersonic exit | A2 supersonic at $A_e/A_t$ → $p/p_0$ | $M_e = 2.64$, $p_d = 0.047\,p_0$ |
| $p_4$: normal shock at the exit plane | $p_d \times (p_y/p_x)$ at $M_x = M_e$ | $7.97 \times 0.047 = 0.374\,p_0$ |

- $p_b \ge p_3$: subsonic throughout (choked only at $p_3$).
- $p_3 \gt p_b \gt p_4$: **normal shock inside** the diverging section. Lower $p_b$ → the shock moves downstream and gets stronger.
- $p_4 \gt p_b \gt p_d$: over-expanded, oblique shocks outside (Chapter 12).
- $p_b = p_d$: design. $p_b \lt p_d$: under-expanded, expansion fans outside.

Use the buttons to jump between regimes. A shock at the throat would just be a sound wave ($M = 1$): that limit is the $p_3$ case.

## Worked example: find the shock from the back pressure (notes §8.2.1)

Same nozzle (1 m² throat, 3 m² exit, $p_0 = 1$ atm), but now $p_b = 0.7$ atm is given. Is there a shock, and where?

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Easy case: sonic throat, subsonic after">

<T>A2 @ A/A\* = 3.00 (sub): M = 0.20, p/p₀ = 0.97</T> → $p_3 = 0.97$ atm. $p_b = 0.7 \lt p_3$: the flow must go supersonic somewhere.

</Step>
<Step title="2. Design case: supersonic all the way">

<T>A2 @ A/A\* = 3.00 (sup): M = 2.64, p/p₀ = 0.047</T> → $p_d = 0.047$ atm, far below 0.7. Not design.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Shock at the exit plane">

<T>A3 @ Mₓ = 2.64: M_y = 0.501, p_y/pₓ = 7.97</T> → $p_4 = 7.97 \times 0.047 = 0.374$ atm. Since $p_3 = 0.97 \gt 0.7 \gt p_4 = 0.374$, **a normal shock is definitely in the diverging section.**

</Step>
<Step title="4. First guess: shock at A = 2 m²">

That is the §8.1.1 calculation: $p_e = 0.58$ atm. Too **low**, so the shock is too strong: move it **upstream** (weaker shock, less $p_0$ loss, higher exit pressure).

</Step>
<Step title="5. Second guess: shock at A = 1.5 m²">

<T>A2 @ A/A\* = 1.5 (sup): M = 1.85</T> → <T>A3 @ Mₓ = 1.85: p₀ᵧ/p₀ₓ = 0.790</T>

$A_e/A^*_y = 3 \times 0.790 = 2.37$ → <T>A2 @ A/A\* = 2.37 (sub): M = 0.25, p/p₀ = 0.957</T> → $p_e = 0.957 \times 0.790 = 0.76$ atm. Slightly too **high**.

</Step>
<Step title="6. Interpolate">

| $A_{shock}$ | $p_e$ |
|---|---|
| 2.0 m² | 0.58 atm |
| 1.5 m² | 0.76 atm |

$$
A_{shock} \approx 1.5 + \frac{0.7 - 0.76}{0.58 - 0.76}(2.0 - 1.5) \approx 1.67\ \text{m}^2
$$

Good enough for engineering. Iterating to convergence (the lib solver) gives $A_s = 1.64$ m², $M_x = 1.96$, $M_e = 0.27$: the linear interpolation is within 2 %.

</Step>
</Steps>

<Trap title="The exit pressure is not the pressure behind the shock">

The condition is $p_e = p_b$ (subsonic exit), **not** $p_y = p_b$. After the shock the subsonic flow keeps diffusing and the pressure keeps rising. Anthony made exactly this mistake in [PS5 P3(c)](#/mech430/ps/ps05). Only when the shock sits **at** the exit is $p_y = p_e$ (the $p_4$ case).

</Trap>

<Remember title="Which way to move the shock">

$p_e$ too low → shock too strong → move it **upstream** (smaller A). $p_e$ too high → move it **downstream**. Two guesses that bracket $p_b$, then interpolate linearly.

</Remember>

## Moving shocks

The normal-shock relations hold **only in the frame where the shock is at rest**. A shock moving into still gas is made steady by adding $-V_s$ to every velocity. Static properties ($p, T, \rho$) don't care about the frame; velocities and Mach numbers do.

Prime = lab frame (notes): $V_s'$ is the shock speed and $V_p'$ the gas (= piston) speed behind it.

| | lab frame | shock frame |
|---|---|---|
| gas ahead | at rest | $V_x = V_s'$ toward the shock, $M_x = M_s$ |
| shock | $V_s'$ | 0 |
| gas behind | $V_p'$ | $V_y = V_s' - V_p'$ |

### Notes §8.4.1: Mach 3 shock into still air

Air at 1 atm, 300 K, $c_x = 348$ m/s, $M_s = 3$, so $V_s' = 1044$ m/s.

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Go to the shock frame">

$V_x = 1044$ m/s, $M_x = 3$. <T>A3 @ Mₓ = 3.0: M_y = 0.4752, p_y/pₓ = 10.33, ρ_y/ρₓ = 3.857, T_y/Tₓ = 2.679</T>

So $p_y = 10.33$ atm, $T_y = 804$ K, $c_y = 348\sqrt{2.679} = 569.6$ m/s, $V_y = 0.4752 \times 569.6 = 270.7$ m/s (or $V_x/3.857$).

</Step>
<Step title="2. Back to the lab">

Add 1044 m/s: $V_p' = V_x - V_y = 1044 - 270.7 = 773.3$ m/s.

</Step>
<Step title="3. Surprise: supersonic behind a shock">

$M_y' = V_p'/c_y = 773.3/569.6 = 1.357$. Behind a **moving** shock the gas can be supersonic in the lab. "Subsonic after a shock" is a shock-frame statement.

</Step>
</Steps>

### The piston formula

Writing $V_p'/c_x = M_s - M_y\sqrt{T_y/T_x}$ and simplifying gives a closed form (into **quiescent** gas only):

$$
\frac{V_p'}{c_x} = \frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(\frac{M_s^2 - 1}{M_s}\right)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
M_s = \frac{\gamma+1}{4}\frac{V_p'}{c_x} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{4}\frac{V_p'}{c_x}\right)^2 + 1}
$$

$V_p'/c_x$ is **not** a Mach number (it uses the sound speed ahead). For a fast piston the shock runs $(\gamma+1)/2 = 1.2$ times faster than the piston. Check: $M_s = 3$ → $V_p'/c_x = (2/2.4)(8/3) = 2.222$ → $V_p' = 773.3$ m/s. ✓ <CalcLink calc="moving">moving-shock calculator</CalcLink>

<Trap title="Quiescent gas only">

The piston formula assumes the gas ahead is at rest. If it is already moving (a reflected shock, a shock running into a flow), first change frame so that the gas ahead is still. More of this in [Chapter 16](#/mech430/ch/unsteady).

</Trap>

## Check yourself

1. Across a shock in a nozzle, what happens to $A^*$? → It grows: $A^*_y = A^*_x/(p_{0y}/p_{0x})$.
2. $p_b$ is between $p_3$ and $p_4$. Where is the shock and what is $p_e$? → In the diverging section; $p_e = p_b$ (subsonic exit).
3. Your trial shock gives $p_e$ lower than $p_b$. Which way do you move it? → Upstream (weaker shock).
