# Plumes & diamond airfoils

<p className="lede">Two places where oblique shocks and Prandtl–Meyer fans do real work. A rocket nozzle that doesn't match the ambient pressure makes them at its lip, which is where shock diamonds come from. A thin supersonic wing is just a set of corners, so adding up the pressure on its faces gives its lift and drag.</p>

## Over- and under-expanded nozzle plumes

Back to the converging–diverging nozzle of Chapters 6 and 8. With the throat choked and the diverging section fully supersonic, the exit pressure is fixed at the design value $p_e$. If the back pressure $p_b$ is different, but not different enough to push a normal shock inside, the mismatch is fixed **outside**, at the lip:

| Back pressure | Name | What happens at the lip |
|---|---|---|
| $p_b > p_e$ (up to the normal-shock-at-exit value) | over-expanded | an **oblique shock** turns the jet *inward*; its strength makes $p_y = p_b$ |
| $p_b = p_e$ | perfectly expanded | no waves |
| $p_b < p_e$ | under-expanded | a **P-M fan** turns the jet *outward* until $p = p_b$ |

A sea-level-optimised rocket at altitude is under-expanded: the plume balloons outward (Saturn I photos in the notes show turning near 90°). The sideways velocity gives no thrust, which is why the ideal nozzle matches the ambient pressure (Ch. 6). A vacuum-optimised nozzle tested at sea level is over-expanded.

**Shock diamonds.** The two lip shocks meet on the axis (in an axisymmetric jet they focus into a small Mach reflection, Ch. 12). They reflect off the jet boundary, a constant-pressure surface, as **expansions**; those reflect back as compressions, which steepen into shocks, and the cell repeats for tens of diameters. Explore it in the [3-D rocket engine lab](#/mech430/lab/engine).

### Worked example: Raptor at sea level and in space (Final A P2(c), (d))

$p_0 = 30$ MPa, γ = 1.4, nozzle designed for $p_e = 70$ kPa, so $M_e = 4.822$ (from $p_0/p_e$ with A2).

**(c) Sea level, $p_b = 100$ kPa: over-expanded.**

1. The lip shock must raise the pressure from 70 to 100 kPa: $p_y/p_x = 1.4286$.
2. <T>A3 @ p_y/pₓ = 1.4286: Mₓ = 1.17, M_y = 0.862</T> (exact 1.1693). This is the **normal** component.
3. $\sin\sigma = 1.17/4.822$ → **σ = 14.04°**.
4. tan δ formula with M = 4.822, σ = 14.04° → **δ = 3.06°** inward.

**(d) Vacuum, $p_b = 0$: under-expanded.** Expanding to p = 0 means M → ∞, so the lip fan turns the flow by $\nu_{max} - \nu(4.822) = 130.45° - 75.20° =$ **55.25° outward**.

## Shock-expansion theory for a diamond airfoil

A symmetric diamond (double wedge) has chord c, half-angle ε (each face 10° to the chord in all the course problems) and sits at angle of attack α. Each face is a straight wall, so the flow over it is uniform, and each corner is a wave we already know:

- **Nose, upper face:** the face is inclined $\varepsilon - \alpha$ to the stream. If positive, an oblique shock with δ = ε − α. If negative (α > ε), it turns **away** from the flow: a P-M expansion by α − ε. If zero, no wave.
- **Nose, lower face:** inclined $\varepsilon + \alpha$, nearly always a shock.
- **Shoulders (mid-chord):** each surface turns away by the full included angle **2ε**, whatever α is: a P-M fan from the front-face state.
- **Trailing edge:** shocks or fans bring the two streams to a common pressure and direction. They do not affect the face pressures.

Then integrate. Per unit span, each face has length $\ell = (c/2)/\cos\varepsilon$ and its force $p\,\ell$ acts along the face normal. A face inclined φ to the stream contributes $p\ell\sin\varphi$ to drag (positive on a front face, negative on a rear face) and $p\ell\cos\varphi$ to lift (pushing down on top faces, up on bottom faces). With faces 1 = upper front, 2 = upper rear, 3 = lower front, 4 = lower rear:

$$
D' = \ell\left[(p_1 - p_4)\sin(\varepsilon - \alpha) + (p_3 - p_2)\sin(\varepsilon + \alpha)\right]
$$

$$
L' = \ell\left[(p_4 - p_1)\cos(\varepsilon - \alpha) + (p_3 - p_2)\cos(\varepsilon + \alpha)\right]
$$

At α = 0 this collapses to $D' = (p_{front} - p_{rear})\,c\tan\varepsilon$ and L = 0: pure **wave drag**, which exists even with no lift and no viscosity, a purely supersonic effect.

Coefficients use the compressible dynamic pressure, so you never need ρ or T:

$$
q_\infty = \tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2 = \tfrac12\gamma p_\infty M_\infty^2, \qquad C_L = \frac{L'}{q_\infty c}, \qquad C_D = \frac{D'}{q_\infty c}
$$

<Trap title="Bookkeeping traps">

- "Don't forget the cos" (Anthony's redo of PS10): the face length is $0.5c/\cos\varepsilon$, the hypotenuse, not $0.5c$.
- $p_0$ is constant through a fan but **not** across a shock: the rear-face pressure is $p_{front}\,(p/p_0)_{M_{rear}}/(p/p_0)_{M_{front}}$, using the front-face Mach number.
- Lift is very sensitive to the upper-front pressure, so read the chart carefully or iterate tan δ.
- kPa × m = kN per metre of span. A question in N/m wants 19 110, not 19.11.

</Trap>

**Face 1, upper front: shock, δ = 10° − 5° = 5°.** The chart for M = 2, δ = 5° gives **σ = 34.3°**. The student's reading of 35° is a common misread, and it costs about 13% of the lift. $M_{1N} = 2\sin 34.3° = 1.127$, <T>A3 @ 1.13: p_y/pₓ = 1.315, M_y = 0.891</T>, so $p_1 = 131.5$ kPa and $M_1 = 0.891/\sin 29.3° = 1.821$.

**Face 2, upper rear: fan of 20°.** <T>A6: ν(1.821) = 21.33°</T> → ν = 41.33° → <T>M = 2.597</T>. $p_2 = 131.5 \times 0.05035/0.1685 = 39.3$ kPa.

**Face 3, lower front: shock, δ = 15°.** σ = 45.3°, $M_N = 1.423$, <T>A3: p_y/pₓ = 2.196, M_y = 0.730</T>, so $p_3 = 219.5$ kPa and $M_3 = 1.446$.

**Face 4, lower rear: fan of 20°.** <T>A6: ν(1.446) = 10.32°</T> → 30.32° → <T>M = 2.146</T>. $p_4 = 219.5 \times 0.1018/0.2944 = 75.9$ kPa.

**Forces** with ℓ = 0.5/cos 10° = 0.5077 m:

$$
D' = 0.5077\left[(131.5 - 75.9)\sin 5° + (219.5 - 39.3)\sin 15°\right] = 26.1 \text{ kN}
$$

$$
L' = 0.5077\left[(75.9 - 131.5)\cos 5° + (219.5 - 39.3)\cos 15°\right] = 60.2 \text{ kN}
$$

With $q_\infty = \frac12(1.4)(100)(2^2) = 280$ kPa: $C_L = 0.215$, $C_D = 0.093$, L/D = 2.3. The student's hand solution (σ = 35°) gave about L = 53 kN and D = 25.5 kN.

## Linear (Ackeret) theory as a check

For thin surfaces at small angles every wave is weak, and the pressure on a surface inclined θ to the stream (positive into the flow) is

$$
C_p = \frac{p - p_\infty}{q_\infty} = \frac{2\theta}{\sqrt{M_\infty^2 - 1}}
$$

For the diamond this gives

$$
C_L = \frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{M_\infty^2-1}}, \qquad C_D = \frac{4\left(\alpha^2 + \varepsilon^2\right)}{\sqrt{M_\infty^2-1}} \quad (\text{angles in rad})
$$

For Final A P3: $C_L = 0.202$ and $C_D = 0.088$, against 0.215 and 0.093 from shock-expansion theory. That is within 6%, so it is a fast sanity check of the sign and size of your answer. The drag splits into a part due to lift (α²) and a thickness part (ε²).

## Check yourself

1. Why does a diamond at α = 0 have drag in inviscid flow? → Front faces sit at high pressure behind shocks, rear faces at low pressure after fans: wave drag.
2. At α = 12° on a 10° diamond, what is on the upper-front face? → A 2° expansion fan, not a shock.
3. Over-expanded nozzle: does the lip shock turn the jet in or out? → In. Under-expanded → fan turns it out.
4. What sets the size of each shoulder fan? → The included angle 2ε, independent of α.
5. $q_\infty$ for M = 1.8, p = 100 kPa? → ½(1.4)(100)(3.24) = 226.8 kPa.
